# PART VII — THE PLATFORM AS FIRST ENTITY

## TL;DR

- **The founder's decision to build a wholly-owned software platform as the first of five entities is defensible but inverts the very logic Part IV praised.** Atruvia served hundreds of German cooperative banks that already existed and could not fail for want of customers; this platform must manufacture demand before the sector it serves exists. A vendor-owned platform relocates no cost or capability to the cooperative network — it is a supplier relationship — and by construction the founder becomes the **external-capture** vector Part III identified. The study must, and does, say this plainly: in this architecture the founder is the outsider.
- **Because equity protection is off the table, the entire defensibility of the model rests on a contractual protection architecture** — verified source-code escrow with a full operational deposit, tenant-vested data with portability, capped exit and price terms, real service-level remedies, and above all **key-person and business-continuity protection**, since a platform owned by one individual makes every tenant's operational continuity hostage to one person's survival and solvency.
- **₦50 million cannot build a multi-tenant core banking platform *and* buy a licence.** It is, per Part VI, exactly one Super-Agent licence and nothing else. "All five entities started within three years" is not achievable on ₦50m as a self-funded, from-scratch build; it requires external capital, a white-label/open-source shortcut, or a redefinition of "started" from "viable going concern" to "incorporated."

## Key Findings

**1. The strategic inversion is the analytical spine of this Part.** Atruvia AG and its predecessors (Fiducia IT AG and GAD eG) grew inside a pre-existing, captive market of German cooperative banks. This founder proposes the opposite: build the supplier *before* the tenants exist and *before* any Nigerian cooperative is accustomed to buying core software. A platform with one anchor tenant and a hope is not a utility with captive demand — it is a bespoke build wearing the costume of a product, carrying market, execution, and financing risk simultaneously.

**2. The chosen ownership model is the global vendor norm, not an aberration — but it costs the network something real.** Fiserv, Jack Henry, Temenos, Finastra and Mambu are all investor-owned vendors to cooperative and community institutions. Option 4 (permanent founder ownership plus contractual protection) is mainstream. What it costs the cooperatives is definite: they never own their infrastructure, so Part IV's benefit — capability relocating to the network — never accrues. They get *form* (contractual rights) without *economics* (equity upside). This is a legitimate model with a quantifiable cost, not a betrayal; the honest task is to price that cost and restore what contracts can.

**3. Nigeria has real comparators and a powerful, double-edged policy tailwind.** Kenya's SACCO software market is the most developed comparable: Kwara (~230 SACCOs, $7m raised), Craft Silicon's Bankers Realm, Fintech's FinSacco, and Musoni. Nigeria already hosts cooperative-management vendors (Probitycoop, Coopco, Prolean, CoopSolve, Clé), a bank-led entrant (Wema Bank's CoopHub), and Oradian serving MFIs. In parallel, the Federal Government's **Renewed Hope Cooperative Reform and Revamp Programme (RH-CRRP) 2025–2030** targets the digitisation of more than 30,000 registered cooperatives and proposes a Cooperative Bank of Nigeria — a tailwind and a state-blessed competitive threat at once.

**4. The platform almost certainly needs no CBN licence of its own — until it touches money.** Pure operations/core-banking software is not a licensable activity under the CBN's 2020 payment categorisations. A PSSP licence (₦100m) is triggered only by payment processing; switching triggers ₦2bn; only an MMO (₦2bn) may hold customer funds. The binding constraint arrives *indirectly*: once tenants include microfinance banks, the CBN's Risk-Based Cybersecurity Framework for OFIs (2022) makes the *tenant* responsible for third-party vendor due diligence, so the platform must be audit-ready even though it is itself unlicensed.

**5. ₦50m is the binding reality and forces a fork.** Nigerian mid-level engineer total compensation runs to several million naira per year each; a credible multi-tenant core-banking build needs a small multi-year team plus infrastructure, security, legal and compliance overhead. ₦50m is roughly one year of such a team's fully-loaded cost. The sequencing is a genuine fork: platform *or* licence, not both.

## Details

### VII.1 The platform's position in the architecture

The platform is the first of the founder's five entities and the load-bearing one: the cooperative society, the remittance capability, the central/apex institution, and the credit bureau (entities two through five) all assume it exists and works. It is **core banking and operations software for cooperative societies** — member management, a savings-and-shares ledger, loan origination and servicing, accounting, statutory and AGM reporting, and an integration layer to Nigeria's payment and identity rails (NIBSS, BVN/NIN). Its customers are cooperative societies registered under the Nigerian Co-operative Societies Act, and later, potentially microfinance banks. It is **a company the founder owns outright** — not cooperatively owned by its tenants, with no buy-in mechanism and no path to tenant ownership. Contractual protections substitute for equity. This is the Fiserv/Jack Henry/Temenos model, explicitly **not** the Atruvia model.

Why first? The strategic logic is that the supplier is the reusable asset. Build the software once; sell it to many; use the recurring revenue to fund the licensed entities that follow. This is coherent capital strategy — software has near-zero marginal cost of reproduction, and a working platform is the cheapest way to touch many cooperatives at once.

But the logic contains an inversion that is the spine of this Part. **Atruvia and its predecessors served a market that already existed.** Fiducia IT AG and GAD eG were the data-processing houses of the German cooperative banking sector — hundreds of Volksbanken and Raiffeisenbanken that had existed for generations, were regulated, were solvent, and *had* to run on some core system. When Fiducia and GAD merged in 2015 to form what was renamed Atruvia in 2021 (running the agree21 platform), the group grew to nearly 8,400 employees across its subsidiaries — the AG entity alone reporting roughly 5,500 staff and about €1.7 billion in revenue in FY2024. The customer base was captive by construction: the banks owned the utility and could not realistically leave it. Demand was never the question; cost discipline was — Part IV's finding that owners who cannot fire their utility lose cost discipline.

This founder faces the mirror-image problem. **Demand is the entire question.** No functioning cooperative apex infrastructure exists in Nigeria (Part IV). No cluster of cooperatives is accustomed to buying core software. The founder must therefore manufacture demand — persuade societies that currently run on paper, spreadsheets, or nothing that they should pay a recurring fee to an unproven vendor — while simultaneously building the product. A platform with one tenant and a hope is a fundamentally different business from a utility with captive demand: the former carries market risk, execution risk, and financing risk all at once; the latter carries only cost-discipline risk.

The Prologue's Guinnane/Colvin transplant analysis bites directly. Institutional forms do not travel intact; they require local complements to function. Atruvia's model presupposes a dense, pre-existing, federated cooperative banking sector — a complement Nigeria lacks. Transplanting the *technology* without the *federated demand structure* that made it viable in Germany is precisely the kind of partial transplant that fails. The founder is not importing Atruvia; he is importing the software layer of Atruvia into a market with none of Atruvia's institutional preconditions.

**Analytical inference [AI]:** The first-mover advantage the founder hopes to capture — owning the rails before competitors — is real but conditional. It converts to durable advantage only if demand materialises fast enough to fund the build before capital runs out. If demand is slow (the base case in a market with no buying habit), first-mover status becomes first-mover *burden*: paying to educate a market that later entrants will harvest at lower cost.

### VII.2 The ownership decision — the four options, and the case for and against the one chosen

The founder has chosen permanent founder ownership with contractual protection. That choice deserves testing against the alternatives rather than assuming.

**Option 1 — Tenant-owned (the Atruvia model).** The cooperatives that use the platform own it, one-member-one-vote or by federated shareholding. *Mechanism:* a jointly-owned utility funded by member cooperatives, governed by them, serving them at cost. *Incentive properties:* the network genuinely owns its infrastructure; the platform cannot extract monopoly rents from its owners; capability and cost relocate to the network exactly as Part IV describes. *What it costs the tenant:* capital contributions up front, and the Atruvia pathology — the erosion of cost discipline, because owners who cannot fire the utility stop pushing on price. *What it costs the founder:* almost everything; under this model the founder is a hired manager, not an owner, and cannot capture the equity value of the software. *Real-world evidence:* Atruvia itself; the US **CU\*Answers**, a 100% credit-union-owned cooperative CUSO, one-owner-one-vote regardless of size, serving credit unions representing over two million members. **Verdict:** genuinely superior for the network, impossible for a founder with ₦50m who wants to own what he builds — and impossible anyway, because the owning cluster does not exist.

**Option 2 — Founder-owned with a buy-in path.** The founder bootstraps now and commits contractually to sell equity to tenants at a defined threshold. *Mechanism:* founder ownership today, cooperative ownership later. *Incentive properties:* aligns the founder's short-term funding capacity with the network's long-term ownership interest; gives tenants reason to invest in the platform's success. *What it costs the tenant:* uncertainty — buy-in triggers are gameable, and valuation at conversion is where founders and tenants fight. *What it costs the founder:* a capped upside and a governance headache later. *Real-world evidence:* rare and fragile; buy-in paths are more often announced than honoured. **Verdict:** the most *honest* compromise between the founder's economics and the network's interests. Its rejection is defensible on simplicity grounds but forecloses the one route that would let the cooperatives eventually own their rails.

**Option 3 — CUSO-style jointly-owned services company.** Several cooperatives co-own a services company from the outset. *Mechanism:* the US CUSO model — a limited company owned by multiple credit unions/cooperatives, providing core processing and shared services. *Incentive properties:* mutualises both cost and control; the model scales — the NCUA's "CUSOs at a Glance" recorded **1,108 registered CUSOs at 31 December 2024** (1,032 natural-person and 76 corporate, of which 758 wholly-owned). *What it costs the tenant:* requires a pre-existing cluster of cooperatives willing and able to co-invest. *What it costs the founder:* again, no sole ownership. *Real-world evidence:* CU\*Answers (running the CU\*BASE core), CU\*NorthWest (running CBX), CU\*SOUTH — all functioning, all US. **Verdict:** structurally excellent, but Part IV already established there is no Nigerian cluster to co-own anything. Unavailable today for the same reason as Option 1.

**Option 4 — Permanent founder ownership with contractual protection instead of equity. THE CHOSEN MODEL.** *Mechanism:* the founder owns the company forever; tenants get contractual rights (escrow, data portability, price caps, SLAs, exit rights) instead of shares. *Incentive properties:* commercial discipline (keep tenants happy or lose them), no captive-supplier decay, clear accountability, and — decisively — the founder can actually fund and control it. *What it costs the tenant:* the network never owns its infrastructure; Part IV's degeneration lever points squarely at the founder; tenants get form without economics. *What it costs the founder:* he must credibly bind his own hands, because a rational buyer knows an unconstrained owner-vendor will raise prices once switching costs are sunk. *Real-world evidence:* the entire investor-owned core-banking industry — Fiserv, Jack Henry, Temenos, Finastra, Mambu, Oradian. The global norm, not a deviation.

**The honest defence of Option 4.** The advantages are real. Commercial discipline is a genuine benefit precisely where Atruvia is weak: a founder-owned vendor that loses tenants dies, so it has a structural incentive to control cost and improve service that a captive utility lacks. Accountability is clear — one owner, one throat to choke. And the founder can *actually build it*, which Options 1 and 3 cannot deliver in a market with no cluster.

The costs are equally real and must not be softened. Part IV's central finding cuts against the founder: **shared infrastructure relocates cost and capability to the network only when the network owns it; a vendor-owned platform relocates nothing.** The cooperatives get a supplier relationship, full stop. Part III's capture taxonomy names seven types — managerial, board/insider, factional, staff, regional, **external**, and political — and a founder-owned platform is an **external-capture vector by construction.** The founder is the outsider whose lever over the cooperatives grows with every capability they buy rather than federate. Part IV's warning — "every capability bought rather than federated hands a lever of degeneration to an outsider" — applies to this founder, and this Part says so plainly.

**Reasoned position [AI]:** Option 4 is the right choice *given the founder's constraints* (sole ownership, ₦50m, no cluster), and it is the only buildable option today. But it is second-best for the cooperatives, and its defensibility is entirely contingent on the contractual architecture of VII.3 being real, verified, and enforceable. Absent that architecture, Option 4 is lock-in with extra steps. The founder should keep Option 2's buy-in path in reserve: converting to partial tenant ownership once a cluster exists would convert the study's central criticism into a strength and differentiate the platform from every investor-owned competitor.

### VII.3 The contractual protection architecture — the core of the Part

This is what substitutes for equity, and it must be concrete enough for a Nigerian lawyer to draft. For each protection: what it is, what failure it defends against, how it is triggered, how it is enforced.

**(a) Source-code escrow.** A three-party agreement (founder-company as depositor, tenant as beneficiary, a neutral escrow agent) under which the platform deposits source code and supporting materials with the agent, released to tenants on defined trigger events. *Defends against:* the platform disappearing and stranding tenants who cannot maintain, patch, or migrate a system whose source they do not possess. *Release triggers* (draft broadly — narrow triggers are the classic failure mode): (i) insolvency, liquidation or bankruptcy of the platform company; (ii) cessation of business or discontinuation of the product; (iii) sustained, uncured SLA breach beyond a defined threshold; (iv) failure to maintain or support the software for a defined period; (v) change of control to a competitor or any party that repudiates the agreement. *Enforcement:* the beneficiary notifies the agent with documentary proof (court filings, cessation notice); the agent verifies and, after a cure/challenge window, releases.

The realistic limits must be stated bluntly. **A code release without documentation, data, and operational knowledge is close to worthless.** Industry practice is emphatic on two failure modes: *stale, unverified deposits* (vendors deposit once at signing, never refresh, and the beneficiary later opens the escrow to find code that will not compile) and *narrow triggers that never fire* ("formal bankruptcy only" clauses fail when a vendor quietly winds a product down). To make escrow meaningful the contract must specify a **full operational deposit** — source code plus build instructions, dependency lists, configuration files, environment specifications, database schemas, deployment scripts, and documentation "sufficient for a reasonably skilled programmer to compile, maintain, and operate the software." It must require **updates on every release and at least quarterly**, and it must include **verification** — an independent audit confirming the deposit compiles into the running production version. "Verified escrow" means exactly this: someone has confirmed the deposit is complete and buildable, not merely that a sealed envelope exists. Because the platform holds financial data for multiple separate legal entities, the deposit must also include the data-export tooling (see (b)); released code without the tenants' data is a maintenance manual for an empty building.

For a small Nigerian cooperative, escrow's practical value is bounded by capability: even a perfect deposit assumes the tenant can hire developers to run it. The realistic function is therefore not "the cooperative runs the code itself" but "the cooperative, or a successor vendor it hires, can keep the lights on during migration." That is worth having; it should not be oversold.

**(b) Data ownership and portability.** The contract must vest all tenant member data in the tenant, expressly, and grant a right to a full extract on demand and on termination, in a documented, non-proprietary export format (CSV/SQL with a published schema dictionary), at a defined minimum frequency. *Defends against:* data hostage-taking — the single most powerful lock-in lever a core-system vendor holds. *Trigger:* on demand, and automatically on any termination or escrow release. *Enforcement:* liquidated damages for failure to deliver an export within a defined window.

This connects directly to **NDPA 2023**. Under the Act, the **tenant cooperative is the data controller** (it determines the purposes and means of processing its members' data) and the **platform is the data processor** (it processes on the controller's instructions). The contract must say so and must contain the processor obligations the NDPA requires: process only on documented instructions, implement appropriate technical and organisational security measures, assist with data-subject rights and breach notification, and delete or return data on termination. Note the registration overhang: the NDPC's guidance designates data controllers and processors "of major importance" — those processing the personal data of more than 200 data subjects in six months, among other thresholds — as required to register. A platform aggregating many cooperatives' member data will comfortably exceed this and must register as a **processor of major importance**; each tenant of any size will likely qualify as a **controller of major importance**. Both duties should be allocated in the contract.

**(c) Exit rights and migration assistance.** Defined notice periods (e.g. 90 days for the tenant to exit, longer for the platform to exit so tenants are never abruptly stranded), a contractual obligation to provide transition services (data extract, schema documentation, reasonable cooperation with a successor vendor), and a **cost cap on transition services** so exit assistance cannot be priced as a penalty. *Defends against:* the reality that migration off a core system is the most expensive and dangerous event in a small institution's life. Industry data puts core-banking migration for smaller institutions in the millions of dollars; Karl im Brahm, CEO DACH at the financial-services provider Objectway, put the failure rate starkly — "Around 80 percent of all migration projects fail — usually due to incomplete or incorrect data." That figure makes the exit clause's data-quality and documentation obligations load-bearing, not boilerplate. *Enforcement:* the cost cap and the escrow's data-export tooling together make exit *feasible*; without them, "exit rights" are notional.

**(d) Price-change caps.** Indexation of increases to a published index (capped at CPI or a fixed percentage per annum), a minimum notice period before any increase (e.g. 12 months), and a contractual ceiling on cumulative increases over the term. *Defends against:* the classic vendor lock-in squeeze — raising prices once switching costs are sunk. This is the protection a rational buyer will scrutinise most, because it is where a founder-owned vendor's incentive (maximise price post-lock-in) diverges most sharply from the tenant's interest. *Trigger/enforcement:* automatic; breach entitles the tenant to terminate without penalty and invoke migration assistance.

**(e) Service levels.** An availability commitment (e.g. 99.5%+ measured monthly), defined support-response times by severity, and — the hard part for a small tenant — **remedies that actually bite.** Service credits are the norm but weak medicine for a cooperative that cannot switch. The remedy that bites is escalation: sustained SLA breach as an escrow release trigger and as a no-penalty termination right. *Defends against:* degraded service to a captive tenant with no leverage. *Enforcement:* automatic credits for minor breaches; termination plus escrow for sustained breach.

**(f) A tenant advisory council.** A forum where tenants are consulted on the roadmap — without votes. **Candid assessment:** as usually implemented, this is theatre. Consultation without votes is a suggestion box the owner may ignore, and it does nothing to change the ownership economics Part IV identified. What would make it meaningful: (i) a contractual commitment to allocate a defined share of development capacity to tenant-prioritised features; (ii) published minutes and a "comply-or-explain" obligation on the founder to respond in writing to recommendations; (iii) council *sign-off* (not mere consultation) on price changes and major roadmap pivots. Even then it is governance-lite, not governance. It should be offered honestly as a communication mechanism, not sold as a substitute for the voice ownership would confer.

**(g) Business continuity and key-person protection.** The most under-discussed and most acute risk in the whole architecture, flowing directly from the premise that **all five entities have one owner and founder.** The platform is owned by one individual; the tenants' operational continuity therefore depends on that individual's existence, solvency and capacity. If the founder dies, is incapacitated, or becomes insolvent, every tenant cooperative's core banking system is at risk simultaneously. The protections must address this explicitly: (i) **key-person life and disability insurance** with proceeds directed to fund continuity; (ii) a **documented succession and business-continuity plan** naming who runs the platform if the founder cannot; (iii) **escrow release triggered by the founder's death or incapacity**, not merely corporate insolvency; (iv) a **corporate structure that survives the individual** — held in a company with a functioning board or trustee arrangement, not a sole proprietorship or a company whose every function depends on one person's login; (v) **operational runbooks in escrow** so a successor can *operate*, not just compile, the system. A founder-owned platform that has not solved key-person risk is offering its tenants a continuity it cannot actually promise.

**Summary position [AI]:** These seven protections, drafted with the specificity above, restore to the tenant most of what equity would have given *except upside and control*: continuity, data sovereignty, exit feasibility, price predictability. They do not restore ownership. A cooperative signing this contract should understand it is buying a well-protected supplier relationship, not an asset — and the founder should present it exactly that way.

### VII.4 The cold-start problem

The founder must build a multi-tenant platform with one tenant, in a market with no cooperative apex infrastructure and therefore no established buying habit.

**How many cooperatives, and what do they use?** Nigeria has a large cooperative sector — membership commonly cited in the tens of millions across the 36 states and the FCT, and the Federal Government's reform programme targets more than 30,000 registered cooperative societies. Cooperatives are registered by state Registrars under the NCSA, not federally supervised, and the sector's records are notoriously fragmented — the government's own rhetoric cites "ghost cooperatives," manual recordkeeping, and the need for a unified database. The realistic answer to "what do they currently use" is: **paper, spreadsheets, or nothing**, with a minority on early cooperative-management software (Probitycoop, Coopco, Prolean, CoopSolve, Clé) or a bank-led offering (Wema Bank's CoopHub). This is both the opportunity (a greenfield) and the problem (no buying habit, no budget line, no IT staff).

**A realistic first cohort.** Not paper-based village societies — they have neither the money nor the felt need. The realistic first cohort is the **larger, employer-linked and professional cooperatives**: staff multipurpose cooperatives at banks, universities and large employers, and urban thrift-and-credit societies with thousands of salaried members, existing (if painful) digitisation, and a management committee capable of signing a recurring contract. These are the societies for whom manual processing is already breaking down and who can pay.

**The sales cycle.** Selling into a cooperative means selling to a **management committee** — an elected, part-time, consensus-driven body, often risk-averse, with annual general meetings and rotating officers. This is a long, relationship-heavy sale with multiple veto points and little urgency. It resembles enterprise selling more than consumer fintech: months, not weeks, per deal, with the added friction that the buyer has no prior category experience and may not have budgeted for software at all.

**The reference-customer problem.** No cooperative wants to be the first on an unproven system holding its members' savings. But there is no reference customer until someone is first — the classic chicken-and-egg of enterprise infrastructure sales, sharpened here because the anchor tenant is the founder's *own* cooperative (entity two), which both solves the problem (guaranteed first customer) and creates the anchor-tenant capture problem (VII.6).

**The honest question: product or bespoke build?** A platform with one anchor tenant, especially one the founder controls, is at serious risk of being a **bespoke build wearing a product's clothes.** Every feature is specified by, and for, the single tenant; the "roadmap" is that tenant's wish list; the architecture ossifies around its workflows. It becomes a *product* only when a second, independent tenant on commercial terms forces the generalisation of features, the separation of configuration from code, and the discipline of serving needs the founder does not control. **Reasoned conclusion [AI]:** until an independent paying tenant exists, the founder should treat the software as a bespoke build with product *aspirations*, budget accordingly, and resist the temptation to believe the anchor tenant has validated a market. One captive customer validates nothing about willingness to pay.

### VII.5 Multi-tenancy — when, and what retrofitting costs

**The distinction.** In a *single-tenant* architecture each customer gets a dedicated application instance and database; in a *multi-tenant* architecture one application instance and shared infrastructure serve many customers, whose data is logically isolated. Multi-tenancy is the economic engine of SaaS: updates deploy once for all tenants, operational overhead stays roughly flat as tenant count grows, and unit cost falls. Single-tenancy offers stronger isolation and per-customer customisation at the cost of multiplying infrastructure and maintenance for every new customer.

**Why it matters acutely for financial data.** The tenants are *separate legal entities* holding *other people's money*. Data isolation is not a nicety; it is a fiduciary and regulatory requirement. A cross-tenant leak — one cooperative seeing another's members' balances — is catastrophic and, under NDPA 2023, a reportable breach. Multi-tenant financial systems therefore need robust logical isolation (at minimum per-tenant schema separation, ideally with row-level security and rigorous authorisation testing), because the failure mode of multi-tenancy is precisely a broken access control exposing data across tenants.

**Configurability versus customisation — the fork trap.** The trap that kills small platforms: each tenant asks for something bespoke, the vendor forks the codebase per tenant, and within a few tenants the "platform" is actually N divergent single-tenant systems that must each be maintained separately. The discipline that prevents this is **configuration, not customisation** — tenant-specific behaviour expressed as data/settings within one shared codebase (custom fields, configurable products, feature flags), never as per-tenant code branches. Oradian's own pitch — set unlimited custom fields and launch new products without complex engineering — is exactly the configuration-over-customisation discipline, and it is the right instinct.

**The timing decision.** Two paths: (A) build multi-tenant from day one at higher initial cost and slower time-to-first-tenant; or (B) build for the anchor tenant now and refactor later. Path B is cheaper today and is what a cash-constrained founder is tempted to do. But a system built for one tenant is not a platform, and **converting single-tenant to multi-tenant later is expensive and sometimes impossible** — the isolation model, the tenant-identity threading through every query, and the configuration layer are architectural foundations, not features to bolt on. Retrofitting means, in practice, a substantial rewrite.

**Recommendation and trigger [AI]:** Build **multi-tenant-ready from day one at the data-architecture layer** — every table tenant-scoped, tenant identity threaded through the data model, isolation enforced in the database — even while the UI and feature set are built for the anchor tenant only. This is the cheapest insurance available: modestly more up front, avoiding the rewrite. Do *not* build the full multi-tenant operational apparatus (self-service onboarding, per-tenant billing, tenant admin consoles) until the **trigger condition** is met: a signed second, independent, paying tenant. Architecture multi-tenant from day one; operational multi-tenancy on first external contract. This sequences cost to match validated demand while avoiding the fatal single-tenant lock-in.

### VII.6 The anchor-tenant capture problem

The founder owns *both* the platform and its first tenant cooperative (entity two). A structural conflict with three faces.

**Roadmap conflict.** A roadmap serving the anchor tenant produces a system exquisitely fitted to *one* cooperative's needs — and therefore a poor fit when tenant two arrives with different products, workflows and reporting needs. Conversely, a roadmap serving hypothetical future tenants underserves the only paying customer the founder actually has. There is no neutral answer; the founder is his own conflicted customer.

**Related-party pricing.** The price the platform charges the founder's own cooperative is neither arm's-length nor transparent. It can be set artificially low (flattering the cooperative's economics and the platform's apparent traction) or artificially high (extracting cash from the cooperative into the platform) — and *no external party can tell which*, because both entities have the same owner. This corrupts every metric a would-be second tenant, investor, or regulator might rely on: the anchor tenant's "willingness to pay" is fictional, and the platform's "revenue" is a book entry moving money from one pocket to another.

**Connection to Part III's capture taxonomy.** This is **external capture** turned inward and doubled: the founder captures the cooperative via the platform (the outsider's lever), and simultaneously uses the cooperative to launder the platform's commercial validation. Part III's finding that **the substitute monitor is the best single predictor of degeneration** bites hard — who monitors related-party dealing between two entities with one owner? In Nigeria, effectively no one: cooperatives are lightly supervised by state Registrars, the platform is an unlicensed software company, and there is no arm's-length board on either side by default.

**Available disciplines.** (i) **Transfer-pricing discipline** — price the anchor tenant at the *same published rate* an external tenant would pay, documented as if to a tax authority, even though no one is forcing it. (ii) **A published rate card** — commit to public, tiered pricing so the anchor tenant's price is visibly the standard price, not a sweetheart or a shakedown. (iii) **An independent tenant on genuinely commercial terms as early as possible** — the single most powerful discipline, because it forces real pricing, real feature generalisation and real arm's-length behaviour. (iv) **Separate boards/governance** for the platform and the cooperative, with the related-party relationship disclosed to the cooperative's members. **Reasoned position [AI]:** treat the anchor tenant as a *proving ground, not a validation* — useful for shaking out bugs and workflows, useless as evidence of demand — and race to a second, independent, paying tenant, because that event dissolves all three faces of the conflict at once.

### VII.7 Pricing that disciplines rather than merely recovers cost

Part IV established the two failure poles. Atruvia's levy (*Umlage*) recovers cost from owners who cannot leave — so cost discipline erodes. A founder-owned vendor has the *opposite* incentive: to maximise price once switching costs are sunk. The pricing model must therefore be one a buyer who *knows they will be locked in* can rationally accept.

**The models, assessed:**

- **Per-member (per active member per period).** *Alignment:* good — the vendor earns more as the cooperative grows its membership, the outcome the cooperative wants (Kwara uses a member-fee component). *Predictability:* good. *As the tenant grows:* scales with success, fair but potentially expensive for large cooperatives; needs a volume taper.
- **Per-account.** *Alignment:* moderate — rewards account proliferation, which may not track value. *Predictability:* moderate. *Growth:* similar to per-member but noisier.
- **Per-transaction.** *Alignment:* poor for a savings-and-credit cooperative — penalises the very activity (members transacting) the cooperative wants to encourage, and makes costs unpredictable. Reserve transaction pricing for genuinely variable-cost integrations (payment rails passed through at cost).
- **Flat subscription.** *Alignment:* moderate — simple but regressive (crushing for a tiny cooperative, trivial for a large one). *Predictability:* excellent. *Growth:* the vendor captures none of the tenant's growth.
- **Tiered by asset size / membership band.** *Alignment:* good — ability-to-pay tracks cooperative size; SASRA-style tiering is familiar in the comparable Kenyan market. *Predictability:* good. *Growth:* smooth, with visible step-ups the tenant can plan for.
- **Revenue-share.** Superficially strong (vendor prospers only if tenant does) but *deeply problematic* here — it gives the founder-owned platform a claim on the cooperative's surplus, which is the members' money, sharpening the external-capture and degeneration concerns. Avoid.

**Recommendation [AI]:** a **tiered subscription banded by membership size, with a modest per-active-member component and a published rate card** — mirroring the model that works in the most comparable market. Kwara charges a fixed annual subscription plus a member fee, with no charge for upgrades or maintenance and unlimited user licences and member accounts. Pass through only genuinely variable third-party costs (payment-rail fees, SMS) at cost, transparently. Crucially, the pricing must be wrapped in the **VII.3(d) caps to be credible to a locked-in buyer**: published rate card, indexation capped at CPI or a stated ceiling, 12-month notice on increases, a cumulative-increase cap over the term, and a right to exit without penalty (with migration assistance) if the cap is breached. Pricing that disciplines is pricing the vendor has *contractually promised not to abuse* — the caps are what convert lock-in into a defensible bargain.

### VII.8 The regulatory question — does the platform need a licence?

This question had not been asked before in the study. Established from primary CBN sources, the answer is: **a pure software platform needs no CBN licence of its own — but the moment it touches funds, switches transactions, or processes payments, it does, and the moment its tenants are licensed institutions, CBN vendor-risk expectations reach it indirectly.**

**Does pure software require a CBN licence?** No. The CBN's *New Licence Categorisations for the Nigerian Payments System* (Payments System Management Department, ref. PSM/DIR/CON/CWD/14/152, dated 9 December 2020, compliance by end-June 2021) licenses defined *activities*, and the object clauses of a licensee's memorandum "shall be limited to the permissible activities under their licensing authorizations." The permissible activities of a **PSSP (₦100m)** are "payment processing gateway and portals, payment solution/application development, merchant service aggregation and collections." **Switching and Processing (₦2bn)** covers "switching, card processing, transaction clearing and settlement agent services, non-bank acquiring." And, decisively, "**only MMOs are permitted to hold customer funds**" — MMO capital being ₦2bn. A platform providing ledgering, member management, loan tracking and reporting — that does **not** process/route payments, does **not** hold or touch customer funds, and does **not** take deposits — performs none of these licensable activities and needs none of these licences. This is a reasoned reading of the permissible-activities language, not an express CBN exemption for "software"; the CBN regulates by defining licensable activities, and pure operations software is not among them.

**The crossing points into regulated activity.** The platform crosses the line when it: (i) **processes payments** — building a payment gateway/portal or payment application → **PSSP, ₦100m**; (ii) **switches/routes transactions** between institutions → **Switching, ₦2bn**; (iii) **holds customer money** (a float or wallet) → **MMO, ₦2bn**; or (iv) acts as an **agent** recruiting agents for agency banking → Super-Agent (₦50m, per Part VI). For the remittance capability (entity three) and any funds-touching feature, these thresholds — not the software itself — are the trigger. Design implication: keep the core platform strictly on the software side of the line and route all funds movement through *separately licensed* rails or partners, preserving the platform's unlicensed status for as long as possible.

**Does BOFIA 2020 §2 catch the platform?** No — §2 bars unlicensed *banking business*, and §2(5) deems a person to be conducting banking business by accepting deposits "from the general public." A software vendor accepts no deposits; its tenants might, but that is the tenants' regulatory problem, not the platform's. BOFIA 2020's expanded definitions (via §131) sweep digital/electronic deposit-taking, payments and investment activity into "banking business" — reinforcing that the line is *touching funds*, not *writing software*.

**Do CBN outsourcing/vendor-risk expectations bind a platform serving licensed institutions?** Indirectly — and this is the operative constraint. **There is no standalone CBN outsourcing guideline** — Nigeria has no equivalent of the Bank of Ghana or MAS outsourcing directives, and no "material outsourcing / critical service provider" prior-approval regime. Instead, the **CBN Risk-Based Cybersecurity Framework and Guidelines for Other Financial Institutions** (OFI Supervision Department, ref. OFI/DOA/CON/ACT/004/155, issued 29 June 2022, effective 1 January 2023) places obligations on the **licensed institution**: Senior Management is responsible for "evaluating and managing any risks introduced by third-party service providers"; and the "Know Your Environment" control (Part 4 / Appendix II) requires the OFI to maintain an inventory of authorised software and vendors, to "identify and document all connections to third parties," to evaluate a service provider's security controls *before* adoption, to review SLAs, and to revoke discontinued providers' access. The **CBN Guidelines for the Regulation and Supervision of Microfinance Banks** (January 2020, effective 1 April 2020) cross-reference this cybersecurity framework (§12) and require MFBs at licensing to demonstrate IT capability and "integration of their infrastructure with the National Payments System" — but impose **no requirement to seek CBN approval merely for adopting a new core banking application or changing IT vendor.** So: once tenants include MFBs, the platform is not licensed but must be *audit-ready* — able to satisfy each tenant's due-diligence, security-control, SLA and data obligations, because the tenant's compliance depends on it.

**NDPA 2023 obligations and NDPC registration.** The platform is a **data processor** and must meet the NDPA's processor duties (process on documented instructions, security measures, breach reporting, assist with data-subject rights). Because it processes personal data at scale it will qualify as a **data processor of major importance** and must register with the NDPC (the threshold guidance captures those processing the data of more than 200 data subjects in six months, among other tests); each tenant will likely register as a controller of major importance. A live, non-optional obligation with penalties for default.

**NITDA's role.** NITDA (under the NITDA Act 2007) regulates IT development and issues the Nigerian Content Development Guidelines and, more recently, software-testing/quality guidelines. For a privately-sold platform (not selling into government MDAs), NITDA's local-content *procurement* rules are not directly binding, but its data-localisation and software-quality expectations, and the NDPC/NITDA overlap on data, form part of the compliance backdrop. **UNKNOWN [U]:** whether NITDA's evolving software-testing/certification regime will, in future, impose mandatory certification on *privately-sold* financial software (as distinct from government-procured software) is not established from a primary source; the reader should watch the NITDA guidelines index and consult the current National Software Testing Guideline for scope.

**Bottom line [AI]:** build and operate the platform as an unlicensed software company; keep funds movement out of the platform and in separately licensed rails; register with the NDPC as a processor of major importance; and engineer the platform from day one to be *tenant-audit-ready* against the OFI cybersecurity framework, because that — not a platform licence — is the real regulatory cost of serving licensed institutions.

### VII.9 Build versus buy for the platform itself

The founder is building a platform but need not build every layer.

- **Core ledger and member/savings/shares management.** *Build.* The differentiating core and the asset the founder wants to own; also where cooperative-specific logic (shares, dividends, guarantor lending, thrift contributions) diverges from generic banking. Buying it forfeits the ownership rationale of the whole enterprise.
- **Loan management.** *Build*, tightly integrated with the ledger — cooperative lending (guarantor chains, salary-linked deductions) is idiosyncratic and central to the value proposition.
- **Reporting.** *Build the cooperative-specific statutory/AGM reports; buy the general BI/analytics layer.* Regulatory and cooperative reporting is a selling point; generic dashboards are a commodity.
- **Integration layer — NIBSS, BVN/NIN, payment rails.** *Buy/integrate.* These are provided via APIs and aggregators; building them is wasted effort and, for funds movement, would drag the platform across the licensing line. Integrate through licensed partners.
- **Identity/KYC (BVN/NIN).** *Integrate* via existing verification providers.
- **Infrastructure (hosting, security).** *Buy* (cloud, with NDPA-compliant local data-residency considerations).

**The white-label / reseller alternative.** The founder could **white-label or resell an existing core banking system** rather than build proprietary software. *Margin:* worse — licensing fees to the upstream vendor compress margin. *Differentiation:* worse — the founder sells what others can also sell and cannot deeply tailor it to Nigerian cooperative workflows. *Speed and risk:* much better — time-to-first-tenant collapses from years to months, and the technical risk of building a core ledger from scratch (the part most likely to fail) transfers to a proven vendor. *Bureau strategy (Part VIII):* mixed — reselling gets tenants (and therefore data) faster, which *helps* the eventual bureau, but the data rights depend on the upstream vendor's contract, which *may constrain* the founder's ability to aggregate and use tenant data. **Reasoned recommendation [AI]:** given ₦50m and a three-year, five-entity ambition, the founder should *seriously consider building on an open-source core (the Mifos/Apache Fineract lineage that underpins Musoni) rather than from a blank sheet* — capturing most of the ownership and differentiation upside while avoiding the highest-risk, highest-cost part of the build. Pure from-scratch proprietary development is the most defensible for the bureau strategy and the most expensive and slowest; a full third-party white-label is the fastest and cheapest but weakest on margin, differentiation and data rights. The open-source-core middle path best fits the capital constraint.

### VII.10 The competitive landscape

**Nigeria.** The cooperative-software niche is populated but shallow: **Probitycoop/Coopify, Coopco, Prolean, CoopSolve and Clé** offer cooperative-management software, largely to individual societies; **Wema Bank's CoopHub** is a commercial bank's play for the same customers; general accounting tools (QuickBooks customised) fill gaps. For microfinance banks and larger institutions, **Oradian** (its Instafin core launched from Bauchi State in 2013, now serving MFIs including via FairMoney, and explicitly targeting cooperative societies in West Africa) is the most credible incumbent, alongside global cloud cores. The niche is *thinly served at the top* — most Nigerian cooperative tools are lightweight, single-society administrative apps, not multi-tenant core banking with payment and identity integration.

**Kenya — the most developed comparable.** Kenya's SACCO software market is the reference case. **Kwara** (founded 2018 by Cynthia Wandia and David Hwan; ~230 SACCOs; a fixed annual subscription plus member fee; USSD access via *341# for feature-phone members) is the venture-backed, digital-first player. It has raised **$7 million total seed funding — a $4m round led by Breega in December 2021 plus a $3m seed extension in January 2023** — and in early 2023 acquired KUSCCO's software subsidiary IRNET Coop Kenya. **Craft Silicon's Bankers Realm (BR SACCO)** and **Fintech's FinSacco** were assessed by an FSD Kenya/WOCCU study as offering the best price-performance for Kenyan SACCOs, with strong local presence. **Musoni** (built on the Mifos/Fineract platform, cloud-native, flexible pricing by organisation size) serves MFIs and SACCOs across East Africa. Numerous smaller vendors (CoopMIS, Vanguard/Centrino, Dynamics-based Coretec deployments) fill the tiers. The Kenyan lesson: a viable market emerged *because* SASRA supervision created a compliance-reporting need software solves, and *because* M-PESA created a rail worth integrating — two structural demand drivers Nigeria's cooperative sector currently lacks.

**Global credit-union vendors.** Investor-owned: **Fiserv, Jack Henry, Temenos, Finastra, Mambu** — the model the founder is copying. Credit-union-*owned*: **CU\*Answers** (100% credit-union-owned CUSO, running CU\*BASE, one-owner-one-vote) and its network partners (CU\*NorthWest running CBX) — the model the founder is *not* copying, and the living proof that the cooperative-ownership alternative works at scale in a mature market.

**Where a Nigerian entrant can differentiate.** (i) **Cooperative-native design** — Nigerian workflows (thrift, guarantor lending, salary deductions, AGM/statutory reporting under state cooperative law) that generic MFI cores handle awkwardly. (ii) **Local integration** — NIBSS, BVN/NIN, and USSD for feature-phone members (Kwara's *341# is the template). (iii) **Price for the mid-market** — below global cores, above the lightweight local apps. (iv) **The digitisation tailwind** — the FG's **Renewed Hope Cooperative Reform and Revamp Programme (RH-CRRP) 2025–2030**, led by the Minister of State for Agriculture and Food Security, Sen. Dr. Aliyu Sabi Abdullahi, will assign a Cooperative Verification Number to every registered society and a Cooperative Members Identification Number to individual cooperators, both integrated with the National Identity Number architecture; aligning with this could confer distribution advantage. **Honest caveat [AI]:** the Nigerian cooperative-software market is thinly served at the top *because it is hard to serve profitably* — fragmented buyers, no compliance-driven demand (unlike SASRA-regulated Kenya), no dominant rail (unlike M-PESA), and management-committee sales cycles. The differentiation is real; the profitability is unproven; and the FG initiative is as much a competitive threat as a tailwind — the proposed **Cooperative Bank of Nigeria** (structured with 65% controlling equity reserved for cooperative societies, 30% for institutional and private investors, and the FG mobilising ₦200 billion of share capital for take-off) could become a state-blessed incumbent occupying exactly the founder's intended space.

### VII.11 The ₦50 million reality

₦50m is, per Part VI, exactly one Super-Agent licence and nothing else. Confront what it buys in platform development in Nigeria in 2026.

**Team cost.** Nigerian engineering salaries have risen sharply as local fintechs (Flutterwave, Paystack, Moniepoint, Interswitch) and global remote employers compete for talent. Mid-level engineers command several million naira per year each in total compensation, and senior engineers considerably more; a credible core-banking build needs at least a small senior-led team (a lead, two or three engineers, and part-time product/QA/security) plus the founder. Even at conservative local rates, a four-to-five-person team's fully-loaded annual cost runs to a substantial fraction of ₦50m *per year*, before infrastructure, security tooling, NDPC registration, legal (the VII.3 contracts and escrow) and sales.

**Build duration.** A multi-tenant core banking system — ledger, member/savings/shares, loan management, reporting, integrations, with the data isolation and security financial data demands — is not a six-month build. Realistically it is a multi-year effort to reach production quality for even one tenant, and longer to reach true multi-tenant robustness.

**Is a multi-tenant core banking system achievable at ₦50m?** **Not as a from-scratch proprietary build.** ₦50m is roughly one year of a small serious team's fully-loaded cost — not the multi-year runway a ground-up core requires, and it leaves nothing for the licence, the contracts, or sales. The arithmetic is stark: ₦50m either funds ~one year of build (and no licence, no revenue-generating entity), or funds one Super-Agent licence (and no build). It cannot do both.

**The sequencing consequence.** This is the crux. If the platform consumes the capital, no licence is obtained and entities three-to-five are stranded. If the licence is obtained, the platform must be built on **revenue or founder sweat-equity** — slowly, part-time, or by pre-selling to an anchor tenant who funds the build (recreating the anchor-capture problem of VII.6). There is no configuration of ₦50m that simultaneously delivers a robust multi-tenant platform *and* a licensed entity within three years.

**Is "all five started within three years" achievable on ₦50m? [AI]** **No — not as a self-funded, from-scratch build.** Stated plainly: ₦50m is enough to *start* one thing well or several things badly. "Started" can be stretched — one could incorporate five companies and call them "started" — but *started as viable going concerns* is not achievable on ₦50m. What would have to change, in order of leverage:

1. **Redefine the platform build as an open-source-core assembly** (VII.9) rather than a ground-up build — cutting the most expensive, riskiest, longest layer and bringing a first version within reach of the budget and timeline.
2. **Pre-sell the anchor tenant to fund the build** — accepting and *managing* (via VII.6 disciplines) the related-party tension.
3. **Sequence, don't parallelise** — build and prove the platform and one cooperative first (years 1–2), obtain the Super-Agent licence from platform revenue (year 2–3), and treat remittance, apex and bureau as *later* stages beyond the three-year window.
4. **Raise external capital** — the Kenyan comparators (Kwara's $7m across seed rounds) show this category is venture-fundable; ₦50m of founder capital is seed-of-seed money by the standards of what building a real core banking platform costs. External capital, however, reintroduces exactly the outside-ownership dynamic the founder sought to avoid by owning the platform outright — a tension the founder must reconcile.

The kindest honest verdict: **₦50m starts the platform *or* buys the licence; the five-entity, three-year plan is a five-to-seven-year plan on this capital, unless the founder either raises money or radically de-scopes the build.**

### VII.12 Ten propositions on founder-owned cooperative infrastructure

1. **A vendor-owned platform relocates nothing to the network.** Part IV's benefit — cost and capability moving to the cooperatives — accrues only under network ownership. This platform is a supplier; the cooperatives gain a service, not an asset. State this to every prospective tenant.
2. **The founder is the external-capture vector.** By Part III's taxonomy, a founder-owned platform is external capture by construction. The contractual architecture is not decoration; it is the only thing standing between "supplier" and "captor."
3. **Contracts substitute for equity imperfectly.** Escrow, portability and price caps restore continuity, data sovereignty and predictability — but never ownership or upside. Sell the relationship as what it is.
4. **Unverified escrow is theatre.** Escrow protects tenants only if the deposit is complete (code, build tools, docs, data-export tooling, runbooks), refreshed at least quarterly, and independently verified to compile. Anything less is a sealed envelope of nothing.
5. **Key-person risk is the platform's original sin.** One owner means every tenant's continuity hangs on one life. Insurance, succession, a surviving corporate structure, and death/incapacity escrow triggers are non-negotiable.
6. **One captive customer validates nothing.** The anchor tenant proves the software runs; it proves nothing about willingness to pay. Until a second, independent, paying tenant signs, this is a bespoke build, not a product.
7. **Multi-tenant at the data layer from day one; multi-tenant operations on first external contract.** Isolation retrofitted is isolation rewritten. Thread tenant identity through the data model from the first commit; defer the operational apparatus until demand is validated.
8. **Price with caps or not at all.** A founder-owned vendor's incentive is to squeeze post-lock-in. Tiered subscription banded by size, a published rate card, CPI-capped indexation, and no-penalty exit on breach are what make lock-in defensible to a buyer who can see it coming.
9. **The platform is unlicensed until it touches money — keep it that way.** Route all funds movement through separately licensed rails; register with the NDPC as a processor of major importance; and engineer the platform to be tenant-audit-ready against the CBN OFI cybersecurity framework, because that indirect obligation, not a platform licence, is the real regulatory cost.
10. **₦50m starts the platform or buys the licence, not both.** The five-entity, three-year ambition is not fundable on ₦50m as a from-scratch build. De-scope to an open-source-core assembly, pre-sell the anchor, sequence rather than parallelise, or raise capital — and accept that raising capital reintroduces the outside owner the model was built to avoid.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 (months 0–12): De-risk the build and the ownership story.**
- Choose the **open-source-core assembly path** (Mifos/Fineract lineage) over from-scratch proprietary development. *Benchmark:* a working single-tenant instance for the anchor cooperative within 9–12 months. *Threshold to switch to full build:* only if the open-source core cannot support cooperative-native workflows (thrift/guarantor/dividend logic) after genuine effort.
- Architect **multi-tenant at the data layer from day one**; defer operational multi-tenancy.
- Draft the **full VII.3 contract suite** now (escrow, data/NDPA processor terms, price caps, exit, SLA, key-person) — before the first tenant, not after. *Benchmark:* verified escrow live with the first tenant, not promised.
- **Register with the NDPC** as a data processor of major importance.

**Stage 2 (months 6–24): Prove product, not just software.**
- Sign a **second, independent, paying tenant** on the published rate card. *This is the single most important benchmark in the whole plan* — it dissolves the anchor-capture conflict, validates pricing, and converts bespoke build into product. If no independent tenant will pay within 18 months of a working product, the market thesis is falsified; stop and reassess.
- Operate the anchor cooperative at **arm's-length, published pricing** with separate governance and disclosed related-party status.

**Stage 3 (months 18–36): Sequence the licensed entities.**
- Obtain the **Super-Agent licence (₦50m)** from platform revenue, not founder seed capital — meaning the platform must be generating cash by month 18–24.
- Treat **remittance, apex and bureau as post-three-year stages.** Do not attempt to parallelise them on ₦50m.

**Capital trigger.** If founder capital remains the only source by month 12 and the build is behind, **raise external capital** — and pre-decide how to reconcile outside ownership of the platform with the founder's control thesis (e.g. dual-class structure, or revisiting Option 2's tenant buy-in path as a differentiator). *Benchmark that changes the plan:* if external capital is raised, the three-year five-entity plan becomes realistic; if not, formally re-baseline to a five-to-seven-year horizon.

**Reconsider Option 2.** Once a cluster of 5–10 tenants exists, offering a **tenant buy-in path** would convert the study's central criticism (the network owns nothing) into a market differentiator no investor-owned competitor can match. Keep it in reserve.

## Caveats

- **Vendor and federation sources are promotional and are treated as such.** Kwara's, Oradian's, CU\*Answers's and the Nigerian cooperative-software vendors' own materials are marketing; their client counts, uptime claims and pricing descriptions are self-reported and uncorroborated by audited data. Kwara's "~230 SACCOs" and CU\*Answers's "over two million members" are vendor/association figures.
- **Migration-cost figures are drawn from general and largely non-Nigerian sources.** The "millions of dollars" and the "around 80 percent of migration projects fail" figure (attributed to Karl im Brahm of Objectway) come from vendor/consultancy commentary on bank core migrations generally; they establish direction and magnitude, not a Nigerian cooperative-specific benchmark. Treat as [CL]/[AI], not [CF].
- **The regulatory conclusion is a reasoned reading, not an express exemption.** The CBN does not publish a sentence saying "pure software needs no licence"; the conclusion is inferred from the permissible-activities language of the 2020 categorisations and confirmed by the absence of any contrary provision in the MFB Guidelines. A definitive comfort letter would require CBN engagement. The absence of a dedicated CBN outsourcing guideline is a *negative finding* — strong, but confirmable only against the full CBN circulars index.
- **NITDA's forward trajectory is [U].** Whether NITDA's software-testing/certification regime will bind privately-sold financial software is not established from a primary source.
- **Cooperative-sector statistics are soft.** "Tens of millions of members" and "more than 30,000 registered societies" come from government and press statements, not an audited census; the sector's own reform rhetoric admits records are fragmented and populated with "ghost" entities. The Cooperative Bank of Nigeria's equity structure (65%/30%) and ₦200bn share-capital target are ministerial announcements of a *proposed* institution, not an operating reality.
- **Atruvia's staffing figure is a group total.** "Nearly 8,400" refers to the Atruvia group including subsidiaries; the AG entity alone reported roughly 5,500 staff and about €1.7bn revenue in FY2024.
- **The ₦50m arithmetic depends on 2026 salary data** dispersed across self-reported sources; the qualitative conclusion (₦50m cannot fund both a robust build and a licence) is robust to that dispersion, but the precise team-cost figures are indicative, not audited.
