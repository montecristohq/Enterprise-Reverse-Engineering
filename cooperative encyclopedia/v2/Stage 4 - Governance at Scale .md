# PART III — GOVERNANCE AT SCALE

*The Cooperative Encyclopedia: Legal Form, Capital, Governance and the Nigerian Path*

> Part I supplied the legal half of the institutional environment that decides whether the property-rights mechanisms fire or misfire. Part III supplies the governance half. The question it answers for the founder is narrow and practical: **across six jurisdictions, which governance mechanisms actually preserve member control at scale, which merely perform democracy while insulating management, and which of them can a Nigerian cooperative adopt by bye-law given that the statute supplies almost nothing?**

---

## III.1 The problem: why one-member-one-vote breaks at scale

### III.1.1 Formalising Olson for the cooperative

Part I established the legal architecture of member voting; this Part asks whether that architecture is used. The theoretical engine, carried forward from the Prologue, is Mancur Olson's *The Logic of Collective Action* (1965): in a large group, the rational individual will not contribute to a collective good because his individual contribution is negligible while its cost is private and certain. Monitoring management and participating in governance is precisely such a collective good. The benefit of good governance is shared by all members; the cost of producing it — reading the accounts, attending the meeting, forming a view, standing a candidate — falls entirely on the participating member.

Formalise the individual member's calculus. Let a cooperative have *n* members. Let *B* be the total member-welfare gain from a member casting an informed, monitoring vote rather than an uninformed or absent one — the expected improvement in the institution's decisions attributable to one additional scrutinising vote. Let *c* be the private cost to the member of becoming informed and voting: time to read disclosures, to travel to and sit through the AGM, to evaluate candidates. A rational member participates only if his *share* of the expected benefit exceeds his private cost.

Two features collapse that inequality as *n* grows. First, the member's influence on the outcome is, at most, of order 1/*n*: with one vote among *n*, the probability that his vote is decisive, and the fraction of any collective gain he can attribute to his own action, both fall roughly as 1/*n*. Second, his share of the benefit *B* — even if the good is produced — is also of order *B*/*n*, because the gain is spread across the whole membership. The private cost *c*, by contrast, does not fall with *n*; it is fixed or even rising (larger, more complex institutions are harder to monitor). So the participation condition, roughly *B*/*n* > *c*, fails for all but the smallest *n* or the largest personal stake. **ANALYTICAL INFERENCE (reasoning shown):** rational abstention is therefore not a moral failing of members but the predicted equilibrium of the ownership structure. As *n* rises, the rational member abstains; as outcomes become more homogeneous (every candidate is much like every other, every year's accounts resemble the last), the marginal *B* of scrutiny falls too, reinforcing abstention.

This is the exact governance analogue of Cook's **control problem** carried forward from the Prologue: dispersed residual claimants, each holding a tiny, non-saleable stake, with no share price and no takeover market to discipline management. In an investor-owned firm, an under-monitored management is disciplined by a falling share price and the threat of acquisition; the residual claimant who does the monitoring can buy more shares and capture the gain. In a cooperative, the member who monitors cannot increase his stake, cannot sell it at a premium if he improves the firm, and cannot be bought out by a raider who will. The private return to monitoring is therefore close to zero, and monitoring falls, as the Prologue put it, "to a volunteer board and an apathetic membership."

### III.1.2 Apathy and capture: the first enables the second

Two distinct phenomena must be kept separate. **Apathy** is rational non-participation: the member who does not vote, does not attend, does not stand. **Capture** is active minority control: a subgroup — managers, a lay board clique, a faction, a political patron — that does participate, precisely because *its* private benefit from control is large. The decisive analytical point is that **apathy is the enabling condition for capture.** Where the great majority abstains because their per-capita stake in control is trivial, the small minority whose stake in control is large faces an open field. The 1/*n* logic that silences the ordinary member simultaneously amplifies anyone for whom control yields concentrated private benefit: the executive whose salary and autonomy depend on it, the director whose status and access to credit depend on it, the politician whose patronage network depends on it. Olson's large-group failure and the capture that follows are two faces of one mechanism.

### III.1.3 Observable symptoms

The mechanism is a hypothesis about incentives; its footprints are observable. The literature and the regulatory record document a consistent syndrome: falling AGM attendance; uncontested board elections in which the number of candidates equals the number of seats; low turnout in the ballots that do occur; board self-perpetuation, where incumbents are routinely returned and the same individuals rotate through the offices; and the migration of real authority from the board to a professional management that possesses the information, continuity and expertise the volunteer directors lack. **CONTESTED / MEASUREMENT CAVEAT:** none of these symptoms is directly and systematically measured across jurisdictions. Turnout and contestation data are not collected by most regulators (see III.2.5 and III.4). The syndrome is well-attested anecdotally and in case studies but poorly quantified — a point this Part will repeatedly flag rather than paper over.

### III.1.4 The paradox stated precisely

The result is a paradox at the centre of the whole study. **The democratic legitimacy that justifies the cooperative form is strongest when the cooperative is small — when *n* is low, per-capita stakes in control are high, members know one another and monitoring is cheap — and weakest when the cooperative is large. But scale is exactly what the capital constraint of Part II demands.** Part II showed that a Nigerian cooperative fintech cannot bridge organically to CBN capital walls, that the §27 20% shareholding cap forbids a concentrated equity raise, and that licensed scale forces a two-tier structure. Scale is the price of survival on the capital frontier; degraded member control is the price of scale. Part III is, in effect, the study of how six jurisdictions have tried — with varying honesty and varying success — to refuse that trade, and of how much of their machinery a Nigerian founder can borrow.

## III.2 The empirical evidence — the US credit union distribution

### III.2.1 Why this is the best evidence in the study

Every federally insured US credit union files a standardised quarterly NCUA 5300 Call Report, and the aggregate datasets are publicly downloadable. No other jurisdiction in this study produces comparable population-level, longitudinal, standardised data on a cooperative financial sector. Germany's cooperative banks are audited privately through the Prüfungsverband; Kenya's SASRA publishes rich but recent annual reports covering only the regulated tier; Nigeria produces almost nothing usable. The US data is therefore the empirical spine of this Part — with one crucial limitation stated at the outset and returned to in III.2.5: **the 5300 measures assets, members and mergers; it does not measure participation, turnout or contestation.** It can prove that the system has consolidated into a small number of very large institutions. It cannot, by itself, prove that member control has eroded inside them. The inference from the first to the second must be made carefully and labelled as inference.

### III.2.2 The current system and the consolidation trajectory

**CONFIRMED FACT (NCUA).** As of 31 December 2025 there were **4,287 federally insured credit unions** — 2,686 federally chartered and 1,601 federally insured state-chartered — serving **144.7 million members** and holding **$2.433 trillion in assets** (NCUA Quarterly Credit Union Data Summary, 2025 Q4, released 6 March 2026). Membership grew by 2.4 million over the year even as the number of institutions fell by 168.

That divergence — more members, fewer institutions — is the defining fact of the sector and has held for over half a century. The long-run series:

- **1969: 23,866 credit unions** (the historic peak), with roughly $16 billion in assets.
- **2000 (year-end): 10,316 federally insured credit unions**, $438 billion in assets, 77 million members.
- **2010: 7,339.**
- **2015: 6,021.**
- **2020: 5,099.**
- **2023: 4,604.**
- **2024: 4,455.**
- **2025: 4,287.**

The count has fallen by more than **four-fifths from its 1969 peak** while membership has risen from tens of millions to 144.7 million and assets from $16 billion to $2.4 trillion. The rate of decline is roughly 3–4% per year, driven almost entirely by mergers rather than failures: consolidation, not collapse. The NCUA approved an average of 227 mergers annually from 2012 to 2018, falling to an average of 160 per year from 2019 through 2025 — the slowdown itself a symptom of prior consolidation having shrunk the pool of small partners. Overwhelmingly the stated reason is "expanded services" (about 76% of mergers over a recent five-year window), with "poor financial condition" a distant second (about 13%). The average asset size of a merged-in credit union rose to **$263.4 million in 2025**, a sharp jump from a $23.9 million average over 2012–2018, reflecting a shift toward "mergers of equals" among substantial institutions.

### III.2.3 The size distribution: extreme right-skew

The system is not merely consolidating; it is polarising into a long tail of tiny institutions and a short head of giants. Using the NCUA's asset-tier breakdown for 30 June 2025 (the last quarter before the NCUA discontinued the tier table in its Q4 summary):

| Asset tier | # of CUs | Members (M) | Assets ($B) | ~% of assets |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| < $10M | 830 | 0.6 | 3.5 | 0.1% |
| $10M–$50M | 1,178 | 2.9 | 31.3 | ~1% |
| $50M–$100M | 581 | 3.3 | 42.3 | ~2% |
| $100M–$500M | 1,042 | 16.2 | 239.1 | ~10% |
| $500M–$1B | 284 | 12.7 | 203.4 | ~9% |
| $1B–$10B | 433 | 69.7 | 1,251.9 | ~53% |
| > $10B | 22 | 38.4 | 607.5 | ~26% |
| **Total** | **4,370** | **143.8** | **2,379.0** | **100%** |

The distribution is brutally skewed. **About 59% of all credit unions (2,589 institutions under $100 million) hold roughly 3% of system assets.** At the other pole, the **455 credit unions over $1 billion (about 10% of institutions) hold roughly $1.86 trillion — about 78% of all assets — and about 108 million of the 144 million members (roughly 75%).** The 22 credit unions over $10 billion alone held **$607.5 billion, 26% of the system**, in Q2 2025; NCUA figures reported for Q3 2025 put it at 21 institutions holding about $603 billion, "a quarter of all system assets." The ten largest credit unions collectively hold **approximately $392.6 billion in deposits and $456.4 billion in total assets** as of December 2025 (Bankrate, citing NCUA) — about 19% of all system assets concentrated in ten institutions.

The median tells the same story from below. As of 31 December 2025, half of all credit unions had less than **$64.9 million in assets**; a quarter had less than $16.0 million; only about 10% exceeded $1.1 billion. The mean credit union holds roughly $525 million in assets — but the mean is a fiction created by the giants; the median institution is a small, community-scale body.

Membership concentration is starker still. The mean is about **33,750 members per credit union** (144.7 million ÷ 4,287), but this too is distorted. **Navy Federal Credit Union alone had 15,139,001 members and $197.187 billion in total assets as of 31 December 2025 — roughly one in ten of all US credit union members in a single institution.** State Employees' Credit Union of North Carolina, the second largest, had **2,978,851 members as of the quarter ending 31 March 2026** (up from 2,875,963 a year earlier). The typical credit union, at a median of ~$65 million in assets, has a few thousand members at most.

### III.2.4 Using the distribution analytically

What does the distribution do to governance? The analytical claim of this Part is that the size distribution maps onto a governance distribution. At the tail — the median $65 million community credit union with a few thousand members — Olson's mechanism is muted: *n* is small enough, and the common bond tight enough, that per-capita stakes in control remain non-trivial and informal monitoring (people know the manager, the board, one another) is cheap. At the head — Navy Federal with 15 million members, or any of the 455 billion-dollar institutions with hundreds of thousands to millions of members — Olson's mechanism is at full strength: *n* is so large that the individual member's stake in control is vanishingly small, the AGM is a formality attended by a handful, and real authority sits with a professional executive team overseeing a self-renewing volunteer board.

The system as a whole has migrated from the first regime to the second. In 1969 there were 23,866 mostly tiny, tight-common-bond credit unions; in 2025 three-quarters of all members belong to billion-dollar institutions. **ANALYTICAL INFERENCE:** the US credit union movement has, over fifty years, moved the majority of its members from the high-legitimacy, low-*n* end of the spectrum to the low-legitimacy, high-*n* end — precisely the migration Cook's lifecycle phase 3 ("growth, glory and heterogeneity") predicts and precisely the migration the capital constraint drives. The pathologies of scale are, as the Prologue insisted, diseases of success, not of founding. The credit unions that grew to a billion dollars did so by serving members well; growth then activated the very mechanisms that threaten member control.

Is there an observable size threshold beyond which participation collapses? The statutory answer, borrowed from Germany, is that legislators located the practical breakdown of the plenary members' meeting at **1,500 members** (§43a GenG; see III.3.1) — the point at which a jurisdiction that took the problem seriously judged that the general assembly could no longer function and had to be replaced by delegates. That figure is a legislative judgement, not an econometric estimate, but it is a revealing one: it implies that the overwhelming majority of the 144 million members of US credit unions belong to institutions that are one to four orders of magnitude past the size at which German law presumes direct member democracy has ceased to be viable.

### III.2.5 What the data cannot show

Honesty requires the boundary to be drawn precisely. The NCUA 5300 data measures assets, members, loans, net worth, delinquency and merger activity. **It does not measure AGM attendance, voter turnout in board elections, the number of contested versus uncontested elections, or the degree of management autonomy.** No US regulator collects these systematically. Therefore:

- The claim that the system has consolidated into a small number of very large institutions is **CONFIRMED FACT.**
- The claim that member participation collapses at scale is **ANALYTICAL INFERENCE** — strongly supported by theory (Olson, Cook), by the structural facts (median member is now one of hundreds of thousands), and by qualitative and case evidence (III.4), but **not** directly measured by the population dataset.
- Anyone who asserts a precise participation rate for US credit unions is either citing a single institution's disclosure or extrapolating. The correct posture is that the structural preconditions for the collapse of participation are demonstrably present at scale; the collapse itself is inferred, not counted.

### III.2.6 The academic literature

The leading synthesis is McKillop, French, Quinn, Sobiech and Wilson, "Cooperative financial institutions: a review of the literature," *International Review of Financial Analysis* 71 (2020), 101520 — the standard reference, which reviews the structural, behavioural and performance research on financial cooperatives across countries. Their body of work (with Goddard and Wilson on US credit union size, age and growth; Glass, McKillop and Wilson on efficiency) establishes the consolidation and scale-economy dynamics that drive the distribution above. On governance specifically, the literature is thinner and more equivocal than the size literature — a reflection of the measurement problem in III.2.5. Van Rijn's work on "the cooperative identity at US credit unions" (*Journal of Co-operative Organization and Management*, 2022) and Van Rijn, Zeng and Hueth on executive compensation (*Annals of Public and Cooperative Economics*, 2023) probe whether large credit unions still behave like cooperatives; the recurrent finding is that scale erodes distinctiveness. This is the empirical shadow of the governance question: an institution whose behaviour becomes "virtually indistinguishable" from a commercial bank is one in which member control has ceased to bind management's choices.

## III.3 Delegate structures — the standard institutional answer

Every jurisdiction that has allowed cooperatives to grow large has confronted the collapse of the plenary members' meeting and has answered with some form of representative democracy: delegates elected by members, meeting in place of the whole membership. The question for each is not whether the structure exists but whether it preserves accountability or merely inserts a filter that insulates management behind a layer of delegates who are themselves selected by, or dependent on, the incumbent leadership.

### III.3.1 Germany's Vertreterversammlung (§43a GenG)

German law is the most explicit. Under §43a GenG, a cooperative with **more than 1,500 members** may provide in its statutes that the general assembly be replaced by a **Vertreterversammlung (delegate assembly)**. The delegate assembly exercises the full powers of the general meeting — profit appropriation, election of the supervisory board (Aufsichtsrat), major structural changes. The statute imposes hard floors and prohibitions carried forward from Part I:

- The delegate assembly must consist of **at least 50 delegates** (§43a(3)).
- Delegates must be elected by the members in a **general, direct, equal and secret ballot**.
- A delegate must be a natural person, a member, and **not** a member of the Vorstand or Aufsichtsrat (§43a(2)) — the management and supervisory organs cannot pack the delegate body with their own members.
- Under §43a, each delegate has **one vote, which cannot be transferred**; proxies are excluded and there are no plural votes for delegates.

The election machinery is set by the statutes and an electoral regulation (Wahlordnung) adopted by the Vorstand and Aufsichtsrat and approved by the general (or delegate) assembly. Crucially, an electoral committee runs the ballot, and the statute requires the list of elected delegates and their contact details to be made available for member inspection for at least two weeks (§43a(6)) — a transparency requirement designed to keep the delegate body visible to the members it represents.

**Assessment.** On paper this is the strongest delegate model in the study: a bright-line threshold, a substantial minimum size, mandatory secret ballot, exclusion of the boards from the delegate body, no proxies, no plural votes, and mandatory publication. **CONTESTED / UNKNOWN:** the evidence on whether German delegate elections are actually *contested* — whether more candidates stand than seats, whether turnout is meaningful — is not systematically published, and the promotional literature of the cooperative federations (source rank 5, to be treated with caution) is silent on contestation rates. The German answer to Olson is not that it makes members participate; it is that it (a) shrinks the decision-making body to a size at which participation is rational again (50–200 people can meaningfully deliberate) and (b) backstops the whole structure with the compulsory Prüfungsverband audit (III.7). The delegate assembly by itself would be as capturable as any other; it is the audit federation that does the real work.

### III.3.2 The Netherlands: ledenraad and Rabobank's General Members' Council

The Dutch answer is the members' council (ledenraad), and its most consequential deployment is the 2016 Rabobank restructuring carried forward from Part I. On 2 December 2015 the local members' councils agreed unanimously to the reform; the General Meeting approved on 9 December 2015; and with effect from 1 January 2016 **all 106 local Rabobanks surrendered their independent banking licences and merged into a single Coöperatieve Rabobank U.A.** — one cooperative, one banking licence, one set of consolidated accounts.

The governance that replaced 106 local boards is a tiered representative structure. Members in each local area elect a **Local Members' Council of 30 to 50 members**, which appoints a Local Supervisory Body (3–5 members) with a defined role over customer service and local social performance, and appoints one of its number to the national **General Members' Council (GMC)**. The GMC — currently around 78 members, one from each local supervisory body — is described by Rabobank as its "highest decision-making member body," with rights of consent or advice over the strategic framework and identity, material investments, the budget's main lines, and the annual report.

**Assessment. CONTESTED IN THE LITERATURE.** The 2016 reform can be read two ways, and the founder should understand both. The charitable reading — advanced by Rabobank and by the academic account of "the road towards one cooperative Rabobank" — is that the reform *strengthened* countervailing member power: by abolishing the confusing hybrid of 106 separately licensed banks and replacing it with a clean tiered council, it clarified where member voice sat and made it more effective, "simpler and less hybrid." The sceptical reading is that the reform completed the centralisation of a system in which the local banks had already lost operational autonomy, formalising the transfer of real control to the central executive while preserving an elaborate representative superstructure — degeneration in Cornforth's sense (substantive erosion of member control while the form is retained), dressed as democratic renewal. **ANALYTICAL INFERENCE:** the truth is that the reform did both — it rationalised a genuinely dysfunctional structure *and* moved the locus of control decisively upward. Whether the GMC substantively constrains the Managing Board, or merely advises and ratifies, is not resolvable from public sources; the balance of powers (consent on some matters, advice on others) suggests real but bounded influence. The honest verdict is that Rabobank's council preserved *more* member voice than a demutualisation would have, and *less* than the pre-2016 federated structure notionally provided.

### III.3.3 Quebec: the Desjardins federation

The Desjardins model is a federation of local caisses feeding a central Fédération des caisses Desjardins du Québec. The 2001 restructuring merged the regional caisse federations, the Fédération des caisses d'économie and the Confédération into a single Fédération (secondary sources vary between eleven and fourteen predecessors — flagged as contested in Part I). Loi 141 (2018) folded the Desjardins-specific rules into the general C-67.3 regime as the "Groupe coopératif Desjardins" and gave the Autorité des marchés financiers (AMF) resolution powers.

Member governance runs upward: caisse members elect the caisse's board at caisse general meetings; caisses are represented in regional and central bodies that feed the Fédération. **Assessment.** The 2001 consolidation, like Rabobank's 2016 reform, traded local autonomy for scale and coherence. The distinctive Quebec feature relevant to the founder is the pairing of the federated democratic structure with a strong external prudential supervisor (the AMF) armed since 2018 with resolution powers — the same pattern seen in Germany (Prüfungsverband) and Kenya (SASRA): representative democracy at the top, professional supervision behind it. The member-voice half of the structure, standing alone, faces the same degeneration risk as every federation; it is the supervisory half that constrains management.

### III.3.4 The United States: annual meetings, nominating committees and the petition route

US federal credit unions run the thinnest version of representative democracy in the study — and, revealingly, most do not use delegates at all; they retain a plenary annual meeting that almost no one attends. Governance carried forward from Part I: one member, one vote is mandatory and proportional voting is prohibited; boards must have an odd number of directors, at least five and no more than fifteen. The nomination and election machinery is set by the standard Federal Credit Union Bylaws (12 CFR Part 701, Appendix A). At least 120 days before the annual meeting the chair appoints a **nominating committee** of three or more members, which nominates at least one member per vacancy. Additional candidates may reach the ballot only by **petition**, signed — depending on the bylaw option and the credit union's size — by 1% of the members subject to a floor and a cap (commonly a minimum of 20 and a maximum of 500 signatures). Decisively: **"there are no nominations from the floor if there are sufficient nominations by the nominating committee or by petition to provide at least one nominee for each open position"**, and where the number of nominees equals the number of vacancies, **no ballot is held at all** and the nominees are declared elected.

**Assessment — the sharpest negative verdict in this section.** The US model, as operated, is the paradigm of democracy performed while management is insulated. The combination of (a) a nominating committee appointed by the incumbent chair, (b) a petition route requiring hundreds of signatures that a challenger must gather without access to the membership list, and (c) the automatic cancellation of the election whenever nominees equal vacancies, produces the widely observed reality of **uncontested elections and self-perpetuating boards.** Practitioner commentary is blunt: closed elections mean "members have no choice to select their representatives," boards risk becoming "stocked with family members, or a click of golfing buddies," "picked by or influenced by [the] CEO, and tend to be rubber stampers." This is not a marginal defect of a few credit unions; it is the systemic equilibrium the bylaws produce, and it is exactly what Olson predicts when the machinery of contestation is costly and the incumbents control it.

### III.3.5 Kenya: delegate AGMs at large SACCOs

Kenya's large SACCOs, like their German and Dutch counterparts, replace the plenary meeting with delegate AGMs once membership makes a general meeting impractical. Kenya retains one member, one vote regardless of shareholding (Part I), and SASRA supervises deposit-taking SACCOs. **Assessment.** The delegate structure is real, but the Kenyan evidence (III.5.7, III.7.3) shows that delegate AGMs have not prevented large-scale capture: the KUSCCO scandal unfolded through years of AGMs at which delegates ratified financial statements that were, in fact, fabricated. The delegate layer without a functioning audit and supervisory backstop is precisely the "layer of delegates who are themselves selected by the incumbent leadership" that insulates rather than imposes accountability.

### III.3.6 Cross-jurisdictional verdict

Delegate structures do not, by themselves, solve the governance problem; they relocate it. They shrink the deliberating body to a size at which deliberation is again rational (Germany's 50-plus, Rabobank's 78, a SACCO's delegate assembly), which is a genuine gain over a 100,000-member plenary meeting that cannot function. But the delegate body is only as accountable as its election is contestable and only as effective as the external audit behind it is rigorous. **The mechanism that distinguishes the German model (degeneration-resistant) from the US model (degeneration-prone) is not the delegate assembly — both have representative structures — but the compulsory purpose-examining audit federation that stands behind the German one and is absent from the American one.** This is the thread that III.7 pulls.

## III.4 The election problem and board self-perpetuation

Section III.3 described the machinery; this section isolates the mechanism by which cooperative boards become self-perpetuating, because Appendix C will need it as a discrete failure pathway.

The mechanism has five components, each of which independently favours incumbents and which compound:

1. **Control of the nominating committee.** In the US model the chair appoints the nominating committee; in most jurisdictions the incumbent board or a committee it controls proposes the slate. The body that decides who appears on the ballot is chosen by the people the ballot is supposed to hold accountable. NCUA's own legal opinions concede the tension: a board may adopt a policy that willing incumbents be included in the slate, but may not forbid the nominating committee from nominating others — a rule that protects the *committee's* discretion, not the *challenger's* access.

2. **The difficulty of the petition route.** A challenger who is not blessed by the nominating committee must gather signatures — up to 500, or 1% of members — typically without the membership list, without the institution's communication channels, and against a deadline. The petition route exists; it is, by design, arduous.

3. **The absence of an opposition slate.** There is no organised opposition in a cooperative — no analogue of a rival party or an activist shareholder with a proxy contest — because no one has a concentrated financial stake large enough to fund the contest. The takeover market that would, in an investor-owned firm, assemble a challenging slate does not exist (Cook's control problem again).

4. **Information asymmetry.** Incumbents know the accounts, the strategy, the regulator's concerns and the membership; challengers know what is published. The incumbent can campaign on inside knowledge; the challenger cannot credibly critique what he cannot see.

5. **No share price, no takeover market.** The external disciplines that substitute for member vigilance in an investor-owned firm are absent by construction. A badly governed listed company attracts a raider; a badly governed cooperative attracts nothing but slow decline or, eventually, a "rescue" merger — which, tellingly, is coded in NCUA data as "expanded services," not as failure.

**Evidence, honestly labelled.** Direct, systematic data on contested-versus-uncontested elections in credit unions, cooperative banks and SACCOs is **largely absent — UNKNOWN as a population statistic.** What exists is: the structural fact that the bylaws cancel the ballot when nominees equal vacancies (III.3.4); abundant individual credit union notices showing incumbent-only slates and "there will be no nominations from the floor"; and practitioner testimony that closed, uncontested elections are the norm. This is enough to support the **SCHOLARLY-CONSENSUS-adjacent** claim that board self-perpetuation is the prevailing pattern in large financial cooperatives, but not enough to attach a precise contestation rate. The founder should note the asymmetry: the mechanism is well understood and the direction unambiguous; the magnitude is unmeasured.

## III.5 Capture — a taxonomy

Appendix C (the Failure Catalogue) requires a systematic classification of capture. Capture is the active-minority-control phenomenon of III.1.2, and it comes in seven forms distinguished by *who* captures and *how*. Each is a distinct entry for the Catalogue.

### III.5.1 Managerial capture

Executives accumulate de facto control over a passive board. The mechanism is information and continuity: professional managers are permanent, informed and expert; volunteer directors are transient, part-time and dependent on management for their picture of the institution. Over time the board ratifies rather than directs. This is Cornforth's "organisational degeneration" — control migrating to a managerial elite while the cooperative form is retained. It is the modal outcome at scale in the US and, arguably, the substance of the Rabobank centralisation debate (III.3.2).

### III.5.2 Board / insider capture

A self-perpetuating lay elite entrenches itself and extracts private benefit — director fees and perquisites, loans on favourable terms, status, travel, control of hiring. This is the mechanism of III.4 turned to private advantage. It differs from managerial capture in that the captors are the *directors*, not the executives; the two can coexist or contend. Insider lending is the classic tell: in Kenya, SASRA's 2024 supervision report flagged **22 SACCOs for insider-lending breaches exceeding 20% of core capital**, a direct, measurable instance of board/insider capture siphoning resources from ordinary members.

### III.5.3 Factional capture: net savers versus net borrowers (the worked example)

This is Cook's **influence-costs problem** in its purest form and deserves the fullest treatment, because it is structural, not merely a governance hygiene failure. A credit union (or SACCO, or thrift-and-credit society) intermediates between two groups of its own members whose interests are directly opposed on the single most important decision the institution makes — the pricing of savings against loans.

Consider a simple worked example. Suppose a credit union has members who are **net savers** (their deposits exceed their borrowings) and members who are **net borrowers** (the reverse). The institution earns a spread: it charges borrowers a loan rate *r_L* and pays savers a deposit/dividend rate *r_D*, with *r_L* > *r_D* covering costs and building reserves. Now hold the cost of running the institution and its target reserves fixed. Every basis point by which the board lowers *r_L* transfers value from savers (and reserves) to borrowers; every basis point by which it raises *r_D* transfers value from borrowers (and reserves) to savers. **The two member groups have exactly opposed preferences over the institution's core price.** Borrowers want low loan rates, low fees and lax credit discipline; savers want high deposit rates and strong prudential discipline. A board dominated by borrowers will run loan rates down and credit standards loose — pleasant until loan losses erode the reserves that protect savers' deposits. A board dominated by savers will run deposit rates up and lending tight — safe but starving the borrowing members the institution exists to serve.

The governance literature (Branch and Baker; the WOCCU governance principles) identifies this as the defining, inherent conflict of the credit union form and notes the empirically observed **tendency of borrowers to dominate boards** — plausibly because the private benefit of a favourable loan is concentrated and salient, while the saver's interest in prudential discipline is diffuse and abstract (Olson again, operating *within* the membership). The recommended countermeasures — deliberately recruiting net savers to the board, clear institutional rules separating the roles of management, directors and members — are precisely attempts to prevent one faction from capturing the price. **ANALYTICAL INFERENCE:** because the conflict is structural and permanent, it cannot be "solved," only balanced; the quality of a credit union's governance is in large part the quality of its management of this standing conflict, and an institution that lets either faction capture the price is degenerating whether or not its form survives.

### III.5.4 Staff / employee capture

Where employees are also members (common in worker-influenced cooperatives and in some SACCOs whose common bond is an employer), staff interests — job security, wages, staffing levels — can capture decisions against the interests of the broader membership. In financial cooperatives this is usually secondary to managerial capture but can reinforce it, as staff-members side with the executive against outside members.

### III.5.5 Regional / constituency capture

In federated structures (Desjardins, Rabobank pre-2016, the German network, large multi-branch SACCOs), a region, branch or constituency can capture disproportionate influence — over the allocation of the network's shared resources, over board seats, over cross-subsidy between strong and weak units. The 2001 and 2016 consolidations in Quebec and the Netherlands were, in part, responses to the coordination and constituency problems of loosely federated systems.

### III.5.6 External capture

Creditors, a regulator, or an investor acquiring effective control. The paradigm is the **UK Co-operative Bank** (III.6): when the bank's capital shortfall became unsustainable in 2013, the Co-operative Group lost control to the bank's bondholders — US hedge funds — reducing the Group's stake first to about 30% and then to 20%. External capture is the terminal failure mode in which the members' cooperative is captured from outside by the providers of the capital the members could not supply themselves — a direct governance consequence of the capital constraint of Part II, and the risk that hangs over any two-tier structure (III.8).

### III.5.7 Political capture — the dominant risk in Kenya and Nigeria

This is under-discussed in the Anglo-American literature and must be covered properly, because it is the dominant governance risk in both African jurisdictions in this study.

The historical pattern is well documented. Across Anglophone Africa, and Kenya in particular, cooperatives originated not from members' common interest but from **government policy and colonial then post-colonial directive**; they "were conditioned to emerge as dependent agents and/or clients of the state," serving "the interests of the state [more] than the ordinary members." The scholarship (Wanyama and others) identifies "the politics of state patronage" as a direct contributor to "corruption; mismanagement; inefficiency; and embezzlement of funds." Under the single-party authoritarian era from the 1960s to the early 1990s, cooperative development was captured into state politics, "to the extent that the failures of state policies found expression in the cooperative movement." Liberalisation from the mid-1990s withdrew the state's supportive role — audit, supervision, management training were "the first to be withdrawn" — but critically **without a contingency plan to replace those support services**, on the false assumption that the market would provide them. The result was a cooperative sector simultaneously freed from and abandoned by the state, with neither democratic member control nor competent external supervision to fill the gap. Kenya's KUSCCO scandal (III.7.3) is the contemporary expression of exactly this vacuum.

**For Nigeria**, the pattern is congruent and is treated in full in III.9: cooperatives used as channels for state and party patronage, with governance systems the Federal Government's own 2026 reform materials describe as marked by "weak governance systems, outdated operations, poor transparency, and financial irregularities." Political capture is the master category in both jurisdictions because it combines the mechanisms of the others — insiders installed by patrons, funds diverted to political ends, audit and supervision defeated by political protection — and because the ordinary member has even less ability to resist a captor backed by state power than one backed merely by information and incumbency.

## III.6 Competence versus representativeness — the Co-operative Bank case study

The UK Co-operative Bank is the sharpest available illustration of the trade-off between a democratically representative board and a technically competent one, and it is the one case in this study where the United Kingdom is admitted (per the binding conventions). It is analysed here in detail because it feeds Appendix C directly as the paradigm of external capture (III.5.6) precipitated by governance failure.

### III.6.1 The facts

The Co-operative Bank was owned, through the Co-operative Banking Group, by the Co-operative Group — an organisation of some eight million members — and inherited an elected-member board structure from that Group. In 2009 it merged with the **Britannia Building Society**; in 2011–2013 it pursued **Project Verde**, a bid to acquire 632 branches from Lloyds Banking Group. In 2013 a **£1.5 billion capital shortfall** was revealed; the bank was unable to complete the Verde acquisition; and the Co-operative Group lost control, ceding a majority stake to the bank's bondholders (US hedge funds), reducing its stake first to about 30% and then to 20%. The bank's chairman from 2010 until June 2013 was the **Reverend Paul Flowers**, a Methodist minister and former Labour councillor, who was subsequently arrested on drugs charges and, the Treasury Committee found, "lacked any of the requisite financial services experience to act as chairman of a bank."

### III.6.2 What the reviews concluded

Three authorities examined the collapse:

- **The Kelly Review** (Sir Christopher Kelly, "Failings in management and governance," published 30 April 2014) conducted 130 interviews — the only executive who refused to participate was Flowers — and concluded that the disaster flowed from poor commercial lending (much of it inherited from Britannia), a failed IT project, and PPI mis-selling, the consequences of which were **"magnified" by a weak governance structure** that made some form of failure "almost inevitable." Kelly found it a "serious handicap" that successive chairs (Bob Burlton and Paul Flowers) had no banking experience, and identified "serious failings" in how the Group's board scrutinised the bank's board. Crucially, Kelly stated that his findings **"should not be interpreted as a criticism of the co-operative model"** but of "the particular model of governance adopted by the Group," which "manifestly failed."

- **Lord Myners' Review** (Report of the Independent Governance Review, published 7 May 2014) found the Group suffered a "democratic deficit": the three-tier system of elected member representation had "consistently produced governors without the necessary qualifications and experience to provide effective board leadership," which "massively raised the cost of decision-making and diminished genuine accountability throughout its governance hierarchy." Myners noted that, at the time, ordinary members did not even have the right to attend AGMs or to elect or re-elect Group board directors — democracy performed at the base while real selection happened elsewhere.

- **The Treasury Select Committee** (Fourth Report, "Project Verde," HC 728, published 21 October 2014, chaired by Andrew Tyrie) concluded that the Verde deal was "doomed to failure," that the capital shortfall "could have been discovered sooner," and that **"the structure and composition of Co-op Bank's board was an accident waiting to happen and badly let down Co-op members and customers."** It found Flowers "not a reliable witness" and excluded his evidence.

### III.6.3 The lesson, carefully generalised

Is the lesson that democratic boards cannot govern banks, or that this particular democratic structure was badly designed? The answer, on the evidence, is the second — but with a real residue of the first.

The case *against* the strong reading (that member democracy is incompatible with banking) is decisive and comes from this very study's other jurisdictions. **German cooperative banks (the Volksbanken and Raiffeisenbanken), Dutch Rabobank, and Quebec's Desjardins all have democratic member governance and none failed in the manner of the Co-operative Bank.** If democratic ownership *per se* caused the failure, these institutions — larger, older, equally member-governed — should have failed too. They did not. The difference is structural: the German banks are backstopped by the compulsory Prüfungsverband and a two-tier Vorstand/Aufsichtsrat that separates management from supervision and imposes professional competence requirements; Rabobank and Desjardins pair member councils with professional executive and supervisory boards and strong external regulators. The Co-operative Group, by contrast, allowed a lay elected member with no banking experience to *chair the bank itself* — not to sit on a supervisory tier over professional management, but to hold the pivotal governance office of a bank — and layered over it a "democratic deficit" in which the members whose legitimacy justified the structure had almost no actual power.

The residue of the strong reading is this: the case demonstrates that **representativeness and competence genuinely trade off at the pivotal offices of a complex financial institution, and a governance structure that does not consciously manage that trade-off will resolve it badly.** The German answer — reserve the management board for professionals (with fit-and-proper requirements), place elected/lay representatives on the supervisory tier, and cap and vet everyone through the audit federation — is a *design* that reconciles the two. The Co-operative Group's structure was not such a design; it was an inherited democratic form applied, without adaptation, to an activity (running a bank) that punishes amateurism severely. **ANALYTICAL INFERENCE / verdict:** democratic boards *can* govern banks — the continental evidence proves it — but only within a structure that separates member legitimacy from executive competence, staffs the pivotal technical offices with qualified professionals, and backstops the whole with rigorous external audit and supervision. The Co-operative Bank failed not because it was a cooperative but because it wore the cooperative's democratic form without building the cooperative bank's competence-preserving scaffolding. For the Nigerian founder, the lesson is exact and sobering: the two-tier structure Part II forces (III.8) is dangerous precisely where it is weakest on this scaffolding.

## III.7 Audit and supervision as governance

### III.7.1 The general proposition

The argument of this Part now converges on a single proposition. If Olson's mechanism guarantees that members will not, at scale, monitor management (III.1), and if delegate structures and elections relocate rather than solve the problem (III.3–III.4), then **member monitoring must be substituted for by someone else's monitoring — and the quality of that substitute monitor is the best single predictor of whether a cooperative degenerates.** External audit and supervision are therefore not compliance overhead; they are the functional replacement for the member vigilance that the ownership structure cannot summon. This reframes the entire debate: the question is not "how do we make members participate?" (they rationally will not) but "who monitors on the members' behalf, and how good are they?"

### III.7.2 Germany's Prüfungsverband — the strongest example

Germany's answer is the compulsory audit federation. Under §§53–54 GenG every registered cooperative must belong to a **Prüfungsverband (auditing association)**, and the federation's audit certificate is a **precondition of registration** (§11(2) no. 3, carried forward from Part I). The audit is not the ordinary financial audit of a company. It examines not merely the accounts but the **member-promotion purpose (Förderzweck)** — whether the cooperative is actually serving its members — and it is conducted by a federation that is itself cooperative, permanent, expert and independent of the audited cooperative's management. Membership is mandatory; there is no opting out and no shopping for a compliant auditor.

The Prologue recorded a puzzle: **Germany has no statutory asset lock** (§§91–92 contain no lock), yet German cooperative banks are among the most demutualisation-resistant in the world. The resolution, argued here, is that the Prüfungsverband *is* the asset lock's functional substitute — and more. Where an asset lock passively prevents members from cashing out the collective reserves, the audit federation actively and continuously verifies that management is serving the member purpose, catches financial and governance problems early, and — because it audits every cooperative in the network — accumulates comparative expertise no single institution's members could. **ANALYTICAL INFERENCE (a central claim of this Part):** the compulsory, purpose-examining, federated audit is the real reason German cooperative banks resist degeneration despite having no asset lock. It is the substitute monitor par excellence, and it is the single most transplantable idea in this Part — though, as III.9 and III.10 show, it is also the hardest to transplant because it depends on a dense federation Nigeria does not possess.

### III.7.3 SASRA, NCUA, AMF — and the Kenyan stress test

The other jurisdictions supply weaker or narrower versions of the substitute monitor, and the contrast is instructive:

- **NCUA (US):** a genuine prudential regulator with the 5300 Call Report, examination powers and deposit insurance. It monitors safety and soundness rigorously — but it does *not* examine the member-promotion purpose and does *not* police the governance/participation deficit (III.3.4, III.4). It is a competent financial monitor and an absent democratic one. This is consistent with the US pattern: financially sound credit unions that have quietly become "virtually indistinguishable" from banks.

- **AMF (Quebec):** prudential supervisor of the Desjardins group, armed since Loi 141 (2018) with resolution powers — a strong external monitor paired with the federation's internal structure.

- **SASRA (Kenya):** a dedicated SACCO prudential authority created under the Sacco Societies Act 2008, publishing detailed annual supervision reports. The Sacco Supervision Annual Report 2024 — released on 25 September 2025 by Cabinet Secretary for Cooperatives and MSMEs Wycliffe Oparanya under Section 22 of the Sacco Societies Act — covered **357 regulated SACCOs (177 deposit-taking and 178 non-withdrawable deposit-taking) with KSh 1.08 trillion in combined assets.** SASRA is a real and improving monitor — but the **KUSCCO scandal is the stress test it partly failed.** The Kenya Union of Savings and Credit Co-operatives, the sector's apex body, was found by a PwC forensic audit (submitted February 2025) to have lost about **KSh 13.3 billion** to fraud, leaving it insolvent by about **KSh 12.5 billion** (assets ~KSh 5.2 billion against liabilities ~KSh 17.7 billion), with the **KSh 24.8 billion it received from 247 SACCOs as deposits now at risk.** Financial statements were overstated by about KSh 9.3 billion, and the fraud ran for years, concealed by falsified statements bearing the **forged signature of an external auditor who had died before signing** — the ultimate demonstration that a captured or fraudulent audit is worse than none, because it manufactures false assurance. Critically, KUSCCO "operated without a regulatory watchdog": as an apex union it fell into a gap in SASRA's remit. **The lesson is exact:** the substitute monitor works only to the extent of its coverage and its independence; where coverage has gaps (apex bodies, the thousands of smaller Kenyan SACCOs outside SASRA's full prudential purview) or the audit itself is captured, the members are wholly exposed.

### III.7.4 Nigeria — the near-absence of the substitute monitor

Nigeria has almost none of this machinery, and III.9 develops the diagnosis. Audit is weak and self-chosen (§36 NCSA 1993 lets the society appoint its own auditor); there is no Prüfungsverband, no SASRA-equivalent prudential authority, no NCUA-style call report. The ICA's own legal-framework analysis and Nigerian academic case studies document recurrent fraud in thrift-and-credit societies attributed to poor book-keeping and unskilled auditors. In the terms of this section, Nigeria has neither the member monitor (which Olson says will not appear) nor the substitute monitor (which the state has never built) — a cooperative sector monitored, in effect, by no one.

### III.7.5 Testing the proposition

The proposition — that the quality of the substitute monitor best predicts degeneration — can be tested against the six-jurisdiction spread. Germany: strongest substitute monitor (compulsory purpose-examining audit), strongest degeneration resistance. Quebec and the Netherlands: strong prudential supervision plus federated structure, robust but with real centralisation/degeneration debate. US: strong financial supervision, absent democratic supervision — financially sound, democratically degenerated (participation collapsed, boards self-perpetuating). Kenya: improving but gap-ridden supervision — episodic catastrophic capture (KUSCCO). Nigeria: near-absent supervision — endemic fraud and governance failure. **The rank-ordering of substitute-monitor quality matches the rank-ordering of governance outcomes closely enough to treat the proposition as strongly supported (SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS on the direction; ANALYTICAL INFERENCE on it being the *single best* predictor).** The one important refinement, from the US, is that "monitor quality" must be disaggregated: a monitor can be excellent on solvency and useless on member control. Germany's audit is distinctive precisely because it monitors *both*. This is the finding the Nigerian design brief must act on.

## III.8 Governance in the two-tier structure — the question Part II created

### III.8.1 The question restated

Part II reached a hard conclusion: a Nigerian cooperative fintech at licensed, remittance-capable scale cannot be housed in a single cooperative. The §27 NCSA 20% shareholding cap forbids a concentrated cooperative equity raise; the growth arithmetic forbids organic bridging to CBN capital walls; CBN licensing presumes a company. The forced architecture is therefore **two-tier: a member-owned cooperative that sponsors, and holds shares in, a separately CBN-licensed, investor-capitalised operating company.** Part III must now confront the governance consequence: **where does member control actually sit when the operating entity is a company with investor shareholders and the cooperative is merely one shareholder among others?**

### III.8.2 Where governance actually sits

In the two-tier structure, legal control of the operating company sits, by default, with whoever controls the company's shares and board under company law (CAMA 2020) and the CBN's fit-and-proper and prudential regime — **not** with the cooperative's members exercising one-member-one-vote. The cooperative's members govern the cooperative; the cooperative, as a shareholder, governs the operating company only to the extent of its shareholding and whatever contractual rights it has secured. If investors supply most of the operating company's capital (which is the entire point of the two-tier structure — Part II showed the cooperative cannot supply it), then absent deliberate engineering the investors control the company, and the members' cooperative is reduced to a minority shareholder whose "cooperative" character is invisible at the operating level. This is external capture (III.5.6) built into the architecture from birth — the Co-operative Bank's terminal condition as a *starting* condition.

### III.8.3 The mechanisms that can preserve member primacy

Member control in the operating company can only be preserved by contract and share-structure engineering, and Part II's real-world analogues show the menu:

1. **A controlling majority stake.** Crédit Agricole's listed vehicle preserves cooperative control only because **SAS Rue La Boétie holds ≥50%** of the listed entity. A majority stake is the cleanest protection: whoever holds >50% of the votes controls the company. But it is expensive — it requires the cooperative side to hold half the equity, which collides directly with Part II's finding that the cooperative cannot supply the capital. There is a genuine tension here the founder cannot wish away.

2. **A golden share / dual-class structure.** A special share (or a class of super-voting shares held by the cooperative) can lock specified control rights — appointment of a majority or a blocking minority of directors, veto over changes to purpose — regardless of economic ownership. This delivers control without requiring 50% of the economics, but it depends on CAMA and CBN permitting the structure and on investors accepting it (they will demand a discount or refuse).

3. **Reserved matters and a shareholders' agreement.** A shareholders' agreement can reserve defined decisions — change of the company's purpose or mission, disposal of the business, related-party dealings, dividend policy — to the cooperative's consent, even where the cooperative is a minority. This is the most flexible and most negotiable instrument and the most likely to be achievable in practice.

4. **Board appointment rights.** The cooperative can secure the right to appoint the chair, or a fixed number of directors, or a director with a veto over mission-critical matters — mirroring the German cap that investor members may hold **no more than one quarter of the supervisory board (§8(2) GenG)**, read here in reverse to guarantee the *members* a controlling or blocking board presence.

5. **The Rabobank-certificate lesson, inverted.** Part II established that Rabobank Certificates deliver listed perpetual CET1 with **near-zero governance transfer** — capital that carries almost no control. The two-tier founder should seek the mirror image: raise investor capital in an instrument that carries *economic* return but *minimal governance*, so that governance stays with the cooperative even as economics are shared. This is the single most important design principle for the two-tier structure — separate the cash-flow rights the investors need from the control rights the members must keep.

### III.8.4 The degeneration risk specific to the two-tier form

Even with these protections, the two-tier structure carries a degeneration risk sharper than any single-tier cooperative, because **three forces pull the operating company away from the cooperative's member-promotion purpose simultaneously:**

- **The operating company's own commercial logic.** A company is legally oriented to its own commercial success; its directors owe duties to the *company*, and once it has outside investors, to all its shareholders — not to the sponsoring cooperative's members as such.
- **Investors' return expectations.** Investors supplied capital to earn a return; they will press for pricing, cost and growth decisions that maximise their return, which need not coincide with (and often opposes) the members' interest in cheap, inclusive service — the net-saver/net-borrower conflict of III.5.3 re-appearing as a member-versus-investor conflict.
- **The regulator's prudential expectations.** The CBN will supervise the operating company as a licensed financial institution, demanding capital, risk controls and governance appropriate to a bank/PSP — expectations that push toward professional, commercial, arms-length management and away from member-directed informality.

All three vectors point the same way: toward an operating company that behaves like a commercial firm and treats the cooperative as a legacy shareholder to be managed. This is degeneration (Cornforth's process — form retained, substance eroded) engineered into the structure. The Co-operative Bank (III.6) shows the endpoint; the Crédit Agricole ≥50% lock shows the (expensive) defence.

### III.8.5 Verdict: can member control survive the two-tier structure, and what does it take?

**Position (ANALYTICAL INFERENCE, argued).** Member control *can* survive the two-tier structure, but only conditionally, and the conditions are demanding:

1. **Control rights must be secured by instrument, not assumed.** The cooperative must hold either a majority stake, a golden/dual-class share, or a shareholders' agreement with robust reserved matters and board-appointment rights. Bare minority shareholding with no special rights = member control does not survive.
2. **The capital instrument sold to investors must be governance-light** (the inverted Rabobank-certificate principle): economics to investors, control to members.
3. **A purpose-lock must be entrenched** — a contractual and constitutional statement of the member-promotion purpose that the cooperative can enforce against the operating company, functioning as the Förderzweck the German audit polices.
4. **An independent monitor must watch the interface** — because the cooperative's own members will not monitor (Olson), a substitute monitor (III.7) must verify that the operating company is honouring the purpose-lock and the reserved matters. In Nigeria, where no Prüfungsverband exists, this monitor must be built by bye-law and contract (III.9.6, III.10).

Absent these, the two-tier structure is a demutualisation deferred — the members retain a cooperative shell whose operating substance has passed to investors and managers. **The founder should treat the two-tier structure as survivable but adversarial: every control right the members keep must be fought for at the point of capital-raising and written down, because none of it is supplied by default and all of it is under continuous commercial and prudential pressure thereafter.**

## III.9 Governance in Nigeria — the specific diagnosis

### III.9.1 The statutory baseline

Nigerian cooperative governance rests on the Nigerian Co-operative Societies Act 1993 (NCSA), and the statute supplies strikingly little. **§25 gives one vote only per member, with the chairman holding a casting vote on equality** (a registered society that is itself a member may exercise weighted voting per its bye-laws). Democratic form is thus guaranteed at its thinnest: equality of votes, but no delegate machinery, no protection of contestation, no separation of supervisory from management organs. Governance is exercised through the **management-committee model** — an elected committee that both manages and is supposed to supervise, without the German Vorstand/Aufsichtsrat separation that keeps management and oversight in different hands.

### III.9.2 The audit vacuum

The decisive weakness is audit. **§36 permits self-chosen auditors** — the society appoints its own auditor, with none of the German compulsion, independence or purpose-examination. There is **no Prüfungsverband, no SASRA-equivalent prudential regulator, no call-report dataset.** §37 provides for inquiry and inspection by the Director (a reactive, discretionary power, not continuous supervision). In the framework of III.7, Nigeria has neither the member monitor (Olson guarantees its absence) nor the substitute monitor (the state never built one) — the worst position of any jurisdiction in this study.

### III.9.3 The §46 surcharge power — and whether it is used

**§46 empowers the Director to surcharge managers who misapply, retain or cause loss to the property of a society** — a genuine personal-liability weapon, at least on paper. The comparison with Kenya is instructive: Kenya has an analogous surcharge power and it is visibly, if imperfectly, used — the SASRA-driven prosecutions of Metropolitan National SACCO officials over an alleged KSh 14.5 billion fraud proceeded expressly on the basis that "members of the SACCO had already been notified of potential surcharge proceedings." **In Nigeria, evidence that §46 is actively and systematically used is essentially absent — UNKNOWN, tending to NOT USED.** A surcharge power that is not exercised is not a deterrent; it is a dead letter. The Nigerian problem is not the absence of the tool but the absence of an institution with the mandate, capacity and independence to wield it — the same institutional vacuum that leaves audit self-chosen and supervision reactive.

### III.9.4 Documented fraud and governance failure

The empirical record, though thin and dominated by lower-tier academic case studies (which must be treated with caution as evidence), is consistent and adverse. Nigerian academic case studies of cooperative thrift-and-credit societies — for example, studies of societies in Imo State, Ekiti State, Osun State and Enugu State — document recurrent fraud, loan default, poor book-keeping and unskilled auditing as endemic features. Historical NDIC reporting cited in this literature recorded cooperative thrift-and-credit society staff as heavily implicated in fraud cases. The Federal Government's own 2026 reform materials — the clearest official admission — describe a sector suffering from "weak governance systems, outdated operations, poor transparency, and financial irregularities," and the **Renewed Hope Cooperative Reform and Revamp Programme (RH-CRRP, 2025–2030)** is being launched precisely to address governance, "ghost societies," fraud risk and the absence of financial oversight, with proposals including a **Cooperative Verification Number** and a **Cooperative Members Identification Number (CoopID)** integrated with the National Identity Number architecture, a review of the NCSA to create "a modern regulatory framework," and financial oversight "in collaboration with the Central Bank of Nigeria." **CONFIRMED (as official characterisation):** the Nigerian state itself now describes its cooperative sector as governance-failed.

### III.9.5 Political capture in Nigeria

The political-capture history developed generally in III.5.7 applies squarely to Nigeria. Nigerian cooperatives have a long history as instruments of state and party patronage; the sector's governance weakness is not merely technical (bad book-keeping) but political (cooperatives as patronage vehicles). This matters for the founder in a specific way: a Nigerian cooperative that grows large and handles remittance flows will be an attractive target for political capture, and the ordinary member's ability to resist a politically backed captor is even weaker than his ability to resist a merely entrenched board. The defences against political capture are the same substitute-monitor defences as against every other capture — independent audit, external supervision, entrenched purpose — which is exactly what the statute fails to supply.

### III.9.6 The practical reality and the bye-law remedy

The practical reality is stark: **a Nigerian cooperative's members are unlikely to exercise meaningful monitoring** — Olson's mechanism operates in Nigeria as everywhere, and it is aggravated by lower financial literacy, weaker disclosure and the absence of any external monitor to give members something to hold management to. The statute supplies almost none of the machinery that other jurisdictions rely on.

But the statute's very thinness is also an opening: **because the NCSA supplies so little, the bye-laws can supply a great deal.** What the German legislator mandated, the Nigerian founder can, in principle, adopt voluntarily by bye-law and contract:

- A **delegate assembly** above a defined membership threshold (borrowing the logic of §43a GenG's 1,500-member trigger) — adoptable by bye-law even though the NCSA does not require it.
- A **separated supervisory committee** distinct from the management committee (borrowing the Vorstand/Aufsichtsrat separation), with its own election and defined oversight powers.
- A **contractual external audit** stronger than §36 requires — appointment of a reputable independent auditor, rotation, and (the hardest and most important) a **voluntary purpose-examining audit** approximating the Prüfungsverband, perhaps procured collectively through a federation of like-minded societies to create the network effect Germany relies on.
- **Nominating and election rules** designed for genuine contestation (III.10) — open nomination, member access to the register for candidacy, and a prohibition on cancelling elections merely because nominees equal vacancies.
- Bye-law **entrenchment of the §46 surcharge logic** as a contractual liability of committee members, so that the deterrent exists by contract even where the Director does not enforce the statute.

This is the pivot to the design brief: in Nigeria, governance protection is overwhelmingly a matter of **private ordering by bye-law and contract**, because the public machinery is absent. That is a heavier burden on the founder than in any other jurisdiction — and the central practical message of this Part.

## III.10 The governance design brief — preliminary

Part VI must resolve the governance design in full. This section states the questions and provisional answers, so that the design brief is framed before it is answered.

1. **At what membership size does a delegate structure become necessary?** *Provisional answer:* borrow the German legislative judgement of ~1,500 members as the point at which the plenary meeting ceases to function, but adopt it earlier by bye-law if growth is rapid; the goal is to keep the deliberating body small enough (target 50–150 delegates, per §43a's floor of 50) that participation is again rational. *Threshold that would change this:* if the common bond stays tight and members remain few, a plenary meeting can persist; the trigger is *n*, not assets.

2. **How to design a nominating process that permits genuine contestation?** *Provisional answer:* do the opposite of the US default. The nominating committee must not be controlled by the incumbent chair; members must have low-cost ballot access (a signature threshold set low, plus a right to the register for candidacy); floor nominations must be permitted; and elections must *not* be cancelled when nominees equal vacancies — an uncontested seat should trigger disclosure and review, not automatic election. *Benchmark:* contested elections (more candidates than seats) in a majority of cycles.

3. **How to constitute a board that is both representative and competent?** *Provisional answer:* the Co-operative Bank lesson (III.6) — separate legitimacy from competence. Reserve the executive/management tier for vetted professionals (fit-and-proper), place elected member representatives on a supervisory tier, and impose competence requirements on the pivotal offices (never a Paul Flowers chairing the bank). Cap any single constituency's board share (mirroring §8(2) GenG's one-quarter cap on investor-member supervisory seats).

4. **What audit substitute to adopt in the absence of a Prüfungsverband?** *Provisional answer:* this is the highest-priority design decision, because III.7 identifies the substitute monitor as the best predictor of degeneration. Procure a rigorous, independent, rotating external audit by contract; add a voluntary purpose-examining review; and, ideally, build or join a federation of societies that jointly commission audit and supervision, manufacturing the network monitor Nigeria's state has not supplied. *Benchmark that would change the plan:* if the RH-CRRP reforms actually create a SASRA-equivalent supervisor with real coverage, lean on it; until then, assume no public monitor.

5. **What protections to embed in the two-tier structure?** *Provisional answer:* (III.8.5) secure control rights by instrument (majority stake, golden share, or shareholders' agreement with reserved matters and board-appointment rights); raise investor capital in a governance-light instrument (inverted Rabobank certificate); entrench a contractual purpose-lock; and appoint an independent monitor of the cooperative/operating-company interface. *Threshold:* the moment investors hold economic majority, the control-rights instruments must already be in force — retrofitting is not available.

6. **How to detect degeneration early?** *Provisional answer:* define and track leading indicators the NCUA data cannot capture but a well-designed cooperative can self-collect — AGM/delegate attendance, contestation rate, board tenure and turnover, insider-lending ratios (the SASRA 20%-of-core-capital red flag), the gap between the operating company's pricing and the members' interest, and audit findings on the purpose-lock. Degeneration is a process (Cornforth); it is detectable in these metrics before it is complete, and regeneration is possible if the review is regular and honest.

## III.11 Ten propositions on cooperative governance at scale

1. **Governance is a collective good, and Olson applies to it.** In a cooperative of *n* members, the rational member's incentive to monitor management falls roughly as 1/*n*; at scale, rational abstention — not civic failure — is the equilibrium. The founder should design for apathetic members, not hope for vigilant ones.

2. **Apathy enables capture.** The same 1/*n* logic that silences the ordinary member amplifies anyone for whom control yields concentrated private benefit — managers, insiders, factions, patrons. Where the many abstain, the few who care capture.

3. **The legitimacy–scale paradox is real and unavoidable.** Democratic legitimacy is strongest at small *n* and weakest at large *n*, but the capital constraint (Part II) demands large *n*. Every large financial cooperative is therefore governing against the grain of its own ownership structure.

4. **Consolidation is the master fact, degeneration its shadow.** US credit unions fell from 23,866 (1969) to 4,287 (2025) while members rose to 144.7 million and three-quarters of them now belong to billion-dollar institutions. The population data proves the structural precondition for participation collapse; it cannot, and does not, measure the collapse itself — a boundary that must be respected.

5. **Delegate structures relocate the problem; they do not solve it.** Germany's Vertreterversammlung, Rabobank's General Members' Council, and SACCO delegate AGMs all shrink the deliberating body to a workable size, which is a genuine gain — but a delegate layer is only as accountable as its election is contestable and only as effective as the audit behind it.

6. **The US election machinery is the paradigm of democracy performed while management is insulated.** Incumbent-controlled nominating committees, arduous petition routes, and automatic cancellation of uncontested elections produce self-perpetuating boards as a systemic equilibrium, not an accident.

7. **The net-saver/net-borrower conflict is structural and permanent.** It is Cook's influence-costs problem in pure form: two member groups with directly opposed interests in the institution's core price. It cannot be solved, only balanced; borrower-dominated boards degenerate toward loose credit and eroded reserves.

8. **Democratic boards can govern banks — but only with competence-preserving scaffolding.** The Co-operative Bank failed not because it was a cooperative but because it wore the democratic form without the German scaffolding (professional management tier, supervisory separation, compulsory audit). Continental cooperative banks with member governance did not fail this way. Representativeness and competence must be reconciled by design, not assumed to coexist.

9. **The substitute monitor is the best single predictor of degeneration.** Where members cannot monitor (they cannot, at scale), someone else must; the quality of that monitor — Germany's purpose-examining Prüfungsverband at the strong end, Nigeria's near-nothing at the weak end — tracks governance outcomes across all six jurisdictions. Monitor quality must be disaggregated: a monitor can be excellent on solvency (NCUA) and useless on member control.

10. **In Nigeria, member control is a private-ordering problem.** Because the NCSA supplies almost nothing — self-chosen audit (§36), no federated supervision, an unused surcharge power (§46), thin one-member-one-vote (§25) — the machinery that other jurisdictions get from statute, the Nigerian founder must build by bye-law and contract: delegate assembly, supervisory separation, contractual purpose-examining audit, contestable elections, and, in the two-tier structure, control rights secured by instrument before investors hold the economic majority. This is the heaviest governance burden in the study, and it is the founder's to carry.
