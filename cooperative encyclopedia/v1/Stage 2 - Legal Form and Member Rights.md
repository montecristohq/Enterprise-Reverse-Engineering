# PART I — LEGAL FORM AND MEMBER RIGHTS

## TL;DR
- The single most consequential legal variable across the six jurisdictions is the **asset lock** on accumulated reserves. Quebec makes the general reserve legally indivisible (the strongest lock); Germany, counter-intuitively, does not; the United States locks credit-union net worth procedurally through conversion rules rather than a reserve rule; Kenya and Nigeria have no true asset lock at all.
- What a member "owns" is far thinner than intuition suggests: in every jurisdiction the member share is redeemable at or near par (or non-withdrawable but still refundable on exit), deposits are creditor claims, and the member has **no individual claim on accumulated reserves** while the cooperative is a going concern. The divergence lies entirely in what happens to those reserves on exit, conversion or dissolution.
- Nigerian cooperative law is **permissive but weakly supervised**: it supplies one-member-one-vote, limited liability and a member-deposit capacity, but lacks a compulsory audit federation (Germany's Prüfungsverband), a dedicated prudential regulator (Kenya's SASRA), non-withdrawable shares (Kenya) and any indivisible-reserve asset lock (Quebec). A Nigerian founder must therefore build these protections by bye-law and contract, because the statute will not supply them.

## Key Findings

1. **Germany (eG under the GenG 1889).** The statute rigorously separates the *Geschäftsanteil* (the nominal share — a ceiling on subscription) from the *Geschäftsguthaben* (the member's actual paid-in credit balance). On termination (*Kündigung*) the member is settled out (*Auseinandersetzung*) at the value of the *Geschäftsguthaben* only — never a share of reserves. Critically, German law contains **no mandatory statutory asset lock**: §91 GenG distributes net assets on dissolution to members in proportion to their credit balances, and the bylaws may vary this. The genuine institutional asset lies elsewhere: **compulsory membership of an auditing federation (Prüfungsverband) under §§53–54 GenG**, which has no analogue in the other five systems.

2. **The Netherlands (coöperatie under Book 2 BW, arts. 2:53–63).** The most permissive statute of the six — near-total freedom over profit distribution, non-member business and governance design. Three liability regimes (W.A. full, B.A. limited, U.A. excluded) explain why the bank is "Coöperatieve Rabobank U.A." No asset lock. The flexibility that allowed Rabobank to merge its local banks into one legal entity in 2016 is the same flexibility that offers weak structural protection against degeneration.

3. **Quebec/Canada.** The *Loi sur les coopératives* and, for financial cooperatives, the *Loi sur les coopératives de services financiers* (RLRQ c. C-67.3) impose a genuinely **indivisible general reserve (réserve générale impartageable)** — the strongest asset lock of the six. Quebec also pioneered **parts permanentes** (permanent shares), a permanent-capital instrument Part II will dissect. The AMF supervises; Desjardins consolidated to a single federation in 2001.

4. **United States.** There is **no single federal cooperative statute** — itself an analytical finding. Credit unions operate under the Federal Credit Union Act 1934; they issue **no capital stock**, member "shares" are functionally deposits, and net worth is **retained earnings only** (until the subordinated-debt rule effective 2022). The asset lock operates not on the reserve but on the **conversion channel**: NCUA rules (12 CFR Part 708a) govern conversion to mutual savings bank form — the US demutualisation route.

5. **Kenya.** The most developed SACCO regime in Africa: the Co-operative Societies Act (Cap 490) plus the SACCO Societies Act 2008 and SASRA supervision. The **BOSA/FOSA** distinction and the rule that **share capital is non-withdrawable while deposits are withdrawable** are the structural core. Non-withdrawable member deposits function as quasi-capital — legally fragile because they remain refundable on exit. A Cooperatives Bill 2024 was pending in the Senate as of 2026.

6. **Nigeria.** The reader's home jurisdiction and the thinnest evidence base. Cooperatives are a state matter; the Nigerian Co-operative Societies Act 1993 (Cap N98 LFN 2004) applies federally alongside state laws (Lagos as worked example). The statute supplies one-member-one-vote (§25), limited liability (§2), a 20% shareholding cap (§27) and member deposit-taking (§19) — but audit is weak (self-chosen auditors, §36), there is no Prüfungsverband and no SASRA, and no asset lock. The CBN boundary is crossed when deposits are taken from the **general public** rather than members.

## Details

### I.0 The organising question

The Prologue established a thesis this Part must test: the property-rights critique correctly identifies the *mechanisms* that erode cooperatives — Cook's five problems of free-rider (common property), horizon, portfolio, control and influence costs (Michael L. Cook, *American Journal of Agricultural Economics* 77(5), 1995, pp. 1153–1159) — but is wrong to predict inevitable failure. Cooperatives persist where the institutional environment neutralises those mechanisms, and convert or degenerate where it activates them. Part I supplies the *legal half* of that environment.

To make the comparison genuine rather than six unconnected descriptions, ten questions are asked identically of each jurisdiction: (1) legal form and supervisor; (2) what the member owns; (3) transferability of the stake; (4) exit terms; (5) voting and control; (6) liability; (7) formation; (8) audit and supervision; (9) non-member business; (10) conversion and — the master variable — the asset lock.

A caution held throughout: cooperative-federation literature (BVR, Desjardins, WOCCU, national alliances) is promotional and its factual claims are treated as claims to be tested against statute. The source hierarchy is inverted relative to a company study because cooperative law is *constitutive* — a cooperative is what its enabling statute says it is.

### I.1 Germany — the eingetragene Genossenschaft

**Legal form and supervisor.** The *eingetragene Genossenschaft* (eG) is governed by the **Genossenschaftsgesetz (GenG) of 1 May 1889**, still in force as amended (most recently by the Act of 23 October 2024, BGBl. 2024 I Nr. 323). The eG acquires legal personality on entry in the **Genossenschaftsregister** kept by the local court (§13 GenG). There is no single prudential supervisor for cooperatives as such; credit cooperatives (Volksbanken and Raiffeisenbanken) are supervised as banks by BaFin under the ordinary banking regime. [CONFIRMED FACT]

**What the member owns — the Geschäftsanteil/Geschäftsguthaben distinction.** This distinction is the most-mistranslated concept in comparative cooperative law, and it is legally decisive. The **Geschäftsanteil** is the nominal amount fixed in the bylaws (§7 no. 1 GenG) up to which a member may make contributions — a *ceiling* or accounting reference figure, not a sum of money the member holds. The **Geschäftsguthaben** is the member's *actual paid-in credit balance* — the real equity attributable to that member. Profit is credited to the Geschäftsguthaben until it reaches the Geschäftsanteil (§19 GenG); losses are written off against it. The member therefore owns: (a) a membership (the voting and participation right), and (b) the Geschäftsguthaben (a credit balance). The member does **not** own any divided share of the cooperative's reserves (Rücklagen) while the cooperative continues. Rendering *Geschäftsanteil* and *Geschäftsguthaben* both as "share" in English collapses the very distinction the statute is built on. [CONFIRMED FACT]

**Liability and the Nachschusspflicht.** Members' liability is limited to the subscribed Geschäftsanteil; §22a GenG governs the **Nachschusspflicht** — an obligation to make additional contributions to the insolvency estate — which exists only if the bylaws impose it, and which may be capped (a *Haftsumme*) or excluded entirely. This is a default that can be varied by bylaw, and the modern default in practice is exclusion: the great majority of modern eGs exclude the Nachschusspflicht in their statutes. [CONFIRMED FACT]

**Transfer and exit.** Membership itself is not freely tradable, but §76 GenG permits a member to **transfer the Geschäftsguthaben** to another (existing or incoming) member by written agreement, thereby ending membership *without* a settlement (*ohne Auseinandersetzung*). Absent transfer, a member leaves by **Kündigung** (§65 GenG): notice in writing, only to the end of a financial year, and at least **three months** in advance; the bylaws may lengthen this notice period (up to a statutory maximum), and §65(3) grants a hardship early-exit right against notice periods longer than two years. On exit the member is settled (**Auseinandersetzung**, §73 GenG) at the value of the Geschäftsguthaben as shown in the approved annual accounts; §73(2) provides that payment falls due **six months** after the membership ends unless the bylaws provide otherwise. Crucially, the departing member has **no claim on the reserves** (§73(3)): the reserves stay with the cooperative. [CONFIRMED FACT]

**Voting and delegate structures.** §43 GenG establishes **one member, one vote** as the default in the general meeting. Plural votes (**Mehrstimmrechte**) are permitted only within narrow statutory limits and typically not for consumer or credit cooperatives. For cooperatives with **more than 1,500 members**, §43a GenG permits the bylaws to replace the general meeting with a **delegate assembly (Vertreterversammlung)** of at least 50 elected delegates; delegates cannot be represented by proxy and cannot be granted plural votes (§43a(3)). [CONFIRMED FACT]

**Governance and the 2006 reform.** The eG has a two-tier board: a management board (**Vorstand**) and a supervisory board (**Aufsichtsrat**, §36 GenG). The reform of 14 August 2006 (implementing the European Cooperative Society, BGBl. I S. 1911) introduced simplifications for small cooperatives — notably allowing cooperatives of up to 20 members to dispense with the Aufsichtsrat — and permitted investor (non-user) members. [CONFIRMED FACT]

**Audit and the Prüfungsverband — a genuine institutional asset.** §§53–54 GenG require every eG to belong to an **auditing federation (Prüfungsverband)** to which the right of audit has been granted, and to undergo regular statutory audit (§53 *Pflichtprüfung*). Membership is a **precondition of registration** (§11(2) no. 3): the federation issues a pre-registration certificate assessing economic viability to protect members and creditors. This compulsory, federated external audit — examining not merely the accounts but the *member-promotion purpose* (*Förderzweck*) — has no analogue in the Netherlands, the US, Kenya or Nigeria, and the study treats it as a real mechanism that neutralises the control and influence-cost problems. [CONFIRMED FACT; the protective effect is SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS]

**Non-member business.** Permitted within limits: the cooperative's purpose is member promotion (§1 GenG), and non-member business is ancillary; the 2006 reform relaxed but did not abolish this orientation. [CONFIRMED FACT]

**Conversion and the asset-lock question — the surprising German position.** It is widely *assumed* that German cooperative reserves are locked. As a matter of the GenG, they are not. §91 GenG provides that on dissolution the net assets are distributed among members up to the total of their credit balances, in proportion to those balances; §92 GenG provides only that any *undistributable* net assets (where the bylaws direct them elsewhere) fall to the municipality of the seat for charitable use. The rechtswissenschaftliche study by Volker Beuthien (long-time director of the Marburg Institute for Cooperative Studies and a leading GenG commentator) and Verena Klappstein concluded expressly that German law — and the closely related Austrian and Swiss laws — **permits reserve distribution (*Rücklagenauskehr*)**: "there is no indivisible fund." What restrains German cooperatives from being raided is therefore *not* a statutory asset lock but a combination of the transformation regime (the Umwandlungsgesetz, which governs conversion of an eG into an AG), the compulsory audit federation, and the homogeneity of membership. [The popular claim that German reserves are "locked" is CONTESTED; the higher-ranked statutory reading (§§91–92) and the Beuthien/Klappstein study establish they are NOT automatically indivisible.] This counter-intuitive finding is developed in I.8.

### I.2 The Netherlands — the coöperatie

**Legal form and supervisor.** The **coöperatie** is a special form of association with legal personality regulated by **Book 2, Title 3 of the Burgerlijk Wetboek (arts. 2:53–63)**. It is founded by notarial deed and must operate a business meeting members' material needs under agreements concluded with them (art. 2:53 BW). Cooperative banks are additionally supervised as banks by De Nederlandsche Bank and the ECB. [CONFIRMED FACT]

**The three liability regimes.** Dutch law offers a choice, signalled in the cooperative's name: **W.A.** (*wettelijke aansprakelijkheid* — members jointly liable for a deficit on liquidation), **B.A.** (*beperkte aansprakelijkheid* — liability limited to a stated amount), and **U.A.** (*uitgesloten aansprakelijkheid* — liability excluded). Rabobank is **Coöperatieve Rabobank U.A.** precisely because members bear no liability for the cooperative's deficits, even after bankruptcy. For W.A. and B.A. cooperatives the board must file a member list with the Chamber of Commerce (KVK) annually so creditors know whom to pursue. [CONFIRMED FACT]

**Permissiveness — freedom as the defining feature.** Dutch commentators emphasise that far fewer mandatory rules apply to a coöperatie than to an NV or BV: freedom as to profit distribution, member admission and exit, voting arrangements and financing (a coöperatie may issue certificates or shares, or simply distribute profit to members). This flexibility is the statute's signature. [CONFIRMED FACT / SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS]

**What the member owns, transfer, exit.** Membership arises by agreement and is generally personal; the statute leaves share and contribution arrangements largely to the articles. There is no par-value member share mandated by statute in the German sense; the articles define financial participation, and exit terms are set by the articles subject to the general association rules in Book 2. [CONFIRMED FACT; because the statute is permissive, the specifics are *constitutional* (in the articles) rather than statutory — an ANALYTICAL INFERENCE that this shifts protective work onto the articles.]

**Governance and the ledenraad.** The general meeting is the supreme organ, but large cooperatives typically interpose a **members' council (ledenraad)**. The 2016 Rabobank restructuring is the paradigm case and is directly relevant to Part III. On the recommendation of a specially created governance committee, the local members' councils **agreed unanimously to the governance changes on 2 December 2015**, and the General Meeting of Rabobank Nederland approved the merger at meetings held on **9 December 2015**; the new structure took effect on **1 January 2016**. All **106 local Rabobanks** surrendered their independent banking licences and merged with the central institution so that the group now operates as **one cooperative — Coöperatieve Rabobank U.A. — with one banking licence and one set of consolidated financial statements**, governed through a General Members' Council composed of representatives drawn from the local members' councils. [CONFIRMED FACT — Rabobank's counsel, De Brauw Blackstone Westbroek, confirms the 106 figure and the 9 December 2015/1 January 2016 dates; note that some later commentary cites up to 139 local banks by counting entities that surrendered licences in the transition.]

**Asset lock.** Dutch law imposes **no statutory asset lock**; distribution of surplus and of liquidation proceeds is governed by the articles. The trade-off the study draws: a flexible statute is easier to *build into* — a founder can design almost any capital and governance structure — but offers weaker default protection against degeneration, because nothing in the statute prevents members from later voting to distribute reserves or restructure away from member control. [ANALYTICAL INFERENCE]

### I.3 Quebec/Canada

**Legal forms and supervisor.** Quebec has two relevant statutes: the general **Loi sur les coopératives (RLRQ c. C-67.2)** and, for financial cooperatives, the **Loi sur les coopératives de services financiers (RLRQ c. C-67.3)**, under which the **caisses Desjardins** operate. The **Autorité des marchés financiers (AMF)** supervises financial-services cooperatives and designated Desjardins a domestic systemically important financial institution in 2013. Federally, the **Canada Cooperatives Act** governs federally incorporated cooperatives, and **Part XV.1 of the Bank Act** provides a federal credit-union framework. [CONFIRMED FACT]

**What the member owns — the share taxonomy.** Quebec law distinguishes: **parts sociales** (ordinary/member shares), **parts de qualification** (qualifying shares required for membership), **parts privilégiées** (preferred shares, which may carry interest and be issued to members or non-members), and — the innovation — **parts permanentes** (permanent shares). The parts permanentes are engineered to be **non-redeemable at the member's initiative** (redeemable only in restricted circumstances), so they qualify as permanent, loss-absorbing capital, solving the redeemability problem that afflicts ordinary member shares. Their precise legal character and regulatory-capital treatment are the subject of Part II; what is established here is that they are a distinct class of participation designed to be *permanent* rather than a redeemable member stake. [CONFIRMED FACT]

**Ristournes and the reserve.** **Ristournes** (patronage refunds) are, per the modernised statute, defined as a benefit arising from member (or auxiliary-member) status, allocated by patronage. The decisive structural point is the **réserve générale**: Quebec law makes it **impartageable (indivisible)** — it cannot be shared among members during the cooperative's life, cannot be reduced by paying ristournes out of it, and is subject on liquidation to a *dévolution désintéressée* (disinterested devolution) rather than distribution to members. The rationale, in the words of the Québec scholarship, is that the cooperative's purpose is not "the accumulation of capital in order to distribute it among members" but "the creation of a collective capital that benefits all present and future members." In the financial-cooperative statute (C-67.3), annual surpluses (*trop-perçus*) are applied in a statutory order — the art. 87 reserve, then the réserve générale, then interest on capital shares, then a stabilisation reserve and a reserve for eventual ristournes, and only then ristournes to members. This is the strongest and clearest **asset lock** of the six jurisdictions. [CONFIRMED FACT]

**Voting, exit, liability.** One member, one vote governs the caisses. Ordinary member shares are redeemable at par subject to the cooperative's financial capacity; permanent shares are not redeemable at will. Members are not liable for the cooperative's debts beyond their shares. [CONFIRMED FACT]

**Structure and the 2001 restructuring.** The **2001 restructuring** consolidated the previously separate regional federations into a single **Fédération des caisses Desjardins du Québec**; legal personality sits at the level of the individual caisses (each a cooperative) and the Fédération. Per Desjardins' own history, the single Fédération in 2001 resulted from the merger of the ten regional caisse federations, the Fédération des caisses d'économie and the Confédération; secondary business histories variously cite eleven or fourteen pre-existing federations, and this discrepancy is flagged rather than resolved. Subsequent legislation (Loi 141, sanctioned July 2018) abolished the standalone *Loi sur le Mouvement Desjardins* and folded Desjardins-specific rules into the C-67.3 regime as the "Groupe coopératif Desjardins," giving the AMF resolution powers over the group. [CONFIRMED FACT; number of predecessor federations CONTESTED across secondary sources.]

### I.4 The United States

**No single federal cooperative statute.** The defining structural fact is fragmentation. There is no general federal cooperative law. Cooperatives are chartered under a patchwork: state cooperative-corporation statutes; the **Capper-Volstead Act of 1922** (7 U.S.C. §§291–292), which grants agricultural producers a limited antitrust exemption to market collectively but does *not* itself charter cooperatives; and, for depository cooperatives, the **Federal Credit Union Act of 1934 (12 U.S.C. ch. 14)**. This fragmentation is itself an analytical finding: US law has no unified concept of "the cooperative." [CONFIRMED FACT]

**Credit unions — chartering and the common bond.** Federal credit unions are chartered and supervised by the **National Credit Union Administration (NCUA)**; a dual system lets institutions choose a state or federal charter. §109 FCUA requires a **field of membership / common bond** — single occupational, single associational, multiple common bond, or community. This was progressively liberalised: the NCUA began permitting multiple-group fields in 1982; the banking industry challenged this and won in **NCUA v. First National Bank & Trust Co.** (118 S. Ct. 927, decided 25 February 1998), which held that all members of a federal credit union must share a single common bond. Congress promptly overrode the decision with the **Credit Union Membership Access Act (H.R. 1151)**, signed by President Clinton on **7 August 1998**, which authorised multiple-common-bond credit unions. The statute codified the group-size limit at **12 U.S.C. §1759(d)(1)**: only a group with **fewer than 3,000 members** is eligible for inclusion in a multiple-common-bond credit union's field of membership (subject to enumerated exceptions). [CONFIRMED FACT]

**What the member owns — no capital stock.** The critical structural fact: a US credit union has **no capital stock**. Member "shares" are functionally **deposits** (withdrawable, insured up to the NCUSIF limit), not equity in the German or Quebec sense. §216(o) FCUA defines net worth as the **retained-earnings balance** determined under GAAP; the net-worth ratio drives prompt-corrective-action categories (a regime modelled on §38 of the Federal Deposit Insurance Act). Until the NCUA's subordinated-debt rule (effective 2022, as established in the Prologue), credit unions could build net worth *only* through retained earnings — they could not issue capital. The member thus owns a withdrawable deposit and a vote; the member owns **no claim on retained earnings**. [CONFIRMED FACT]

**Voting.** One member, one vote is mandatory; proportional voting is prohibited. Federal credit-union boards must have an odd number of directors — at least five and no more than 15. [CONFIRMED FACT]

**Conversion — the US demutualisation channel and its asset lock.** Because there is no capital stock and net worth is collective retained earnings, the demutualisation question presents at the point of **conversion to a mutual savings bank**. §202 of the 1998 Act authorised conversion of an insured credit union to a mutual savings bank or association subject to member approval, without prior NCUA approval; the NCUA governs the process under **12 CFR Part 708a**, with detailed member-notice, disclosure and voting requirements (a credit union with members' approval "may convert to a mutual savings bank or a savings association that is in mutual form"; the rule was first adopted 1995 and revised in 1999, 2005 and 2006). The concern the NCUA has repeatedly flagged is the **second-step windfall**: conversion to mutual form, then a mutual-to-stock IPO, generates windfall subscription rights for insiders while the members' collective retained earnings — built over generations — are effectively appropriated. The asset lock here is therefore *procedural* (conversion rules and disclosure) rather than a substantive bar on distributing retained earnings. [CONFIRMED FACT]

### I.5 Kenya

**Legal forms and supervisor.** Two statutes: the general **Co-operative Societies Act (Cap 490)**, administered by the **Commissioner for Co-operative Development**, and the **SACCO Societies Act No. 14 of 2008**, administered by the **SACCO Societies Regulatory Authority (SASRA)**, which licenses and supervises deposit-taking SACCOs. Regulations: the SACCO Societies (Deposit-Taking SACCO Business) Regulations 2010 (Legal Notice No. 95 of 2010) and the (Non-Deposit-Taking Business) Regulations 2020 (effective 1 January 2021). SASRA supervises deposit-taking SACCOs and, since 2021, specified non-deposit-taking SACCOs. [CONFIRMED FACT]

**BOSA vs FOSA — the core structural concept.** This is the single most important structural distinction in East African SACCO practice. **BOSA (Back Office Savings Activity)** is the traditional savings-and-loan operation: members' savings are held as **non-withdrawable deposits** that are not accessible on demand, that serve as collateral for loans, and that are refundable only on cessation of membership. **FOSA (Front Office Savings Activity)** is a banking-counter operation offering **withdrawable** demand deposits, transfers and ATM services — this is what makes a SACCO "deposit-taking" and requires a SASRA licence. The statutory definition tracks this: a SACCO conducting deposit-taking business receives "withdrawable deposits, domestic money transfer services, loans... and credit facilities," whereas non-deposit-taking business receives "non-withdrawable deposits from members... not available for withdrawal for the duration of the membership." [CONFIRMED FACT]

**Share capital vs deposits — and the quasi-capital fragility.** Kenyan law draws the decisive line: **share capital is non-withdrawable** — a member who leaves cannot withdraw shares but must find a transferee — while **deposits are withdrawable** (in a FOSA) or non-withdrawable-but-refundable-on-exit (BOSA). Because BOSA deposits are typically large relative to share capital and function economically as the SACCO's working capital, they operate as **quasi-capital** — but they are *legally* deposits, refundable on exit. This is legally fragile: quasi-capital that can walk out the door on member exit is not true permanent capital, and a run of exits can decapitalise a SACCO. This is a direct instance of the horizon and portfolio problems the Prologue identified. [CONFIRMED FACT on the legal distinction; ANALYTICAL INFERENCE on the fragility]

**Capital requirements.** DT-SACCOs must maintain a minimum **core capital of KSh 10 million** and prescribed ratios — **core capital of at least 10% of total assets** and **at least 8% of total deposits** — with at least 50% of core capital composed of retained earnings and disclosed reserves (SACCO Societies (Deposit-Taking Sacco Business) Regulations 2010, LN 95 of 2010). SASRA has revoked the licences of SACCOs failing these standards. [CONFIRMED FACT]

**Voting, liability, non-member business.** One member, one vote regardless of shareholding; limited liability; SACCOs transact essentially only with members, membership being the gateway to services. [CONFIRMED FACT]

**Audit and asset lock.** SACCOs are audited and file returns with SASRA (DT) or the Commissioner/SASRA (specified NDT); SASRA's annual supervision reports are a genuine regulator dataset. Kenyan law does **not** impose a Quebec-style indivisible reserve; statutory reserves are required, but the regime is prudential (capital adequacy) rather than an asset lock against distribution or conversion. [CONFIRMED FACT on the prudential regime; ANALYTICAL INFERENCE on the absence of an asset lock]

**Legislative position 2026.** A **Cooperatives Bill 2024** (National Assembly Bill No. 7 of 2024) passed the National Assembly with amendments on **3 December 2024** and was introduced in the Senate on **12 February 2025**, under Second Reading and public participation as of 2026; a separate **SACCO Societies (Amendment) Bill 2025** is also in progress. The Bill aims to align cooperative law with the 2010 Constitution's devolved-governance structure. [CONFIRMED FACT — labelled PENDING as of 2026]

Kenya matters disproportionately for the reader because it is the most developed SACCO regulatory regime in Africa and the most plausible model for Nigeria — a point the Nigerian government has itself conceded by benchmarking its reform against Kenya (see I.6).

### I.6 Nigeria — the most important section, and the thinnest evidence

**A candid statement of evidence quality.** Nigerian state cooperative laws are inconsistently published, and several are effectively inaccessible without paid access to state gazettes. Where a specific provision cannot be located in a primary source, this study writes **UNKNOWN** rather than inferring from other Commonwealth jurisdictions. The federal Act text is available (FAOLEX); Lagos State provisions are reconstructed largely from law-firm summaries and are flagged where unverified against the gazette.

**Constitutional position.** Cooperatives are a **state** matter in Nigeria. There is consequently **no single national cooperative law of general application**. The **Nigerian Co-operative Societies Act 1993 (Cap N98 LFN 2004; originally Decree No. 90 of 1993, commenced 26 August 1993)** applies at the federal level, and **state Co-operative Societies Laws** operate alongside it, with the **Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999 (as amended)** as backdrop. Lagos State's law is the worked example; other states vary and several provisions are UNKNOWN. [CONFIRMED FACT]

**Registration and formation.** Registration is with the **Director/Registrar of Co-operatives** — a Federal Director appointed by the President and, in each state, a State Director appointed by the Governor (§1). A **primary society** requires **at least ten persons** qualified for membership (§3(1)); an industrial society a minimum of six (§3(2)); a secondary society at least five registered societies (§3(3)). The name must include "Co-operative" and end in "Limited" (§3(4)–(5)), and "bank"/"banking" is prohibited except for a central financing society (§3(6)). Bye-laws accompany the application (§4), the Director may alter them to conform to the Act, and registration renders the society a **body corporate** with perpetual succession and a common seal (§6). In Lagos, administration runs through the Ministry responsible for cooperatives and the Office of the Registrar/Director of Co-operative Societies (the Director being drawn from senior State civil-service ranks); the base primary-society minimum tracks the federal figure of ten, though some practitioner guides cite higher type-specific minimums (savings/credit societies commonly cited at 20 or more). The divergence is noted, and the exact Lagos statutory figure beyond ten is UNKNOWN pending gazette verification. [CONFIRMED FACT (federal); PARTLY UNKNOWN (Lagos specifics)]

**What the member owns; shares and withdrawability.** The Act contemplates member **shares** and member **deposits** but does not theorise the distinction as sharply as German or Kenyan law. Bye-laws must address "the manner of raising the share capital, if any" (§11(2)(i)), "the transfer of shares or interests of members" (§11(2)(h)), and "the withdrawal and expulsion of members and the payment, if any, to be made to those members" (§11(2)(g)). No member other than a registered society may hold **more than one-fifth (20%)** of the share capital (§27). Whether shares are withdrawable is left to the bye-laws — there is **no statutory rule making shares non-withdrawable** as in Kenya. The society has a statutory **charge and set-off** over a member's shares, interest and deposits for debts owed (§16), and shares/interest are **not liable to attachment or sale** by a member's creditors (§17). [CONFIRMED FACT]

**Deposits from members.** The Act expressly contemplates the society **receiving deposits**, including from or for minors (§19), and treats member deposits as a category distinct from shares (§16). The legal basis for member deposit-taking is therefore the cooperative statute itself — but only for **members**. [CONFIRMED FACT]

**Liability.** A registered society is a **limited liability** society (§2(1)(a)); a member's liability is limited by the bye-laws. A **past member** remains liable for debts existing at the date of cessation for up to **two years** (§29), and the estate of a deceased member likewise for two years (§29(2)). [CONFIRMED FACT]

**Voting.** §25 provides **one vote only** per member, with a chairman's casting vote on equality; a registered society that is itself a member may exercise weighted voting per its bye-laws. [CONFIRMED FACT]

**Non-member business.** §32 governs transactions with non-members (permitted within limits set by the Act and bye-laws). [CONFIRMED FACT — precise limits UNKNOWN without full text]

**Audit and supervision — and how weak it is.** §36 provides for **audit**, and the ICA's own legal-framework analysis notes that the Act lets societies choose their own auditor "without any interference from the Director" — praised as autonomy but, from a supervisory standpoint, a weakness. §37 covers inquiry and inspection; §46 empowers the Director to **surcharge** managers who misapply property, requiring restoration with interest. There is **no compulsory auditing federation** (contrast Germany's Prüfungsverband) and **no dedicated prudential regulator** (contrast Kenya's SASRA). Multiple assessments converge on weak enforcement in practice: the ICA/coops4dev analysis rates the law's "cooperative friendliness" as very limited and calls for members to be protected against loss caused by "unscrupulous management committee members"; Nigerian academic case studies (e.g., in Kaduna and Imo States) document recurrent fraud in thrift-and-credit societies attributed to poor book-keeping and unskilled auditors; and the Federal Government's own 2026 reform materials describe a sector suffering "weak governance systems, outdated operations, poor transparency, and financial irregularities." [CONFIRMED FACT (statutory sections); SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS and government admission (weakness in practice)]

**CAMA 2020 does not apply — and why it matters.** The **Companies and Allied Matters Act 2020** governs companies, LLPs and business names but **expressly does not govern cooperative societies**, which are creatures of the Cooperative Societies laws and file with the Director of Cooperatives, not the Corporate Affairs Commission. Cooperatives are therefore outside CAMA's annual-return, charge-registration and disclosure obligations and enjoy tax exemptions. This matters because a Nigerian cooperative sits *outside* the country's main corporate-transparency and filing regime — the very machinery that would otherwise supply external discipline. [CONFIRMED FACT]

**The CBN boundary — flagged, not resolved.** At what point does a cooperative taking member deposits become an institution requiring a **Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN)** licence? The controlling instrument is the **Banks and Other Financial Institutions Act (BOFIA) 2020** (signed into law 13 November 2020). §2(1) bars carrying on banking business without a licence; §2(2) makes unlicensed banking a criminal offence (refund of deposits plus imprisonment of at least ten years or a fine of at least ₦20,000,000, or both). "Banking business" (§131) centres on receiving deposits on current, savings or similar accounts and expressly extends to digital and electronic deposit-taking; the deeming provision (§2(5)) turns on accepting deposits **from the general public** or soliciting deposits. The analytical boundary is therefore: a cooperative taking savings **only from its registered members** operates within the cooperative statute and does not require a CBN licence; a cooperative (or "cooperative" fintech) that **solicits or accepts deposits from the general public / non-members** triggers BOFIA §2 and requires CBN licensing. No dedicated CBN circular directed at cooperative societies was located; the CBN's 2021 microfinance framework leaves financial cooperatives outside CBN prudential supervision, and enforcement against fake "cooperative" deposit schemes is pursued by the EFCC and SEC rather than by CBN licensing. Part V develops this; Part I only flags it. [CONFIRMED FACT on the BOFIA provisions; ANALYTICAL INFERENCE on the member/public boundary; no Nigeria-specific CBN cooperative circular found — flagged]

**Conversion and asset lock.** Nigerian law contains **no asset lock**: reserves are not statutorily indivisible, and the Act provides for division (§51), amalgamation (§52) and liquidation with distribution of funds (§43). There is no equivalent of Quebec's *impartageable* reserve or even Germany's audit-federation discipline. [CONFIRMED FACT / ANALYTICAL INFERENCE]

**Pending federal legislation — status with a date.** As of August 2026, **no formal federal Cooperative Societies Bill has been introduced in the National Assembly**. Reform exists only as (a) the **Renewed Hope Cooperative Reform and Revamp Programme (RH-CRRP) 2025–2030**, formally launched at the LASCOFED Multipurpose Hall, Ikeja, Lagos, on **Monday 11 May 2026** (as approved at the 8th Regular Meeting of the National Council on Cooperative Affairs in March 2026), led by the Minister of State for Agriculture and Food Security, Sen. Dr. Aliyu Sabi Abdullahi; and (b) a stated government *intention* to draft a new Cooperative Societies Act aligned with ICA standards, benchmarked against Kenya. A federal Ministerial Technical Committee to drive the reform was inaugurated in Abuja on **14 May 2026**, with a mandate that includes guiding "the amendment and modernization of the Nigerian Cooperative Societies Act." A proposed member-owned "Cooperative Bank of Nigeria," explicitly inspired by Kenya's cooperative banking structure, remains a plan, not an operating institution. **No bill name, number or reading date exists — the reform is prospective.** [CONFIRMED FACT that reform is only at policy stage; the existence of any introduced bill is UNKNOWN because none appears to exist.]

### I.7 The five structural variables that actually differ

Stripping away detail, the jurisdictions genuinely diverge on five variables, each with direct consequences for a founder:

1. **Redeemability of the member share.** Germany: the Geschäftsguthaben is redeemed at book value on exit after notice, never including reserves. Quebec: ordinary shares redeemable at par subject to financial capacity; permanent shares *not* redeemable at will. US: "shares" are withdrawable deposits — fully redeemable, which is precisely why net worth must be retained earnings. Kenya: **shares non-withdrawable** (transfer only); deposits withdrawable. Nigeria: withdrawability left to bye-laws — the statute is silent, so the founder must choose. The spectrum runs from Kenya's non-withdrawable share (protective) to the US withdrawable share (which forces reliance on retained earnings).

2. **Asset lock on reserves.** Quebec: **yes** — indivisible general reserve, the strongest. Germany: **no** statutory lock (§§91–92 permit distribution), restrained instead by audit federation and transformation law. Netherlands: **no**. US: no reserve lock, but a **procedural** lock via conversion rules on retained earnings. Kenya: **no** true lock (prudential reserves only). Nigeria: **no**. This is the master variable (I.8).

3. **Strength of compulsory audit/supervision.** Germany: **compulsory Prüfungsverband** — strongest. Kenya: **SASRA** prudential supervision for DT-SACCOs — strong and improving. Quebec: **AMF** — strong. US: **NCUA** — strong prudential regulator. Netherlands: bank supervision by DNB/ECB for cooperative banks; ordinary cooperatives lightly supervised. Nigeria: **weakest** — self-chosen auditors, under-resourced Director, no federation, no prudential regulator.

4. **Plural voting / delegate structures.** One-member-one-vote is universal as the baseline. Germany permits narrow Mehrstimmrechte and a Vertreterversammlung above 1,500 members. Others largely prohibit plural voting; delegate and representative structures appear by scale (Rabobank's members' councils, Desjardins' federation, large SACCOs' delegate AGMs), but plural voting is generally barred. The divergence is modest but matters at scale (Part III).

5. **Legal separability of ownership from the customer relationship.** In a German eG or Quebec caisse, membership (ownership) is a distinct legal act from being a customer — one can, within limits, be one without the other, and investor members are possible in Germany post-2006. In a US credit union and a Kenyan SACCO, ownership is **fused** to the customer relationship (a share is a deposit; membership is the gateway to services). This determines whether a fintech can separate its user base from its owner base — a first-order design question for the reader.

### I.8 The asset lock as the master variable

The Prologue established that the demutualisation incentive — the temptation to convert collective reserves into individual windfalls — is the mechanism most likely to destroy a cooperative that has accumulated value. The claim to be tested here is that **the presence or absence of an asset lock is the single most consequential legal difference between these systems, because it determines whether that incentive can be acted upon at all.**

The evidence supports the claim, with one important refinement. The cleanest natural experiment is the **UK building societies**. Their reserves were, in effect, **distributable to members on conversion**, and the Building Societies Act 1986 permitted demutualisation. The result was a wave of conversions — **Abbey National (1989)**, Cheltenham & Gloucester (1995), **Halifax (1997)**, Woolwich, Alliance & Leicester, **Northern Rock (1997)** and **Bradford & Bingley (2000)** — driven substantially by **member windfalls**, with "carpetbaggers" opening accounts purely to force conversion votes and capture the payout. As the Co-operative News records, "Demutualisation was an undoubted failure, as the shareholder owned mortgage banks that resulted all failed" — every UK society that floated in this wave was subsequently sold or nationalised. Where reserves were distributable, the incentive was acted upon, and the sector was hollowed out.

Contrast **Quebec**, where the *impartageable* reserve means there is simply **nothing to distribute** — a member who forces a wind-up receives no share of reserves, so the carpetbagger calculus collapses at the outset. The **US credit unions** occupy an intermediate position: retained earnings are not distributable as such, but conversion to mutual-then-stock form can capture them at a second step, which is exactly why the NCUA's Part 708a disclosure-and-voting rules exist as a *procedural* substitute for a substantive lock.

The refinement is **Germany**. On the master-variable logic one would predict German cooperatives to be vulnerable, because §§91–92 GenG do **not** lock reserves. Yet German cooperative banks have proved highly demutualisation-resistant. The resolution: the asset lock is the *most consequential single* legal variable, but it is not the *only* mechanism. Germany substitutes a dense institutional environment — compulsory audit federations, an institutional-protection scheme (the BVR guarantee), homogeneous membership, and a transformation law that makes conversion cumbersome — that neutralises the incentive even without a statutory lock. This is precisely the Prologue's thesis: cooperatives persist where the environment neutralises the property-rights mechanisms. An asset lock is the most direct neutraliser; where it is absent, *equivalent* neutralisation must come from audit, protection schemes and membership homogeneity, or the cooperative is exposed.

**Position reached:** the asset lock is the master variable in the sense that its *presence* is close to sufficient to defeat the demutualisation incentive (Quebec), and its *absence* is dangerous unless compensated (Germany compensates through its institutional environment; the UK did not, and converted). For a founder, the operational lesson is stark: if the governing statute supplies no asset lock — as in Nigeria — one must be **built by bye-law**, and even then a bye-law lock is weaker than a statutory one, because members can amend bye-laws. [ANALYTICAL INFERENCE, strongly supported by the UK/Quebec/Germany evidence]

### I.9 What Nigerian law lacks, and what that implies

Measured against the other five systems on the ten organising questions, Nigerian cooperative law is **permissive but weakly supervised**. Question by question:

- **Legal form/supervisor (Q1):** present but fragmented (state matter; Director of Cooperatives; no prudential regulator). Weaker than Kenya's SASRA-plus-Commissioner two-tier.
- **What the member owns (Q2):** shares and deposits contemplated but not sharply distinguished; the decisive Kenyan rule (shares non-withdrawable) is **absent**.
- **Transfer (Q3):** permitted per bye-laws (§11(2)(h), §28); 20% holding cap (§27).
- **Exit (Q4):** governed by bye-laws (§11(2)(g)); **no statutory notice period and no statutory bar on refunding reserves** — a gap relative to Germany's §§65/73.
- **Voting (Q5):** one-member-one-vote present (§25) — a genuine protective mechanism Nigeria *does* have.
- **Liability (Q6):** limited (§2); two-year tail for past members (§29) — present and adequate.
- **Formation (Q7):** ten members for a primary society (§3(1)); low barrier — present.
- **Audit/supervision (Q8):** **the critical deficiency** — self-chosen auditors (§36), no Prüfungsverband, no SASRA, weak enforcement in practice.
- **Non-member business (Q9):** regulated (§32) but limits UNKNOWN in detail.
- **Conversion/asset lock (Q10):** **no asset lock** — reserves divisible; the single most consequential absence.

The conclusion is not softened: Nigeria supplies the *cheap* protective mechanisms (one-member-one-vote, limited liability, a low formation threshold, a member-deposit capacity) but **lacks the expensive, structural ones** — compulsory external audit, prudential supervision, non-withdrawable shares, and an asset lock. These absences map precisely onto the mechanisms the Prologue identified as decisive. A Nigerian cooperative fintech therefore operates in an environment that *activates* rather than *neutralises* the property-rights problems, unless the founder builds the missing mechanisms by contract and bye-law. This is the bridge into Parts V and VI. [ANALYTICAL INFERENCE]

### I.10 Preliminary implications for the design brief

Part VI must resolve, given this legal landscape, at least the following — stated as questions, not answers:

1. **Asset lock by contract.** Since Nigerian statute supplies none, can an indivisible reserve be created by bye-law that members cannot later amend away — e.g., a Quebec-style *dévolution désintéressée* clause directing reserves to a foundation on dissolution? How robust is such a clause against a subsequent member vote to amend the bye-laws?
2. **Share design.** Should member shares be made **non-withdrawable** (Kenyan model) by bye-law to convert them into quasi-permanent capital, and can a permanent-share instrument (Quebec's parts permanentes) be replicated contractually? (Part II.)
3. **Audit substitute.** With no Prüfungsverband, what private substitute — mandatory external audit by a reputable firm, an apex-body audit function, or a contractual supervisory covenant with lenders — can approximate compulsory federated audit?
4. **The deposit boundary.** Where exactly must the fintech draw the member/public line to stay within the cooperative statute and outside BOFIA §2, and can a remittance offering be structured for members only? (Part V.)
5. **Ownership/customer separability.** Given that Kenyan and US practice fuses ownership to the customer relationship while German and Quebec practice separate them, which model should a Nigerian cooperative fintech adopt, and does the chosen model determine whether it can raise outside capital without degenerating?
6. **Governance at scale.** At what membership size do delegate structures become necessary, and can Nigerian bye-laws create a Vertreterversammlung-style delegate assembly without statutory authorisation? (Part III.)

## Recommendations

For the founder building a cooperative fintech in Nigeria, staged and concrete:

1. **At formation.** Register under the applicable state Co-operative Societies Law (Lagos as base case), not CAMA. Draft bye-laws that (a) make member shares **non-withdrawable** and transferable only (Kenyan model), (b) impose an **indivisible-reserve clause** with disinterested devolution on dissolution (Quebec model), and (c) mandate **annual external audit by a reputable independent firm** as a standing bye-law obligation (German-substitute). These three provisions are the load-bearing protections the statute will not supply. *Benchmark that would change this: enactment of a federal Cooperative Societies Act with a statutory asset lock and a prudential regulator — then rely on statute rather than bye-law.*

2. **Before taking any member funds.** Draw the deposit boundary explicitly. Take savings and deposits **only from registered members**, document the member relationship rigorously, and avoid any public solicitation of deposits, to remain within the cooperative statute and outside BOFIA §2. *Benchmark: if the business model requires public (non-member) deposits, or general remittance for non-members, a CBN licence becomes unavoidable — re-architect as, or partner with, a licensed institution.*

3. **Before scaling past a few thousand members.** Adopt a delegate-assembly governance structure by bye-law (a Vertreterversammlung / members'-council model) to preserve democratic control at scale, and pre-commit reserve policy so that the reserve grows faster than any single member's redeemable stake. *Benchmark: membership crossing the low thousands, where a plenary general meeting becomes unworkable — Germany fixes this trigger at 1,500 members.*

4. **Ongoing.** Track the RH-CRRP reform and any Cooperative Societies Bill; if a federal Act with a prudential regulator and an asset lock is enacted, migrate governance to comply and rely on the statutory protections in place of the bye-law substitutes.

Higher-ranked (statutory) sources prevail here; the conflict is flagged rather than papered over.
