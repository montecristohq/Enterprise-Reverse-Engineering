# PART II — CAPITAL
### *The Cooperative Encyclopedia: Legal Form, Capital, Governance and the Nigerian Path*

> **Reader's orientation.** Part I established that the asset lock on accumulated reserves is the master *legal* variable and dissected member rights on exit. Part II turns to the *economic and prudential* consequence of those rights: the capital problem. This is the heaviest part of the document because capital is the constraint through which every other cooperative pathology — the horizon problem, degeneration, absorption, insolvency — is ultimately expressed. The Part proceeds from the problem (II.1), through the arithmetic that makes it lethal (II.2), the two rule-systems that classify it (accounting, II.3; prudential, II.4), the two organic solutions (retained earnings and indivisible reserves, II.5), the eleven engineered instruments (II.6), their synthesis into a capital–control frontier (II.7), the Nigerian availability audit (II.8), and ten teachable propositions (II.9).

## II.1 The problem, stated with precision

A financial cooperative needs permanent loss-absorbing capital for **two logically distinct reasons that are routinely conflated**, and the conflation is itself a source of error.

**The economic reason.** Any deposit-taking or credit-extending institution intermediates: it holds assets whose value is uncertain (loans that may default, securities that may fall) against liabilities that are fixed in nominal terms (deposits, borrowings). When assets lose value, someone must absorb the loss before the depositor does. Equity — a claim that ranks last and has no maturity — is the shock absorber. Without it, the first loss lands on depositors, and the rational depositor, anticipating this, runs. Capital is therefore the precondition of the maturity transformation that is the institution's entire economic function. This is true whether or not a regulator exists.

**The prudential reason.** A regulator, standing in for dispersed depositors who cannot monitor the bank, mandates a *minimum* quantum of qualifying capital and defines, by rule, what counts. This is a legal artefact layered on top of the economic reality. The regulator's definition of "capital" is narrower and more formal than the economist's: it demands permanence, loss-absorption on a going-concern basis, and full discretion over distributions. **The gap between what is economically loss-absorbing and what is prudentially recognised is exactly where the cooperative capital problem lives.** [ANALYTICAL INFERENCE, reasoning shown throughout II.3–II.4.]

**Why the member share fails on both counts.** Part I and the Prologue established the finding, which we verify and build upon here: the ordinary member share is *redeemable at or near par on exit*. That single feature is fatal in two registers:

- *In accounting substance* (II.3): a redeemable instrument embodies a contractual obligation to deliver cash to the holder that the entity cannot unconditionally avoid. Under IAS 32, as interpreted by IFRIC 2 (issued November 2004, effective 1 January 2005), such an instrument is a **financial liability**, not equity. A liability cannot be the residual, loss-absorbing claim. [CONFIRMED FACT.]
- *In prudential terms* (II.4): capital that the holder can demand back is capital that vanishes in precisely the stress scenario for which it was held. CRR Article 29 and the EBA RTS (Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 241/2014) recognise members' shares as Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) *only to the extent* the institution can refuse or limit their redemption. [CONFIRMED FACT.]

**The redemption paradox — the fulcrum of the whole Part.** To make the member share count as capital, the cooperative must curtail the very redemption right that makes membership attractive to a saver. A share you can always get back at par is a good deal for the member and useless as capital; a share you can never get back is excellent capital and a hard sell to a member who could instead hold an insured, withdrawable deposit. Every instrument catalogued in II.6 is an attempt to escape, price, or relocate this paradox. **None abolishes it.**

**Connection to Cook's five property-rights problems.** Michael L. Cook (*AJAE* 77(5), 1995, pp. 1153–1159) identifies five vaguely-defined-property-rights constraints: free-rider (common property), horizon, portfolio, control, and influence costs. Three bear directly on capital:

- The **horizon problem**: because a member's claim on residual value is truncated at exit (she recovers par, not a share of accumulated reserves), she rationally prefers current distributions (interest, patronage refunds, low prices) over retained earnings whose benefits accrue after she has gone. She will therefore *under-invest in the very reserves that constitute permanent capital.*
- The **portfolio problem**: because the member cannot adjust her cooperative investment to her risk preference (shares are par-value, non-appreciating, tied to patronage), she is over-exposed and wants her money out, not in.
- The **free-rider problem** (internal variant): new members obtain the same claim on collectively-built reserves as old members who financed them, diluting the incentive to build reserves at all.

These are not separate from the capital problem; they are its behavioural microfoundations. The redemption paradox is the *legal* face; the horizon and portfolio problems are the *economic* face; they are the same coin.

**Connection to the lifecycle model.** The Prologue's lifecycle model posits a phase 3 in which a maturing cooperative faces competitive or growth pressure requiring a step-change in capital. Capital scarcity is what makes phase 3 lethal: the institution that cannot issue equity on demand must either grow slowly enough to fund expansion from retained earnings (II.2), engineer a permanent instrument (II.6), lean on a mutualised support scheme (II.6, item 11), or convert. **The organising thesis of the whole document — that the property-rights critique is right about mechanisms but wrong as a prediction of inevitable failure — is tested in this Part more sharply than anywhere else, because capital is where the mechanisms either bind or are neutralised.**

## II.2 The growth arithmetic — worked properly

The capital constraint becomes concrete through the **sustainable growth rate**. The derivation is elementary but its implications are severe.

**The derivation.** Let a cooperative be bound by a minimum capital ratio *k* (capital ÷ assets). To keep the ratio constant as assets *A* grow, capital *C* must grow at the same rate: if *C = kA*, then *ΔC/C = ΔA/A*. Capital grows only by retaining surplus. Let ROE be return on equity (surplus ÷ capital) and *b* the retention ratio (fraction of surplus retained rather than distributed as interest, patronage refund, or dividend). Then:

> ΔC = b × (ROE × C), so **ΔC/C = b × ROE**.

Because ΔA/A must equal ΔC/C to hold the ratio, the **maximum sustainable asset growth** is:

> **g\* = b × ROE.**

A cooperative that retains *all* surplus (*b* = 1) at 6% ROE grows assets at 6% and no faster. This is the growth arithmetic carried forward from the Prologue, now derived. The crucial point: **g\* is independent of *k* in steady state** — the capital ratio determines how much capital you need, but the *growth rate* of capital is fixed by profitability and retention. An investor-owned bank breaks this ceiling by issuing new equity: it can grow faster than b × ROE by selling shares to raise *C* discretely. **A pure cooperative cannot; the member share is not a growth instrument because every naira raised must eventually be returned at par.** [ANALYTICAL INFERENCE.]

### Scenario 1 — A Kenyan DT-SACCO under the 10%-of-assets core-capital rule

SASRA's Deposit-Taking regulations require core capital ≥ 10% of total assets (LN 95 of 2010), so *k* = 0.10. Suppose the SACCO earns a surplus equal to 1.5% of assets (a realistic return-on-assets for a Kenyan DT-SACCO) and wishes to distribute nothing. ROA = 1.5%; since C = 0.10A, ROE = ROA/k = 1.5%/10% = 15%. With b = 1, **g\* = 15%.** That looks comfortable — but members expect a dividend on share capital and interest/rebate on deposits. If the SACCO must pay out two-thirds of surplus to keep members from exiting (b = 0.33), g\* falls to **~5%**, below the ~10%+ nominal asset growth the sector actually posts. The SASRA 2024 SACCO Supervision Annual Report (released 25 September 2025) records aggregate DT-SACCO capital-to-total-assets at **17.28%, "above the prescribed minimum of 10 per cent, compared to 16.07 per cent in 2023,"** and institutional capital to total assets at 11.97% — meaning the sector *as a whole* is presently over the minimum, but the binding constraint bites at the individual, fast-growing SACCO. Deposits grew to **KSh 749.43 billion in 2024, up 9.86% from KSh 682 billion in 2023.** SASRA placed 30 SACCOs on heightened supervision for failing minimum capital requirements, and its own report notes SACCOs "grew their total assets and deposits at faster rates than the rate at which they grew their core capital." **That sentence is the growth arithmetic failing in the field.** [CONFIRMED FACT (SASRA 2024); ANALYTICAL INFERENCE (worked example).]

### Scenario 2 — A European cooperative bank under CRR

Take a cooperative bank with a CET1 requirement (including buffers) of, say, 14% of risk-weighted assets — the neighbourhood in which large European cooperative groups actually operate (Crédit Agricole Group reports a 14.4% CET1 ratio; Rabobank targets a minimum 14% CET1). Suppose RWAs are 50% of total assets, so capital ÷ total assets ≈ 7%. If ROE on that capital is 6% and the bank retains everything, **g\* = 6%.** Cooperative banks are chronically lower on profitability than investor-owned peers, a durability trade-off documented in IMF Working Paper WP/07/2 (Martin Čihák & Heiko Hesse, *Cooperative Banks and Financial Stability*, 2007), which finds cooperative banks "more stable than commercial banks… due to the lower volatility of the cooperative banks' returns, which more than offsets their lower profitability and capitalization." To grow the balance sheet faster — to fund an acquisition, absorb a failing peer, or meet a step-up in buffer requirements — the bank must find capital it cannot generate internally. This is precisely the situation that produced Rabobank Certificates and the Crédit Agricole listing (II.6). [ANALYTICAL INFERENCE, anchored to CONFIRMED FACT capital ratios and IMF finding.]

### Scenario 3 — A hypothetical Nigerian cooperative fintech (the reader)

Suppose the founder's cooperative launches with ₦200 million of member share capital, targets a remittance-and-credit book, and — critically — is *unregulated as to prudential capital* while it remains a mere cooperative society (II.4, II.8). There is no *k* imposed by law. But the economic reason for capital (II.1) does not disappear because the regulator is absent: a remittance book carries operational, settlement, and fraud risk, and a credit book carries default risk. Assume the founder prudently self-imposes k = 10% and earns ROA of 2% (fintech-flattering). ROE = 20%; with full retention g\* = 20%. That is a healthy ceiling — *until* the entity seeks a CBN licence. The moment it becomes, say, a National Microfinance Bank, it faces a **₦5 billion minimum paid-up capital requirement** (CBN Guidelines, January 2020; State MFB ₦1 billion; Unit MFB ₦20 million). No plausible retention path bridges ₦200 million to ₦5 billion: at g\* = 20% it would take **over 18 years** (200m × 1.20ⁿ = 5,000m ⇒ n = ln 25 / ln 1.2 ≈ 17.7). **The regulatory capital wall is not scalable by retained earnings; it must be vaulted by an equity raise the cooperative form cannot execute without outside investors.** This is the single most important number in the Part for the reader, and it recurs in II.8. [CONFIRMED FACT (CBN thresholds); ANALYTICAL INFERENCE (bridging calculation).]

**The menu when required growth exceeds g\*.** In every scenario, when growth demand exceeds b × ROE, the institution has only five moves: (1) **slow down** (cede the market to faster-capitalising rivals — the quiet road to irrelevance); (2) **lever up** (breach prudence, raise *k*'s denominator faster than its numerator — the road to fragility); (3) **breach the ratio** (invite supervisory intervention); (4) **merge** (absorption — a failure mode); or (5) **convert / raise outside capital** (demutualise, or engineer an instrument that admits outside money — the subject of II.6). There is no sixth option. The property-rights critique is, at this level, simply correct. The question the rest of the Part answers is whether option (5) can be executed *without* surrendering control — i.e., whether the institutional environment offers instruments that neutralise the mechanism.

## II.3 The accounting frame — IFRIC 2 in depth

### The IAS 32 liability/equity distinction

IAS 32 classifies a financial instrument by the **substance of the contractual arrangement**, not its legal label. The defining test of a financial liability is the existence of a **contractual obligation to deliver cash or another financial asset** that the issuer cannot unconditionally avoid. An equity instrument, by contrast, is a residual interest carrying **no such obligation**. A share that the holder can put back to the issuer for cash therefore contains a liability, because the issuer has an obligation to transfer cash it cannot escape. [CONFIRMED FACT — IAS 32; IFRIC 2 Basis for Conclusions.]

### Why a redeemable member share is prima facie a liability

Applying that test, IFRIC 2 states the rule bluntly: *"if a member of a cooperative entity has the right to request redemption of the member's shares in the cooperative, the cooperative normally classifies those shares as a liability."* The member's contractual right to demand par is the issuer's unavoidable obligation to pay it. The IFRIC (now IFRS Interpretations Committee) **explicitly rejected** the argument, pressed by cooperative respondents, that classification should incorporate the *probability* that members will actually request redemption — commentators observed that redemption experience is typically 1–5% and argued it was absurd to classify 100% of shares as liabilities on the behaviour of 1%. The IFRIC did not accept those views: classification under IAS 32 turns on the *terms* of the instrument, not the statistics of holder behaviour. [CONFIRMED FACT — IFRIC 2 BC and text.]

### The two escape routes

IFRIC 2 provides exactly two routes to equity classification for a member share that would otherwise be a liability:

1. **Unconditional right to refuse redemption.** If the entity has an unconditional right to refuse redemption of the shares, they are equity. The right must be unconditional: a right to refuse only if some condition is met (e.g., a liquidity test) does *not* qualify.
2. **Unconditional legal, regulatory or charter prohibition.** If local law, regulation, or the entity's governing charter *unconditionally* prohibits redemption — absolutely, or partially below a specified floor — the prohibited amount is equity.

The text is precise on partial prohibitions: *"An unconditional prohibition may be partial, in that it prohibits redemption of members' shares if redemption would cause the number of members' shares or amount of paid-in capital from members' shares to fall below a specified level."* Shares **above** that floor remain liabilities unless the entity has the unconditional right to refuse. And prohibitions that bite only on conditions — *"provisions … that prohibit redemption only if conditions—such as liquidity constraints—are met (or are not met) do not result in members' shares being equity."* [CONFIRMED FACT.]

### Worked application (following IFRIC 2's own illustrative example)

Suppose a cooperative has 100,000 shares of nominal CU10 (CU1,000,000 paid-in) and its charter prohibits redemption below CU200,000. Then CU200,000 is equity (the unconditionally-prohibited floor) and CU800,000 is a liability. Now the cooperative issues additional shares at a higher price (the maximum redemption amount rises). IFRIC 2's own arithmetic in its illustrative example: the additional issue creates a new liability measured at fair value; the entity ends with CU800,000 of financial liability and CU2,200,000 as equity in the worked example — the mechanical point being that **the liability/equity split moves every time the redemption exposure changes, and the liability is remeasured, with the change running through the accounts.** [CONFIRMED FACT — IFRIC 2 illustrative example.]

### Practical consequence for the balance sheet — and for the reader

Three consequences follow, each material:

- **A cooperative that leaves member shares fully redeemable reports little or no equity.** Its balance sheet shows members' capital as debt. To a depositor, counterparty, regulator, or auditor reading the statements, the institution looks thinly capitalised even if it is economically sound — because the accounting faithfully represents that the "capital" can walk out the door.
- **Remeasurement volatility.** Because the liability is remeasured as redemption exposure and share value change, reported profit and equity can swing for reasons unrelated to operating performance.
- **The escape requires a charter amendment that curtails the member's right.** There is no accounting trick; equity classification is *earned* by writing an unconditional refusal right or an unconditional redemption floor into the bye-laws or statute. That is the redemption paradox appearing on the balance sheet.

### Non-IFRS jurisdictions

Both **Nigeria** and **Kenya** are full-IFRS jurisdictions (Nigeria adopted IFRS from 2012; Kenya has used IFRS since 1999), so IFRIC 2 applies to their cooperatives' financial statements exactly as described. **This matters enormously for the reader:** a Nigerian cooperative fintech that leaves shares redeemable will present accounts showing member capital as a liability, and any prudential regulator, correspondent bank, or remittance partner reading those accounts will treat it accordingly. The **United States** credit-union system is not on IFRS but reaches the same destination by a different statutory road: net worth is defined as the retained-earnings balance under GAAP (§216(o) FCUA, II.4), so member "shares" — being withdrawable deposits — are simply never counted as net worth in the first place. [CONFIRMED FACT.]

## II.4 The prudential frame — CRR Article 29 and its analogues

The accounting frame asks *is it equity?* The prudential frame asks a stricter question: *is it capital of a quality the supervisor will let you count against your requirement?* The answers diverge, and the divergence is the engineering space.

### The EU: CRR Articles 27–29 and Delegated Regulation 241/2014

CRR Article 27 brings capital instruments of mutuals, cooperative societies, and savings institutions within the scope of CET1. Article 28 sets the general CET1 conditions (perpetual, no obligation to distribute, loss-absorbing, most-subordinated, etc.). Article 29 then sets the **cooperative-specific redemption conditions**: the instrument qualifies as CET1 only if *"except where prohibited under applicable national law, the institution shall be able to refuse the redemption of the instruments,"* or, where national law prohibits refusal, *"the provisions governing the instruments shall give the institution the ability to limit their redemption."* Crucially, *"refusal to redeem … or the limitation of the redemption … may not constitute an event of default."* Article 29 also caps distributions to what national law or the statute allows and limits owners' claims on reserves in insolvency to the nominal value. [CONFIRMED FACT — CRR Art. 29.]

The EBA RTS (Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 241/2014), Articles 7–11, specify the "ability to limit redemption": per Article 10, it *"shall encompass both the right to defer the redemption and the right to limit the amount to be redeemed,"* and the institution *"shall be able to defer the redemption or limit the amount to be redeemed for an unlimited period of time."* So the qualifying cooperative must be able to say "no," or at least "not now, indefinitely." [CONFIRMED FACT.]

### The redemption-permission regime and the 2% cap

Article 78 CRR (with Article 77) requires **prior supervisory permission** to reduce, redeem, or repurchase CET1 instruments. Article 32 of the RTS operationalises this for cooperatives: the competent authority may grant *advance* permission to redeem, but the redemptions are capped at a **predetermined amount** — the commonly-cited ceiling being on the order of **2% of CET1** — with anything beyond requiring case-by-case approval. The mechanism means a cooperative bank in practice obtains a standing permission to honour a small, predictable volume of member exits each year and must return to the supervisor for anything larger. [CONFIRMED FACT (permission architecture, CRR Arts 77–78; RTS Art. 32); the 2%-of-CET1 advance ceiling carried forward from the Prologue — SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS / regulator practice.]

**A flagged tension.** The Basel Committee's Regulatory Consistency Assessment of the EU (BIS, d300) recorded that its assessment team considered the CRR concessions for mutuals *"go beyond the permissible flexibility in the Basel standard,"* worrying that regulatory approval and deferral rights "do not … mitigate the public perception that these instruments are redeemable." **This is CONTESTED IN THE LITERATURE**: the EU treats supervised, deferrable member shares as CET1; the Basel assessors regarded that as a stretch. The reader should note that the "CET1 member share" is a European legal construction not universally accepted as best practice. [CONFIRMED FACT that the disagreement exists; CONTESTED as to who is right.]

### United States: net worth = retained earnings

The Federal Credit Union Act §216(o) defines "net worth" as the **retained-earnings balance** of the credit union under GAAP. Member "shares" are functionally withdrawable deposits and never count. The regime is a **leverage-based net-worth ratio** (7% for "well capitalised" under prompt corrective action), supplemented since 1 January 2022 by the **risk-based capital (RBC) rule** and the alternative **Complex Credit Union Leverage Ratio (CCULR)** for "complex" credit unions (those with total assets greater than $500 million). A qualifying complex credit union that opts into CCULR and maintains the minimum net-worth ratio — **9% initially, rising to 10%** — is deemed well capitalised without computing an RBC ratio. [CONFIRMED FACT — 12 CFR Part 702; Federal Register, 23 Dec 2021.]

Because US net worth is *only* retained earnings, US credit unions historically had **no** way to raise regulatory capital externally — the growth arithmetic of II.2 in its purest form. The **2022 subordinated-debt rule** (12 CFR Part 702 Subpart D; final rule Dec 2020, effective 1 Jan 2022) opened a narrow door (II.6, item 7): it lets low-income-designated credit unions (LICUs), complex credit unions, and new credit unions issue subordinated debt, but this debt counts toward the **RBC numerator** for complex CUs and toward **net worth only for LICUs** — for a non-LICU complex credit union it does *not* raise the headline net-worth ratio. [CONFIRMED FACT.]

### Kenya: SASRA core capital and institutional capital

The SACCO Societies Act 2008 and the Deposit-Taking Regulations 2010 (LN 95/2010) impose, for DT-SACCOs: **core capital of at least KSh 10 million**; **core capital ≥ 10% of total assets**; **core capital ≥ 8% of total deposits**; and **at least 50% of core capital** to consist of retained earnings and disclosed reserves (i.e., institutional capital, not member shares). This last ratio is the analytically important one: it is Kenya's statutory acknowledgement that member shares alone are not enough — half of core capital must be the permanent, non-refundable kind. SASRA's 2024 report shows the sector comfortably above minima in aggregate (capital-to-assets 17.28%; institutional capital to assets 11.97%), but with 30 SACCOs on heightened supervision. The newer non-withdrawable-deposit-taking (NWDT) segment recorded core-capital-to-assets of 10.87% against an 8% minimum. [CONFIRMED FACT.]

### Canada/Quebec: AMF treatment of parts permanentes and Desjardins

Desjardins is supervised by Québec's **Autorité des marchés financiers (AMF)** under the *Loi sur les coopératives de services financiers* (RLRQ c. C-67.3), and was designated a domestic systemically important financial institution by the AMF in June 2013. The instructive episode: Desjardins' **old "permanent shares"** (parts permanentes), sold to members from the late 1980s, "could be considered as capital for regulatory purposes" — but because they *"weren't actually permanent — they could be bought back at certain times — they no longer counted under Basel III."* Desjardins therefore engineered a **non-refundable, Basel III-compliant** capital-share product (the "parts de capital F"), described in the Quebec press as *"subordonnées et non rachetables"* — subordinated and non-redeemable — designed with the AMF over roughly 18 months. **This is the redemption paradox playing out in real time inside one institution: the redeemable instrument failed the prudential test and had to be replaced with a non-redeemable one.** [CONFIRMED FACT — Globe and Mail; La Presse.]

### Nigeria: there is no prudential capital regime for cooperatives

This must be stated plainly because it is the hinge of II.8. **A cooperative society registered under the Nigerian Co-operative Societies Act 1993 (or its state analogues) is subject to no prudential capital regime whatsoever.** There is no minimum capital ratio, no CET1 concept, no supervisory redemption-permission regime, no leverage limit. The Act contemplates shares (with the 20% cap of §27) and member deposits (§19) but imposes no capital adequacy. The only capital "rules" are whatever the bye-laws say (§11). **If, and only if, the entity becomes CBN-licensed** under BOFIA 2020 — for example as a microfinance bank or a payment service bank, or by operating a licensed activity such as a Mobile Money Operator — does a hard capital wall appear: the paid-up capital thresholds of the CBN's 2020 MFB guidelines (Unit ₦20m, State ₦1bn, National ₦5bn) or the relevant fintech-category thresholds. The transition from "no regime" to "₦5 billion wall" is discontinuous and is the defining strategic fact for the reader. [CONFIRMED FACT — NCSA 1993; BOFIA 2020; CBN 2020 guidelines.]

## II.5 Retained earnings and indivisible reserves as capital strategy

### Retained earnings: the primary reliable source

Retained earnings (accumulated surplus not distributed) are the cooperative's best capital for three reasons: they are **permanent** (no maturity, no holder who can demand them back), **unambiguously CET1 / net worth / core capital** in every regime surveyed (they are the paradigm of the residual), and **cheap** (no coupon, no dividend, no issuance cost). Every regime privileges them: US net worth *is* retained earnings; Kenya requires ≥50% of core capital to be retained earnings and disclosed reserves; CRR treats reserves as the cleanest CET1.

Their limits are equally clear and follow directly from II.1–II.2: they accumulate **slowly** (bounded by b × ROE), and members may **not want to build them** — the horizon and free-rider problems mean a rational member prefers a patronage refund or dividend today to a reserve she can never individually claim. Retained earnings solve the *quality* problem perfectly and the *quantity* problem only at the speed the growth arithmetic allows. [SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS — Cook 1995; Chaddad & Cook 2004.]

### Indivisible reserves: the deliberate legal solution

An **indivisible reserve** is accumulated surplus that is locked by statute or charter so that no member — present or departing — and in the strongest form no future dissolution distribution to members either — can ever claim it. This does two things at once: it manufactures **permanent CET1-quality capital** (there is no holder with a redemption right, so IFRIC 2's equity test and CRR's permanence test are met automatically), and it operates as an **anti-demutualisation asset lock** (there is no accumulated value to capture by converting, blunting the demutualisation incentive — the link to Part I's master variable).

**France (the paradigm).** Loi n° 47-1775 du 10 septembre 1947, Article 16, requires that available surplus after legal-reserve allocations be placed in reserve or granted to other cooperatives or general-interest works, and provides that *"tant que les diverses réserves totalisées n'atteignent pas le montant du capital social, le prélèvement opéré à leur profit ne peut être inférieur aux trois vingtièmes des excédents d'exploitation"* — i.e., a **mandatory 15% (3/20) of operating surplus to reserves** until reserves equal share capital. Article 19 makes surplus *"prioritairement mis en réserve pour assurer son développement"* — reserves come first, subject to Article 16. On dissolution, net assets after repayment of paid-in capital are devolved (Article 19, as amended) to other cooperatives or social-economy enterprises — *dévolution désintéressée*, the disinterested wind-up. [CONFIRMED FACT — Légifrance.]

**Italy (the paradigm partner).** Civil Code Article 2514 imposes the mutualistic constraints (limits on distributions and on remuneration of capital, and devolution of residual assets to mutualistic funds). Law No. 59 of 1992 created the **3%-of-net-profits contribution to the mutual-promotion funds** (e.g., Coopfond). And, decisively for cooperative *banks*, **TUB Article 37** (the 1993 Banking Law) requires that *"Banche di credito cooperativo must allocate at least seventy per cent of net profits for the year to the legal reserve"* — a **≥70% mandatory retention** into an indivisible legal reserve. This is the most aggressive statutory capital-building rule in the survey. [CONFIRMED FACT — BIS working paper quoting TUB Art. 37; Civil Code Art. 2514.]

**Quebec.** C-67.3 Article 84 sets the surplus-allocation order — first the Article 87 reserve, then the *réserve générale*, then interest on capital shares, then a stabilisation reserve, then a reserve for eventual ristournes, then ristournes to members — and Article 85 makes the general reserve non-distributable and un-shareable among members (*"ne peut … être partagée entre ses membres"*), with *dévolution désintéressée* on liquidation. Quebec is the strongest asset-lock jurisdiction in the survey (Part I finding, verified). [CONFIRMED FACT — C-67.3.]

### The trade-off, stated precisely

The indivisible reserve buys permanent capital and demutualisation-resistance at the cost of a **weakened member incentive to build it**: a reserve nobody can ever claim is, from the individual member's horizon-limited perspective, a tax on current surplus with no personal payoff. The French and Italian systems answer this not by persuasion but by **compulsion** — the 15% and 70% rules remove the decision from members' hands. Compulsion is the mechanism; the member's reluctance is precisely why compulsion is necessary. This is the property-rights critique's horizon problem being neutralised by statute rather than allowed to bind. [ANALYTICAL INFERENCE.]

### Empirical test: do indivisible-reserve jurisdictions accumulate more capital?

The commission requires this to be answered with evidence or flagged. The evidence is **suggestive but not clean**, so I classify it carefully:

- Italian BCCs, subject to the 70% rule, are repeatedly found to have **solvency ratios similar to or higher than the whole banking system** — an IMF working paper (2008) records that *"Solvency ratios are similar—or higher in the case of BCCs—to those of the whole system."* This is consistent with the hypothesis that mandatory indivisible-reserve building produces strong capital. [CONFIRMED FACT (the ratios); ANALYTICAL INFERENCE (attribution to the rule).]
- However, BCCs are also **lower-profitability**, and this depresses ROE (and hence g\*); the reform literature (IMF 2008) argues the governance framework "may hamper raising capital, particularly at time of distress." So the indivisible reserve produces *quality* and *durability* but not necessarily *growth capacity* — indeed it may constrain it, because with distributions suppressed and no external equity, the bank lives entirely on b × ROE with a low ROE.
- A rigorous *causal* comparison — indivisible-reserve versus divisible-reserve cooperatives, holding profitability and market structure constant — **I could not locate.** The confounders (Italian BCCs are small, local, relationship lenders; German Volksbanken have no asset lock but do have the IPS) make a clean natural experiment elusive.

**Verdict:** The claim that indivisible-reserve jurisdictions accumulate *higher-quality and more durable* capital is well supported. The stronger claim that they accumulate *more* capital in absolute or growth terms is **UNKNOWN / not established by clean evidence**, and there is a live counter-mechanism (suppressed distributions with no equity access can cap growth). I flag it as such rather than papering it over. Note the sharp contrast carried from Part I: **Germany, with no statutory asset lock at all, is among the most durable cooperative-banking systems in the world** — which by itself shows the asset lock is *sufficient but not necessary* for durability, and points to the institutional-protection-scheme mechanism (II.6, item 11) as the German substitute. [SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS for the contrast; UNKNOWN for the strong empirical claim.]

## II.6 The instrument catalogue

Each instrument is dissected on the same nine-point template — (a) legal character and governing law; (b) who may issue / who may hold; (c) redeemability and permanence; (d) IFRIC 2 / IAS 32 classification; (e) regulatory capital treatment; (f) cost in money; (g) cost in control; (h) real-world example with figures; (i) availability to a Nigerian cooperative — so that Appendix B can tabulate it cleanly.

### Instrument 1 — The ordinary member share (the baseline)

**(a)** Subscribed, par-value ownership stake in a cooperative society; governed by the general cooperative statute (GenG in Germany, NCSA 1993 in Nigeria, the SACCO/Co-operative Societies Acts in Kenya, etc.). **(b)** Issued by the cooperative to its members; held only by members; typically one-member-one-vote regardless of holding. **(c)** Redeemable at or near par on exit — the defining fragility. **(d)** Prima facie a **financial liability** under IFRIC 2 unless the entity has an unconditional right to refuse redemption or an unconditional prohibition floor exists. **(e)** CET1 only to the extent redemption can be refused/limited (CRR Art. 29); in the US, *never* net worth (it is a deposit). **(f)** Cost in money: dividend/interest on shares where permitted (often capped by statute). **(g)** Cost in control: none beyond the standard one-member-one-vote — the baseline against which every other instrument's control cost is measured. **(h)** Universal; e.g., a Nigerian cooperative society's ₦-denominated shares under §27 NCSA (20% cap). **(i)** Fully available in Nigeria — indeed it is the *only* fully available instrument. [CONFIRMED FACT.]

### Instrument 2 — Non-withdrawable member shares (the Kenyan model)

**(a)** A member share that is **non-withdrawable** — exit is by transfer or sale to another member, not by redemption from the society; governed by the SACCO Societies Act 2008 / Co-operative Societies Act and the DT regulations. **(b)** Issued by the SACCO; held by members; a minimum threshold is typically required (e.g., a KSh-denominated minimum share-capital requirement). **(c)** Non-redeemable by the member against the society: *"Share capital is non-withdrawable … members may opt to retain shares after withdrawing their deposit or choose to sell/transfer to an existing member."* Liquidity is provided by a secondary transfer market (some SACCOs run a "share transfer fund"). **(d)** Because the *member* has no right to demand cash from the *entity*, this is much closer to equity under IFRIC 2 — the redemption obligation is absent; the transfer is between members. **(e)** Counts as core capital in Kenya; would be strong CET1 in the EU. **(f)** Dividend on shares (some Kenyan SACCOs advertise double-digit share dividends in strong years — treat federation/marketing figures with caution). **(g)** Control cost: none — still member shares, still one-member-one-vote. **(h)** Kenyan DT-SACCOs universally; share capital non-refundable, deposits (BOSA) refundable on exit. **(i)** *Potentially* synthesisable in Nigeria by bye-law (II.8) — this is the single most important instrument for the reader to study, because it converts the ordinary share into quasi-permanent capital **at zero control cost** simply by removing the redemption right and substituting a transfer market. [CONFIRMED FACT.]

### Instrument 3 — Permanent shares (Quebec's *parts permanentes*)

**(a)** Capital shares issued by Desjardins caisses/Federation under C-67.3; the older *parts permanentes* were converted into *parts de capital* by the 2000 Desjardins Act. **(b)** Issued by the caisse/Federation; held by members. **(c)** Here is the dissection Part I flagged: the **old** parts permanentes were redeemable at the *member's* request after a holding period (e.g., five years, from a pre-retirement at 60 or from 65) but *"la Fédération n'est jamais tenue d'acquérir des parts permanentes"* — the issuer is never obliged to buy them. They were thus member-requestable but issuer-discretionary. The **new** parts de capital F carry *"aucun droit de rachat"* — no redemption right at all — and are redeemable only with AMF authorisation and essentially only in liquidation/insolvency/dissolution or once base-capital adequacy is met. **(d)** The old redeemable version sat awkwardly under equity tests; the new non-redeemable version is cleanly equity. **(e)** The decisive fact: the **old parts permanentes failed Basel III** ("could be bought back at certain times … no longer counted under Basel III"); Desjardins engineered the **new, non-refundable, subordinated parts de capital** specifically to qualify. **(f)** Interest capped (the 2009 parts permanentes paid ~4.25%; the 2012 parts F carried an interest rate capped at 4.25% or the 5-year Canada bond yield). **(g)** Control cost: limited — capital shares generally carry restricted or no voting at the caisse governance level; participation is economic, not controlling. **(h)** The 2012 parts de capital F issue targeted **C$1.2 billion** and passed the **C$1 billion** mark; nominal **C$10** per share, minimum subscription C$100; since 1989 Desjardins had raised roughly **C$2 billion** across ten permanent-share issues, and cumulatively **C$3.3 billion** in member-acquired shares. **(i)** Not directly available in Nigeria (no equivalent statute); could be *approximated* only by a contractual non-redeemable subordinated instrument (II.8). [CONFIRMED FACT — Globe and Mail; La Presse.]

### Instrument 4 — Cooperative preference shares (*parts privilégiées* and analogues)

**(a)** Non-voting (or restricted-voting) participation shares carrying a preferential economic return; Quebec *parts privilégiées*, and analogues such as CoBank's cumulative preferred stock in the US. **(b)** Issued by the cooperative; may in some regimes be held by non-members/investors. **(c)** Terms vary; can be engineered non-redeemable (CoBank's preferred was described as *"a non-voting, fixed dividend, non-redeemable ownership right"*). **(d)** Non-redeemable, discretionary-dividend preference shares are equity; redeemable/mandatory-dividend ones are liabilities. **(e)** Non-redeemable non-cumulative preference shares can reach AT1; cumulative or dated ones fall to Tier 2. **(f)** Fixed or preferential dividend — more expensive than ordinary shares, cheaper than common-equivalent. **(g)** Control cost: low by design — non-voting is the point; but preferential claims on surplus dilute members' residual economics. **(h)** CoBank privately placed **US$300 million** of cumulative preferred stock in June 2001. **(i)** In Nigeria, the NCSA share architecture is not obviously built for non-voting investor preference shares; approximation would be contractual and weak (II.8). [CONFIRMED FACT — Chaddad & Cook.]

### Instrument 5 — Rabobank Certificates (the paradigm permanent capital instrument)

**(a)** Certificates of participation in Rabobank's own capital, issued through a foundation (Stichting AK Rabobank Certificaten) and governed by Rabobank's articles of association and the Certificates Rules; **the paradigm case of a member-only participation right engineered into a listed perpetual CET1 security.** **(b)** Issued by Coöperatieve Rabobank U.A.; originally held by members, now **listed on Euronext Amsterdam (since 27 January 2014)** and held by institutional and retail investors. **(c)** **Perpetual** and **non-redeemable by the holder**; Rabobank has *"full discretion to cancel these certificates at any time and/or to sell them in the future."* **(d)** No holder redemption right ⇒ equity-like; **(e)** qualifies as **CET1** — the deepest, most subordinated capital Rabobank issues. **(f)** Distribution is **fully discretionary**; per Rabobank's official disclosure, *"Rabobank intends to pay distributions equal to the yield on the most recent 10-year Dutch state loan + 1.5%-point annually with a minimum of 6.5% annually,"* paid quarterly (€0.40 per quarter on the €25 nominal). **(g)** Control cost: **near zero** — this is the instrument's genius. Certificate-holders have **no governance rights** in the cooperative; the STAK holder-meeting structure was wound down (administration terminated by holder vote in June 2025), and the cooperative's member-controlled governance is untouched. Investors supply loss-absorbing capital and receive a discretionary yield, but **no control.** **(h)** Nominal **€25**; **313,005,461 certificates issued**, of which Rabobank held **36,652,880** after a 2024 tender; **~€7.8 billion** outstanding nominal. Per Rabobank's press release *"Rabobank increases capital buffers by issuing EUR 1.5 billion new Rabobank Certificates,"* the January 2017 tap issued 60 million new certificates at 108% of the €25 nominal (nominal issued amount €1.5 billion), with the book *"2.4 times oversubscribed"*; trading commenced 24 January 2017. **(i)** Not directly available in Nigeria (requires a securities-issuance and listing apparatus and a cooperative statute permitting perpetual participation certificates); the *design principle* — capital without control via a discretionary-distribution perpetual — is the most important lesson for the reader, but synthesising it in Nigeria today is not realistic (II.8). [CONFIRMED FACT.]

### Instrument 6 — Subordinated member deposits (incl. the Kenyan BOSA structure)

**(a)** A term deposit or subordinated loan from a member, ranking below ordinary creditors but **not** an ownership stake; governed by deposit law and the bye-laws. **(b)** Issued (borrowed) by the cooperative; held by members. **(c)** Refundable on exit / at maturity — **this is the fragility.** The Kenyan BOSA non-withdrawable deposit is the exemplar: funds are *"non-withdrawable"* during membership but *"refundable upon termination of membership … waiting 60 days for the refund,"* and members recover *"100% of your savings"* on exit. **(d)** A deposit is a **financial liability** under IAS 32 — IFRIC 2 is explicit that *"demand deposits … and similar contracts that arise when members act as customers are financial liabilities."* **(e)** Generally **not** regulatory capital (it is a deposit); in Kenya BOSA non-withdrawable deposits function as **quasi-capital economically** (long-dated, stable, loan-collateralising) but are **legally deposits, refundable on exit** — a genuine fragility, because in a stress the exit wave converts "quasi-capital" into a liability run. **(f)** Interest/rebate on deposits. **(g)** Control cost: none. **(h)** Kenyan BOSA deposits underpin the sector's ~KSh 749 billion deposit base (SASRA 2024). **(i)** **Available in Nigeria** — §19 NCSA contemplates member deposits, and §16 gives the society a charge and set-off over shares, interest and deposits. This is the reader's most *accessible* quasi-capital tool, but its legal character is a refundable liability, so it is the weakest form of "capital" and must not be relied on for loss absorption. [CONFIRMED FACT.]

### Instrument 7 — Subordinated debt and secondary capital (NCUA Subpart D)

**(a)** Subordinated debt notes issued by credit unions; governed by **12 CFR Part 702 Subpart D** (final rule Dec 2020, effective 1 Jan 2022), which also governs "Grandfathered Secondary Capital." **(b)** Issuable only by **low-income-designated credit unions (LICUs), complex credit unions, and new credit unions**; held by qualified investors (institutional/accredited) — for federally insured state-chartered CUs, only to the extent state law permits. **(c)** Dated (minimum and maximum maturities apply; the 2020 rule set bounds, later proposals extended grandfathered-secondary-capital treatment horizons). Not redeemable at holder's option; NCUA approval required to prepay. **(d)** Debt — a financial liability. **(e)** Counts as **Regulatory Capital in the RBC numerator** for complex CUs; counts toward **net worth only for LICUs** — for a non-LICU it does not lift the headline net-worth ratio. Any "secondary capital" issued after 1 Jan 2022 is Subordinated Debt under the new subpart. **(f)** Coupon — market subordinated-debt pricing. Per Catalyst Corporate FCU (September 2025), *"issuing credit unions are usually greater than $1 billion in total assets with a Net Worth Ratio of approximately 8.7 percent… On average, credit unions issue up to 20 percent of their current Net Worth."* **(g)** Control cost: **none** — debt carries no votes; this is why it is attractive to a member-controlled entity. **(h)** Uptake per Catalyst Corporate FCU: *"As of March 31, 2025, 167 credit unions had $4.1 billion in outstanding sub-debt… in 2016, when 70 credit unions had only $182 million outstanding"* (154 credit unions / $3.4 billion by end-2022 per the intervening data). **(i)** Not available to a Nigerian cooperative society (no analogous rule); a private subordinated loan from investors could be contracted, but without a prudential regime it confers no "capital" status — it is simply subordinated borrowing (II.8). [CONFIRMED FACT.]

### Instrument 8 — A listed vehicle over a mutual base (Crédit Agricole / CASA) — and the IPO figure resolved

**(a)** A joint-stock holding company (Crédit Agricole S.A.), listed on Euronext Paris, sitting **over** a cooperative base and controlled by it; governed by French company and banking law plus the cooperative statutes of the base. **(b)** Listed shares held by the public; **control retained by the cooperative base**: ~2,400 Caisses Locales own the (now 39) Caisses Régionales, which control CASA through **SAS Rue La Boétie holding ≥50%**. **(c)** Listed shares are freely tradable and non-redeemable (ordinary equity); the *base* remains cooperative and member-redeemable at its own level. **(d)/(e)** Listed CASA equity is ordinary share capital / CET1 at the listed-entity level. **(f)** Cost in money: an ordinary-equity cost of capital and a public dividend. **(g)** Cost in control: **preserved by legal engineering** — the ≥50% SAS Rue La Boétie holding means outside shareholders fund the balance sheet but cannot capture control. This is the "listed vehicle over a mutual base" archetype. **(h) — the open item resolved.** The commission required pinning the 2001 IPO proceeds against a primary filing. **Resolution:** the CASA IPO priced at the **top of its €14.30–€16.60 range, at €16.60 per share** (COB visa n° 01-1434, dated 13 December 2001; first trade 14 December 2001). Decisively, the offering was **predominantly a *secondary* sell-down, not a capital raise for the group**: the core float of **177,680,000 existing shares** was sold by the 48 Caisses Régionales and an FCPR — those proceeds (≈177.68m × €16.60 ≈ **€2.95 billion**) went to the *selling Regional Banks*, not to CASA. The only genuinely **new** CASA equity was the employee-reserved tranche of up to **18,367,351 new shares at €13.28** (≈**€244 million**), plus an over-allotment of up to 26,653,100 existing shares (≈€442m). Total offering size ≈ **€3.2–3.6 billion**, which reconciles the contemporary "≈€3.5 billion" reporting (Institutional Investor) and the "up to €4 billion" (UPI). **The later "≈€4.8bn / $5bn" summaries are not supported by the 2001 prospectus** and appear to conflate the later 2003 rights issue (**€1.97 billion**, 122,793,536 new shares at €16.07) and the 2007 rights issue (**€4 billion**). **Analytical bottom line for the reader:** the famous "mutualist IPO" raised very little *new* capital for the group in 2001 — its function was to create a listed currency (for the Crédit Lyonnais acquisition) and a mechanism, not to inject equity. The equity injection came later, through rights issues *on the listed vehicle*. That is the instrument's real lesson: **listing is a durable capital-raising machine, not a one-off event, and the control lock (SAS Rue La Boétie ≥50%) is what makes repeated raising safe.** **(i)** Wholly unavailable to a Nigerian cooperative today (requires a listable holdco, a securities-market apparatus, and a two-tier cooperative-over-listed structure); it is the archetype the reader might aspire to at large scale, not a near-term tool. [CONFIRMED FACT — COB visa 01-1434.]

*Dutch analogue:* Rabobank chose the *certificate* route (Instrument 5) rather than a listed holdco, precisely to avoid creating a controlling outside shareholder class — a revealing contrast in engineering choices toward the same end (external CET1 without control transfer).

### Instrument 9 — Retained patronage and revolving funds (US agricultural model)

**(a)** A mechanism converting patronage (use of the cooperative) into equity: the cooperative allocates a portion of net earnings to members as **retained patronage refunds** (non-cash), held in the member's name, and later **revolves** (redeems) the oldest equity for cash on a cycle; governed by cooperative statutes and US tax law (qualified vs non-qualified allocations). **(b)** Issued by the cooperative to patron-members. **(c)** *Revolving* — semi-permanent: redeemed on a cycle (e.g., an age-of-stock plan revolving over 10–15 years, a percentage-of-all-equities plan, an age-of-patron plan redeeming at, e.g., 75–78, or a base-capital plan matching over/under-invested patrons). So it is neither instantly redeemable nor truly permanent. **(d)** Allocated, revolving equity that the cooperative is expected (but often not legally obliged) to redeem sits on the equity/liability boundary; **qualified** allocations pass tax to the member currently, **non-qualified** defer it (and give the cooperative a tax deduction on later redemption). **(e)** Allocated equity is core capital while outstanding. **(f)** Cost in money: the tax the member bears on non-cash allocations (qualified) — the member pays tax on income received as illiquid equity; and the eventual cash redemption. **(g)** Control cost: none — patron-members retain control; but there is an **intergenerational-equity cost**: current users fund the cooperative and are repaid later, tying capital to patronage. **(h)** The base-capital plan is designed so that *"redemptions to overinvested patrons are matched with additional investments by underinvested patrons,"* minimising net redemption budget. **(i)** In Nigeria, patronage-refund-to-equity is contractually constructible under the bye-laws (the NCSA contemplates patronage-style distributions), but without the US tax scaffolding the qualified/non-qualified distinction is moot; a Nigerian cooperative fintech's "patronage" is thin in any case (it is a saver/borrower relationship, not a marketing throughput). Marginal relevance for the reader. [CONFIRMED FACT — USDA/OSU cooperative-finance literature.]

### Instrument 10 — Investor or non-user members (German 2006 reform and analogues)

**(a)** Statutory admission of **"investing members" (investierende Mitglieder)** who join a cooperative to supply capital without using its services; Germany's **2006 GenG reform** (in force 18 August 2006) introduced this (§§8, 8(2), 16(2) GenG), alongside optional minimum capital (§8a). **(b)** Admitted by statute-permitting cooperatives; held by non-user investors. **(c)** Governed by the cooperative's exit rules; the 2006 reform also let cooperatives **delay repayment of departing members' contributions** if repayment would breach the statutory minimum capital (§8a(2)) — a redemption-limitation feature that helps equity classification. **(d)** To the extent redemption can be deferred/limited, closer to equity. **(e)** Contributes to own funds subject to the usual CRR conditions. **(f)** Investing members receive a return on capital, but the GenG binds them to the promotion purpose and **denies them a profit-priority over user-members**. **(g)** Control cost: **capped by design and this is the crucial safeguard** — §8(2) GenG limits investing members' presence on the supervisory board to **no more than one quarter**, and the reform preserves user-member primacy; but scholars (Kling & Cobe, *ZfgG* 2016) note the possibility remains *"controversial"* and raises questions about whether voting restrictions on investing members are even constitutionally sound. **(h)** German cooperative practice; capital raised is modest relative to needs — it is a supplement, not a solution. **(i)** In Nigeria, the NCSA's **20% shareholding cap (§27)** and the general member-user conception make a dedicated investor-member class hard to construct; a cooperative could admit investors as members but each is capped at 20%, and control (one-member-one-vote) is unaffected — meaning investor money is bounded and control-safe but *small* (II.8). [CONFIRMED FACT — GenG 2006; Kling & Cobe.]

### Instrument 11 — Institutional protection schemes as a capital substitute (BVR / CRR Art. 113(7))

**(a)** A **contractual or statutory liability arrangement** among a network of cooperative banks that *"protects those institutions and in particular ensures their liquidity and solvency to avoid bankruptcy where necessary"* — the definition in **CRR Article 113(7)**. **(b)** Operated by the network body; members are the network's autonomous banks (Germany's **BVR-ISG**, operating an approved IPS since 1 July 2015 for about 849 CRR credit institutions as at end-2019). **(c)/(d)** Not an instrument on any single bank's balance sheet — it is a **mutualised commitment**. **(e)** Its regulatory effect is twofold: members may apply a **0% risk weight** to intra-network exposures (Art. 113(7)), and the network reports on a **consolidated / extended-aggregated basis** (Art. 49(3)), so capital is assessed across the network rather than bank-by-bank. **(f)** Cost in money: ex-ante contributions to the scheme's funds plus the contingent mutual liability. **(g)** Control cost: **none in the investor sense** — no outside equity, no votes transferred; control stays with members. The "cost" is the mutual obligation to rescue a failing peer. **(h)** IPSs exist in Austria, Germany, Italy, Poland and Spain; the German Cooperative Financial Network's own funds "arise primarily from the profits generated, and in most cases retained, by the cooperative banks" — i.e., the IPS lets a system of retained-earnings-funded banks operate as if better-capitalised because solvency is mutualised. **(i)** Directly relevant and *partially constructible* in Nigeria: a **secondary/apex cooperative** or a contractual cross-guarantee among a group of cooperatives could approximate a mini-IPS (II.8), though without regulatory recognition it yields no risk-weight or consolidation benefit — only the economic pooling. **Assessment:** the Prologue identified mutualised solvency support as central to European cooperative durability, and the evidence supports treating the IPS as *the* mechanism by which Germany achieves world-class durability **without any statutory asset lock**. Economically, the IPS functions as capital the individual institution does not have to hold on its own balance sheet — it is capital relocated to the network level. **This is the single most important idea in the Part for a founder who cannot raise permanent capital alone: pool it.** [CONFIRMED FACT — CRR Art. 113(7), Art. 49(3); BVR disclosures; European Parliament briefings.]

## II.7 The capital–control frontier

The eleven instruments are not a list; they are a **frontier**. Plot member control on one axis and access to permanent external capital on the other. The instruments arrange themselves along a curve:

- **Maximum control, minimum external capital:** the ordinary member share (1) and non-withdrawable member share (2). All control stays with one-member-one-vote members; external capital access is nil (shares are member-only, par-value). The non-withdrawable variant (2) is strictly superior to (1) because it converts the *same* member money into *permanent* capital **at no control cost** — it moves you *up* the capital axis without moving *down* the control axis. It is the one near-free lunch in the catalogue, and it is exactly the Kenyan innovation.
- **Rising external capital at modest control cost:** subordinated member deposits (6) and subordinated debt (7) — no votes transfer (control preserved) but they are *debt*, not permanent equity, so they raise loss-absorbing capacity only weakly (6) or only for defined issuers under a prudential rule (7). Retained patronage/revolving funds (9) sit near here: member capital, control-safe, but revolving rather than permanent.
- **Substantial external capital, control preserved by *design*:** cooperative preference shares (4), permanent shares (3), and above all **Rabobank Certificates (5)** — the frontier's cleverest point, delivering *listed, perpetual, CET1* capital with **near-zero governance transfer** because the certificate carries economics without control. Investor members (10) sit here too but with a *capped* control leakage (the one-quarter supervisory-board limit).
- **Maximum external capital, control preserved only by *legal engineering*:** the listed vehicle over a mutual base (8) — Crédit Agricole. Outside shareholders fund an entire listed balance sheet; control survives *only* because SAS Rue La Boétie holds ≥50%. Remove the engineering and control is gone.
- **Maximum external capital, control surrendered:** demutualisation — the frontier's endpoint and a failure mode (Prologue). Here the trade-off is not escaped; it is *paid in full*.

**Sitting the jurisdictions on the frontier.** Kenya's SACCOs cluster at the high-control/low-capital end, mitigated by the non-withdrawable share (2) and BOSA quasi-capital (6). US credit unions sit at high-control/low-capital, with the 2022 subordinated-debt door (7) cracked open only for LICUs/complex/new CUs. Germany sits at high-control but achieves *effective* high capital **off the individual balance sheet** via the IPS (11) — it is *off the frontier* in a sense, because it solves the capital problem at the network level rather than the instrument level. The Netherlands (Rabobank) reaches high external capital via certificates (5) with control intact. Quebec (Desjardins) uses permanent shares (3) engineered to Basel III. France (Crédit Agricole) sits at the extreme external-capital point held safe by legal engineering (8).

**The central analytical contribution — does anything escape the trade-off?** My defensible position: **No single instrument escapes it; they only price it differently — with two important qualifications.** The qualifications are the two mechanisms that genuinely bend the curve outward rather than sliding along it:

1. **The non-withdrawable share (2)** is a near-free lunch *because it does not add external capital at all* — it improves the *quality/permanence* of internal capital without touching control. It bends the curve on the *permanence* axis, not the *external-access* axis. It is the cheapest win available and should be the reader's first move.
2. **The institutional protection scheme (11)** genuinely relaxes the constraint, but **not at the level of the firm** — it relocates capital to the *network*. An individual bank need not hold, on its own balance sheet, capital it can draw on mutually. This is why Germany can be durable with neither an asset lock nor large external capital instruments. The IPS is the closest thing to a free lunch in the whole survey, and it is *cooperative in method* — it solves the cooperative capital problem cooperatively.

Everything else — certificates, preference shares, permanent shares, listed vehicles — buys external permanent capital by paying, somewhere, in economics (discretionary but expected yield), in residual dilution, or in the cost and fragility of the legal engineering that keeps control. Rabobank Certificates come closest to a firm-level escape, but even they pay: a targeted perpetual yield (the greater of the 10-year Dutch state loan yield + 1.5 points or a 6.5% floor) is a real, permanent cost of capital, and the "discretion" to skip it is nominal in a going concern that wants to issue again. **The trade-off is real; the art is in pricing it cheaply and keeping control, and the two cooperative-native mechanisms (non-withdrawable shares and mutualised protection) do this better than the market-facing instruments.** [ANALYTICAL INFERENCE — the Part's central argued conclusion.]

## II.8 What is actually available in Nigeria

This is the bridge to Parts V and VI. I audit each instrument against the Nigerian Co-operative Societies Act 1993 (and, where the entity is licensed, BOFIA 2020), and state adverse findings without softening.

**The governing baseline.** A cooperative society under the NCSA 1993 may issue member shares (subject to the **§27 one-fifth (20%) cap** on any one member's holding), take member deposits (§19), and holds a statutory **charge and set-off over a member's shares, interest and deposits (§16)**; shares are not liable to attachment by a member's creditors (§17); **withdrawability of shares is left to the bye-laws (§11(2)(g)–(i)) with no statutory rule**; and — decisively — **there is no prudential capital regime at all.**

**Instrument-by-instrument availability:**

1. **Ordinary member share — AVAILABLE.** The default. But redeemable per the bye-laws, hence prima facie an IFRIC 2 liability, hence poor capital.
2. **Non-withdrawable member share — AVAILABLE BY BYE-LAW, and this is the reader's single most valuable move.** Because §11 leaves withdrawability to the bye-laws with no statutory rule, a Nigerian cooperative can draft its bye-laws to make share capital **non-withdrawable, exit by transfer only** — replicating the Kenyan model. Done unconditionally, this is a plausible route to IFRIC 2 equity classification (the member loses the right to demand cash from the society). **Caveat and adverse finding:** the strength of this depends on the bye-law being an *unconditional* bar on redemption, not merely a discretionary or liquidity-conditioned one — IFRIC 2 is explicit that conditional prohibitions do not achieve equity. And a bye-law can be amended by the members, so unless the constitution entrenches the bar, its "unconditional" quality is contestable. Weaker than a statutory lock (France/Quebec), but far better than nothing, and available today.
3. **Permanent shares (parts permanentes) — NOT AVAILABLE as such;** only approximable via (2) plus a contractual non-redemption covenant. No Nigerian statute recognises a *parts permanentes* class, and no prudential regime exists to reward one. The approximation is materially weaker: it earns no regulatory capital credit (there is no regulator counting) and relies on bye-law entrenchment for its permanence.
4. **Cooperative preference shares — WEAKLY AVAILABLE.** A cooperative could create a preference/limited-participation share by bye-law, but the §27 20% cap and the one-member-one-vote norm constrain it, and there is no statutory non-voting-investor-share class to build on. Any such instrument is a contractual construct of uncertain enforceability.
5. **Rabobank-style listed certificate — NOT AVAILABLE.** Requires a securities-issuance/listing apparatus, a foundation-and-certificate legal structure, and a cooperative statute permitting perpetual participation certificates. None exists for a Nigerian cooperative society. Not synthesisable in any near-term horizon.
6. **Subordinated member deposits — AVAILABLE.** §19 contemplates member deposits and §16 gives the society a charge/set-off. A cooperative can take term or subordinated member deposits contractually. **Adverse finding:** these are *legally refundable liabilities*, not capital. They provide funding and weak going-concern loss buffer at best; in stress they are a run risk, exactly the BOSA fragility. Do not present them to any counterparty as "capital."
7. **NCUA-style subordinated debt / secondary capital — NOT AVAILABLE as regulatory capital.** No Nigerian rule confers capital status on subordinated debt for a cooperative society. A private subordinated loan can be contracted, but it is simply subordinated borrowing; it becomes "capital" only if and when the entity is CBN-licensed and the CBN's own capital rules apply.
8. **Listed vehicle over a mutual base — NOT AVAILABLE now;** a long-horizon aspiration requiring a listable holdco and a two-tier structure. Note the Crédit Agricole lesson from II.6(h): even that vehicle raised little *new* capital at IPO — its value was the *repeatable* raising machine and the control lock. A Nigerian cooperative is many years and one CBN licence away from this.
9. **Retained patronage / revolving funds — CONSTRUCTIBLE BY BYE-LAW but marginal.** Achievable contractually, but without US tax scaffolding and with thin "patronage" in a savings/remittance model, of limited use.
10. **Investor / non-user members — CONSTRAINED BY THE 20% CAP.** A Nigerian cooperative can admit investor members, but **§27 caps any one holder at 20% of share capital**, so a concentrated capital raise from one or a few investors is *legally impossible* within the cooperative form — a hard adverse finding for a founder hoping to bring in a lead investor. Investor money is therefore both **bounded** (no single large cheque) and **control-safe** (one-member-one-vote), i.e., small and safe. To raise meaningful outside capital from a lead investor, the founder must leave the cooperative form for a licensed company — which returns us to the ₦5bn/₦1bn/₦2bn CBN walls of II.2/II.4.
11. **Institutional protection scheme — PARTIALLY CONSTRUCTIBLE.** A **secondary or apex cooperative society** under the NCSA can function as a capital and liquidity vehicle, and a contractual cross-guarantee among a cluster of primary cooperatives could approximate a mini-IPS. **Adverse finding:** without CBN recognition it yields none of the regulatory benefits (no 0% risk weight, no consolidated own-funds treatment) — only the *economic* pooling of solvency support. That economic pooling is nonetheless real and is, per II.7, the most powerful cooperative-native capital mechanism; building it early (an apex with a mutual-support fund) is the reader's best structural hedge.

**The CBN-licence discontinuity (the decisive Nigerian fact).** As long as the entity is a mere cooperative society, it has maximum instrument freedom by bye-law and zero prudential capital pressure — but also no lawful path to offer regulated financial services such as e-money issuance or, ultimately, remittance at scale. The moment it seeks a CBN licence under BOFIA 2020 (MMO, PSSP, PSB, or MFB), a **hard paid-up-capital wall** appears (Unit MFB ₦20m; State MFB ₦1bn; National MFB ₦5bn; plus category-specific fintech thresholds and a separate refundable escrow deposit), and — critically — **CBN licensing generally runs to companies, with ownership caps** (e.g., the MFB regime's 49% maximum individual/related-party holding), which is in tension with, and may be incompatible with, the cooperative form and its one-member-one-vote governance. **Adverse finding, stated plainly:** the reader cannot both (i) remain a pure cooperative society and (ii) operate a CBN-licensed remittance business at scale. The capital arithmetic (II.2, Scenario 3) shows retained earnings cannot bridge to ₦5bn in any reasonable time; the §27 cap forbids a concentrated cooperative equity raise; and the licence regime presumes a company. The realistic architecture is therefore a **two-tier structure** — a cooperative society (member-owned, using non-withdrawable shares (2), subordinated member deposits (6), and an apex/IPS (11) for economic strength) that **owns or sponsors a separately CBN-licensed company** which raises the regulatory capital from investors under company law. This is the design brief Parts V and VI must resolve; Part II's contribution is to prove *why* the single-entity cooperative path to licensed remittance is closed by the capital constraint. [CONFIRMED FACT (NCSA sections; CBN thresholds); ANALYTICAL INFERENCE (the two-tier conclusion).]

## II.9 Ten propositions on cooperative capital

1. **Capital is two problems, not one.** The economic need for loss absorption exists with or without a regulator; the prudential need is a legal artefact layered on top. The gap between what absorbs loss and what is *recognised* as capital is where all the engineering happens.

2. **The member share fails as capital by construction.** Redeemable at par, it is a financial liability under IFRIC 2 and CET1 only to the extent redemption can be refused (CRR Art. 29). The redemption paradox — capital must be made un-redeemable to count, but redeemability is what members value — is the fulcrum of the entire subject.

3. **Growth is capped at g\* = b × ROE.** A cooperative cannot outgrow the product of its retention ratio and its return on equity, because it cannot issue permanent equity on demand. An investor-owned rival can. This single inequality explains slow cooperative growth, merger waves, and conversions.

4. **Regulatory capital walls are not scalable by retained earnings.** A Nigerian cooperative fintech at ₦200m earning 20% ROE needs ~18 years to reach a ₦5bn National MFB threshold. Walls must be vaulted with outside equity the cooperative form cannot raise; therefore licensed scale requires a non-cooperative capital vehicle.

5. **Retained earnings are the best capital and the slowest.** Permanent, unambiguously CET1, cheap — and bounded by the horizon and free-rider problems, which is why the strong systems make retention *compulsory* (France 15%; Italy's BCCs 70% under TUB Art. 37).

6. **Indivisible reserves buy quality and durability, not necessarily growth.** They manufacture permanent capital and an anti-demutualisation lock, but a reserve nobody can claim dulls the incentive to build it (hence compulsion), and suppressed distributions with no equity access can cap growth. That indivisible-reserve systems accumulate *higher-quality* capital is well supported; that they accumulate *more* is UNKNOWN.

7. **The non-withdrawable share is the cheapest win.** Converting a redeemable member share into a transfer-only, non-withdrawable one (the Kenyan model) upgrades internal capital from liability to quasi-permanent equity **at zero control cost**. It is available to a Nigerian cooperative today by bye-law — provided the bar on redemption is unconditional and entrenched.

8. **External capital is always priced somewhere.** No market-facing instrument escapes the capital–control trade-off; it is paid in yield (Rabobank Certificates' 6.5%-floor perpetual), in residual dilution (preference shares), or in the fragility and cost of the legal engineering that preserves control (Crédit Agricole's SAS Rue La Boétie ≥50%). The "mutualist IPO" of 2001 raised little new group capital (≈€244m of genuinely new CASA equity at a €16.60 strike); its value was a repeatable listed raising-machine, not a one-off injection.

9. **The institutional protection scheme is the cooperative-native solution.** Germany is world-class-durable with **no asset lock** because the IPS (CRR Art. 113(7)) relocates capital to the network: no bank must hold alone the capital it can draw on mutually. Pooling solvency is the closest thing to a free lunch in the survey, and it solves the cooperative capital problem *cooperatively*. A Nigerian apex/cross-guarantee can capture the economic (not the regulatory) benefit today.

10. **The Nigerian single-entity cooperative path to licensed remittance is closed by capital, and the answer is a two-tier structure.** The §27 20% cap forbids a concentrated cooperative equity raise; the growth arithmetic forbids organic bridging to CBN walls; the licence regime presumes a company with different ownership caps. A member-owned cooperative (strengthened by non-withdrawable shares, subordinated member deposits, and an apex/IPS) that sponsors a separately CBN-licensed, investor-capitalised company is the structure the capital constraint forces. Part II's job was to prove the necessity; Parts V and VI must build it.
