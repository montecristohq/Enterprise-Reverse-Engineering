# PART VI — WHAT TRANSPLANTS, AND THE DESIGN BRIEF

## VI.1 The transplant problem, methodologically

In 1894 Horace Plunkett's Irish Agricultural Organisation Society began planting Raiffeisen credit cooperatives across rural Ireland. They were faithful copies of the German original — unlimited liability, tiny local common bonds, no dividends, volunteer management, the whole apparatus that had, by then, made the German countryside solvent. By the early 1920s they had been all but wiped out. Timothy Guinnane's "A Failed Institutional Transplant: Raiffeisen's Credit Cooperatives in Ireland, 1894–1914" (*Explorations in Economic History* 31(1), 1994, 38–61) is the standing warning of this study, and it must be the methodological premise of this Part, because the reader is about to attempt exactly what Plunkett attempted: to import a set of institutional devices, worked out in Germany, Quebec, the Netherlands, the United States and Kenya, into different soil — Nigeria in 2026.

Guinnane's diagnosis is not that the Irish were feckless or that cooperation "does not work" in poor agrarian societies. It is precise and mechanical. The German cooperative worked because of three things that did not exist in Ireland: it attracted the more prosperous, literate locals whose participation supplied *monitoring and expertise*; it was embedded in a dense network of *central auditing federations* that supervised and certified each village society; and it operated in a rural credit market that was *underserved*, leaving a niche for the newcomer. In Ireland the prosperous farmers stayed away, no strong auditing federation ever developed, the existing banks and traders already served the rural credit market adequately, and — as Guinnane emphasises — rural Irish people "seemed reluctant to force their neighbours" to repay, so the social-collateral mechanism that made Raiffeisen lending safe did not fire. The *form* transplanted perfectly. The *institutions the form depended on* did not.

Christopher Colvin and Eoin McLaughlin sharpened the point in "Raiffeisenism abroad: why did German cooperative banking fail in Ireland but prosper in the Netherlands?" (*Economic History Review* 67(2), 2014, 492–516). The same German template, planted in the Netherlands at the same time, produced Rabobank — one of the most successful cooperative banks on earth. The difference was not the template. Dutch Raiffeisen banks "operated in niche markets with few competitors," were "largely self-financing, closely integrated into the wider rural economy, and able to take advantage of economic and religious divisions in rural society" — Catholic and Protestant villages each got their own bank, which sharpened the common bond and the social collateral. Irish rural markets, by contrast, "were unsegmented and populated by long-established incumbents, leaving little room for new players, whatever their institutional advantages." One template, two soils, two outcomes.

This gives us the test that the rest of Part VI applies. **A feature of a foreign cooperative system transplants to Nigeria only if the institutions it depends on already exist in Nigeria, can be built by the founder, or can be adequately substituted.** To apply the test we must classify every feature by what it depends on. Three categories:

**(a) Self-contained features.** These can be adopted by drafting alone — a clause in the bye-laws, a term in a share, a provision in a shareholders' agreement. They depend on nothing external except a court willing to enforce a contract and a registrar willing to register a bye-law. Non-withdrawable member shares are the paradigm case: Kenya achieves the effect by regulation, but the *effect* is producible in Nigeria by a bye-law, because the Nigerian Co-operative Societies Act leaves withdrawability to the bye-laws (§11(2)(g)–(i)) and imposes no statutory rule of its own. Self-contained features are the cheap wins. They are where the founder should look first.

**(b) Institution-dependent features.** These require a federation, a supervisor, a statute, or a network that must *already exist* — you cannot conjure it by drafting. Germany's compulsory audit federation (the Prüfungsverband) is institution-dependent: it is not a clause you write, it is a hundred-year-old sector body with statutory standing, professional staff and a compulsory-membership rule. The German institutional protection scheme (Institutssicherung, recognised in CRR Art. 113(7)) is institution-dependent: it is a mutual solvency guarantee backed by decades of accumulated joint funds and a supervisor willing to grant it capital and liquidity waivers. Atruvia (the shared IT company of the German cooperative banks), DZ BANK (their central institution), the Co-operative Bank of Kenya, and the US CUSO tier are all institution-dependent. These are the features that failed to transplant to Ireland, and they are the features most likely to fail to transplant to Nigeria, because — as Part IV established — **no functioning cooperative apex infrastructure exists in Nigeria at all.** For institution-dependent features the question is never "can I adopt it?" but "is there a second-best private substitute, and how much weaker is it?"

**(c) Culture- or scale-dependent features.** These require member homogeneity, membership density, or a level of interpersonal trust that no drafting and no institution can manufacture on demand. The social-collateral mechanism at the heart of Raiffeisen lending — neighbours monitoring and, crucially, being *willing to enforce against* neighbours — is culture-dependent. It is exactly what Ireland lacked and the Netherlands and Germany had. Delegate democracy that actually delegates (rather than rubber-stamping) is partly culture-dependent. The willingness of "prosperous locals" to serve as unpaid monitors is culture-dependent. These features cannot be transplanted; they can only be *selected for* — by choosing a membership within which the culture already exists.

The single most important strategic implication of this taxonomy is stated here and defended throughout: **the features the reader most needs are the ones that transplant worst.** The property-rights defences that Parts I–III identified as decisive — the asset lock, the substitute monitor, the protection scheme — are disproportionately institution-dependent, and Nigeria's institutional environment is the thinnest of all six jurisdictions. The reader's design task is therefore not to copy a foreign model but to *reconstruct its functions from self-contained parts*: to manufacture, by bye-law and by contract, the protections that Germany gets from its federations and Quebec gets from its statute. That is difficult, it is second-best, and it is the honest work of this Part.

One methodological caution before we begin. The direction of the Guinnane–Colvin finding cuts both ways. Ireland shows that a good form fails in bad soil; but the Netherlands shows that the *same* form thrives when it finds an underserved niche and a genuine common bond. Nigeria in 2026 is, on the niche dimension, far more Dutch than Irish: the formal financial system leaves an enormous underserved population, and the informal cooperative culture (ajo/esusu/adashe) is real, dense and old. The pessimism of the transplant literature is about *institutions*, not about *demand*. The reader's advantage is demand; his problem is institutions. Hold both in view.

## VI.2 Feature-by-feature transplant audit

We now work through the mechanisms this study has identified. For each: what it is, where it comes from, what it depends on, whether it transplants, and — if not — the nearest available substitute and how much weaker that substitute is.

### VI.2.1 The indivisible reserve / asset lock (Quebec, France, Italy)

*What it is.* An accumulated reserve that no member can ever claim — not on withdrawal, not on the cooperative's conversion, and in the strongest form not even on dissolution, when it passes instead to another cooperative or a cooperative development fund (the *dévolution désintéressée*, disinterested devolution). Part I established this as **the master legal variable**: the asset lock is what most reliably neutralises the horizon and portfolio problems and blocks the demutualisation failure mode, because it removes the pot of value that opportunistic members or acquirers would otherwise cash out.

*Where it comes from.* Quebec is strongest — an indivisible *réserve générale* with statutory disinterested devolution. Continental Europe supplies the reference points: France requires worker cooperatives to place a minimum of 15% of surplus into indivisible reserves (and French cooperatives on average hold much more); Italy under the 1947 Basevi Law allowed cooperatives to assign all surplus to indivisible reserves tax-free, and after the 2001 Berlusconi reform allocations to indivisible reserves remain 70% tax-exempt for cooperatives doing at least 50% of their business with members, with a 3% levy on net surplus paid into cooperative development funds. Germany, by contrast — and this is the great anomaly of Part I — has *no statutory asset lock* yet resists demutualisation through its institutional environment; the Netherlands has none; the US locks credit-union net worth only procedurally through NCUA conversion rules; **Kenya and Nigeria have none.**

*What it depends on.* Two things. First, a statute or a charter that makes the lock legally effective. Second — and this is the part the reference cases understate — a rule that makes the lock *hard for the members themselves to remove later*. An asset lock that a two-thirds vote can repeal is not an asset lock; it is a suggestion. The Italian and Quebec locks are entrenched in *statute*, above the reach of any single cooperative's general meeting. That is precisely the protection a Nigerian bye-law cannot replicate, because a bye-law is amendable by the body that wrote it.

*Does it transplant?* **Partially, and this is an ANALYTICAL INFERENCE that the reader must not overstate.** The Nigerian Co-operative Societies Act supplies no asset lock, but it does not forbid one, and it requires the bye-laws to state "the purposes to which its funds may be applied and the mode of custody and investment of its funds" (§11(2)(e), confirmed in the primary text at faolex.fao.org). A founder can therefore *write* an indivisible-reserve clause and a disinterested-devolution clause into the bye-laws. The reasoning: what the statute leaves to the bye-laws, the bye-laws may fix; a mandatory-retention and non-distribution clause is within the §11(2)(e) power; a dissolution-devolution clause directing residual assets to another registered society is consistent with the Act's treatment of a registered society as a permissible member (the Act's definition of "member" in §57 includes a registered society, and §27 exempts a registered society from the 20% shareholding cap).

*How robust against later amendment?* **This is the weak link, and it must be stated plainly.** A bye-law asset lock is only as strong as the amendment rule that guards it. Under the Act, bye-laws are amendable, and amendments take effect on registration by the Director/Registrar (§12). Two defences, both imperfect: (i) draft a *super-entrenchment* clause requiring a very high threshold (e.g. 90% of all members, not merely of those voting, plus a cooling-off period and two consecutive general meetings) to amend the reserve and devolution clauses — this raises the cost of capture without eliminating it; (ii) reinforce the lock by *relocating value out of the reach of the general meeting* — routing accumulated capital into the separately-incorporated CAMA operating company (see VI.2.7), where it is protected by company law and a shareholders' agreement rather than by an amendable bye-law. The candid assessment: **the Nigerian asset lock will be materially weaker than Quebec's or Italy's, because it rests on self-entrenchment rather than statute.** It is a strong deterrent, not an absolute bar. UNKNOWN, and flagged: whether a Nigerian court would treat a super-majority entrenchment clause in a cooperative bye-law as fully enforceable against a later duly-registered amendment has not, to this author's knowledge, been litigated; the reader's lawyer should advise on whether entrenchment can be reinforced through the CAMA vehicle's constitution, where the entrenchment jurisprudence is better developed.

### VI.2.2 Non-withdrawable member shares (Kenya)

*What it is.* A member share that the member cannot redeem for cash — cannot "withdraw" — but can only transfer to another eligible member. Part II identified this as **the cheapest win in the entire design space**: it converts internal member capital from a liability (redeemable on demand, and therefore fragile) into quasi-permanent equity, at *zero control cost* (one-member-one-vote is untouched), and it is available in Nigeria by bye-law.

*Where it comes from.* Kenya operationalises it by regulation. The Sacco Societies (Non-Deposit-Taking Business) Regulations 2020 provide that a member "may transfer shares to another or other members on leaving membership of a Sacco society, provided the Sacco society shall not refund shares," and Kenyan SACCOs uniformly describe share capital as "non-refundable … can only be transferred to an existing member." Kenyan "core capital" is defined in those Regulations as "the fully paid up members' shares, capital issued, disclosed reserves, retained earnings, grants and donations all of which are not meant to be expended unless on liquidation of the Sacco Society." Note the deliberate Kenyan distinction, which the reader should copy exactly: **withdrawable deposits** (member savings, repayable) are separated from **non-withdrawable share capital** (permanent equity, only transferable). The two instruments do different jobs and must not be conflated.

*What it depends on.* The accounting effect depends on IFRIC 2. Under IFRIC 2 (*Members' Shares in Co-operative Entities*), a share the member can demand back is normally a *liability*; it becomes *equity* only if the entity "has an unconditional right to refuse redemption" or if "local law, regulation or the entity's governing charter" imposes an *unconditional* prohibition on redemption. The word doing all the work is **unconditional**. IFRIC 2 is explicit that a prohibition triggered only when "liquidity constraints … are met (or are not met)" is a *conditional* prohibition and does **not** produce equity classification; only an unconditional prohibition (absolute, or partial down to a fixed floor) does.

*Does it transplant?* **Yes — CONFIRMED as a self-contained feature.** Nigeria has no statutory rule on withdrawability; §11(2)(g)–(i) leaves it to the bye-laws. The founder therefore writes the prohibition into the governing charter, and IFRIC 2's "governing charter … unconditional prohibition" limb does the rest. This is the rare case where the Nigerian substitute is *not weaker* than the Kenyan original — indeed it is the same mechanism, achieved by charter rather than regulation.

*How to draft it so it achieves equity classification.* Three rules, derived directly from IFRIC 2:
1. **The prohibition must be unconditional.** Draft: "A member's shares shall not be redeemed, repaid or refunded by the Society in cash under any circumstances while the member remains a member, and on cessation of membership shall be capable only of transfer to another eligible member." Do **not** write "the Society may refuse redemption where liquidity permits" or "subject to capital adequacy" — that is a *conditional* prohibition and collapses the share back into a liability.
2. **Beware the three-year tail.** IFRIC 2's basis for conclusions treats proceeds of shares issued in the *preceding three years* as potentially redeemable (hence liability) where the prohibition is only partial; an *absolute* unconditional prohibition avoids this. Draft for absolute prohibition of cash redemption, with transfer as the sole exit.
3. **Separate savings from shares.** Members' withdrawable savings are a deliberate liability and should sit in a distinct "member deposit" account (supported by §19 of the Act), leaving the share account clean for equity treatment.

*What a transfer market among members requires to function.* A non-withdrawable share is only tolerable to members if they can *exit by transfer*. Kenya has spawned secondary platforms (e.g. share-transfer marketplaces) precisely because share capital is locked. For a Nigerian cooperative the transfer market needs: (i) a bye-law right to transfer to any eligible member at a price the parties agree (or at par, or at a formula tied to net asset value per share — the founder must choose); (ii) a society-maintained register and a simple matching mechanism (the society itself, or its digital platform, can run the order book); (iii) a fallback buyer of last resort — often the society itself, *funded from a designated pool*, redeeming at its discretion (discretion preserves the "unconditional right to refuse" and so preserves equity classification). The weakest link is liquidity: in a small or young cooperative there may be no willing buyer, and the member is then genuinely locked in. This is the price of permanence, and it must be disclosed to members at entry, not discovered by them at exit.

### VI.2.3 The compulsory audit federation / Prüfungsverband (Germany)

*What it is.* A sector-owned body of which every cooperative must be a member, which conducts a mandatory, recurring, competence-rich external audit — not merely of the accounts but of the *governance and business model* — and which certifies the cooperative to the outside world. Part III identified the substitute monitor as **the single best predictor of whether a cooperative degenerates**, and Germany's Prüfungsverband as the strongest such monitor in the six jurisdictions. Guinnane's own later work ("A 'Friend and Advisor': External Auditing and Confidence in Germany's Credit Cooperatives, 1889–1914," *Business History Review* 77, 2003) shows the auditing federation was not a compliance afterthought but the load-bearing institution of the whole German system — and its *absence* was a proximate cause of the Irish failure.

*What it depends on.* A statute compelling membership, a mature profession of cooperative auditors, and decades of accumulated institutional knowledge. It is the archetypal institution-dependent feature.

*Does it transplant?* **No — not as a statutory institution.** Nigeria has no compulsory cooperative audit federation, and the founder cannot legislate one into being. Worse, Part I established that Nigeria lacks even *compulsory external audit* as a statutory baseline: audit, like withdrawability, is left to the bye-laws. And Part III's Kenyan cautionary tale shows that *having* an apex is not enough if the apex is itself outside the prudential perimeter. Per PricewaterhouseCoopers' forensic audit, KUSCCO — the Kenyan SACCO apex — put "Sh13.3 billion belonging to depositors at risk" and is "insolvent by KES 12.5 billion, with assets valued at only KES 5.2 billion against liabilities amounting to KES 17.7 billion." The detail that should haunt the reader: its executives forged the signature of a *dead auditor* to sign off falsified 2022 accounts. As the PwC report put it, "the 2022 financial statements were allegedly forged as the signatures of the 2021 and 2022 audited financial statements differ, despite the signing partner being CPA Alfred Basweti of Omenye and Associates" — a partner who, the former internal auditor confirmed, had died before the accounts were signed. The lesson is double: a substitute monitor is essential, *and* a substitute monitor that is unsupervised is worse than useless because it manufactures false confidence.

*Nearest available substitute, and how much weaker.* The founder's realistic options, in ascending order of strength:
1. **Self-imposed mandatory external audit by a reputable independent firm, written into the bye-laws with compulsory rotation.** This is a self-contained feature and should be adopted without hesitation. It is weaker than a Prüfungsverband because a commercial auditor audits the *accounts*, not the *cooperative character* — it will catch theft and misstatement (if competent and genuinely independent) but not degeneration of governance. It also creates the KUSCCO risk in miniature: the auditor is chosen and paid by the audited, so independence and rotation must be hard-wired.
2. **A jointly-commissioned audit-and-supervision function shared by a cluster of cooperatives.** A group of cooperative societies can, by contract, jointly fund and appoint a shared audit and inspection body — effectively a private, voluntary Prüfungsverband — housed in a jointly-owned CAMA services company (see the CUSO discussion at VI.2.8). This is the closest constructible analogue. It is weaker than the German original in three ways: membership is voluntary (a degenerating cooperative can simply leave), it has no statutory certification power, and it has no supervisor standing behind it. But it is *materially stronger* than a solo commercial audit, because it pools the cost of real competence and creates a peer-monitoring dynamic.
3. **Submitting voluntarily to the discipline of a regulated entity.** This is the deepest substitute and the one this Part ultimately recommends: by owning a CBN-licensed subsidiary (an MFB or, later, a PSB), the cooperative pulls a regulated, prudentially-supervised, compulsorily-audited entity *inside its own group*. The CBN's supervision of the subsidiary becomes a de facto substitute monitor for the group's most dangerous activities. This does not monitor the *cooperative's* member-control dimension (the NCUA problem from Part III: excellent on solvency, useless on member control), but it is the best solvency monitor available in Nigeria.

The honest bottom line: **Nigeria offers no full substitute for the Prüfungsverband.** The reader must assemble a partial substitute from mandatory rotating audit + (ideally) a shared inspection function + the reflected discipline of a licensed subsidiary, and must understand that even the sum is weaker than what Germany takes for granted.

### VI.2.4 The institutional protection scheme (Germany, CRR Art. 113(7))

*What it is.* A mutualised solvency-and-liquidity guarantee among a network of cooperatives: each stands behind the others, a joint fund absorbs shocks, and — critically — the banking regulator *recognises* the scheme, granting members capital and large-exposure reliefs (CRR Art. 113(7) permits zero risk-weighting of intra-scheme exposures; related provisions give further relief). Part II called this the cooperative-native solution to the capital problem, because it *relocates capital to the network* rather than raising it from outside investors.

*What it depends on.* Decades of accumulated joint funds, a binding mutual-support contract, and — for the capital relief — *regulatory recognition*. Institution-dependent, twice over.

*Does it transplant?* **The contract can be built; the recognition cannot.** A cluster of Nigerian cooperatives can, by private agreement, constitute a mutual-support fund: each contributes a percentage of surplus to a jointly-held reserve (housed in a CAMA vehicle or a trust), and each undertakes to support a distressed member up to a cap. This is constructible. What is *not* available is the CBN granting the scheme any prudential recognition — there is no Nigerian analogue to CRR Art. 113(7), and the cooperatives are not CBN-supervised in the first place.

*What it achieves without regulatory recognition.* Less than in Germany, but not nothing:
- **Genuine loss-absorption.** A real joint fund really can recapitalise a distressed member, reducing the insolvency failure mode. This is worth having.
- **Peer monitoring.** Members of a mutual-guarantee scheme have skin in each other's solvency and will demand information and discipline — a partial substitute for the missing supervisor.
- **A confidence signal** to members and counterparties.

What it *cannot* achieve without recognition: capital relief (there is nothing to relieve, as the societies hold no regulatory capital), and legal certainty that the mutual-support obligation will be honoured under stress (a member can breach; enforcement is slow). The KUSCCO disaster is again the warning: a network fund that is not itself supervised can become the vehicle of the very fraud it was meant to prevent. **Any Nigerian protection scheme must be built with its own independent audit and governance from day one, or it will concentrate risk rather than mutualise it.** Verdict: a weak-to-moderate substitute, genuinely useful for loss-absorption and peer discipline, useless for the regulatory-capital benefits that make the German scheme transformative.

### VI.2.5 The delegate assembly (Germany §43a; Rabobank; Desjardins)

*What it is.* A representative body — delegates elected by members — that exercises the powers a plenary general meeting exercises in a small cooperative, once the membership grows too large for plenary democracy to function. Part III identified Germany's statutory **1,500-member threshold** (§43a GenG, permitting a *Vertreterversammlung*, a delegate assembly, above that size) as "the best available legislative judgement on when plenary democracy fails," alongside Rabobank's General Members' Council and Desjardins' federal congress.

*What it depends on.* Almost nothing external. It is a governance structure, created by the constitution.

*Does it transplant?* **Yes — self-contained.** The Nigerian Act permits the bye-laws to define the governance structure, and nothing prevents a delegate structure. The founder writes it in. The German 1,500 figure is a useful *default trigger*, not a Nigerian legal requirement; the reader should adopt a bye-law that automatically converts the general meeting to a delegate assembly once paid-up membership crosses a defined threshold (1,500 is a defensible choice; the reader may set it lower for a business-member cooperative where each member is itself an organisation). The mechanism is strong precisely because it is culture-light *in form* — but note the culture-dependent risk (VI.1 category (c)): a delegate assembly that delegates to a captured slate is worse than a plenary meeting, because it launders capture as representation. Draft the delegate *election* rules (VI.6) as carefully as the delegate *structure*.

### VI.2.6 Permanent capital instruments (Quebec *parts permanentes*; Rabobank Certificates)

*What it is.* An instrument that raises outside (or member) money on terms where the *economics* flow to the holder but the *control* stays with the members — Part II's design principle, "economics to investors, control to members." Quebec's *parts permanentes* are non-withdrawable, non-voting capital shares; Rabobank Certificates are perpetual, deeply subordinated, non-voting instruments that trade among investors and pay a discretionary distribution. Both count as high-quality capital because they satisfy the redemption paradox: they are capital precisely to the extent they are *not really the holder's to reclaim* (cf. IFRIC 2 for members' shares, CRR Art. 29 and the EBA RTS for cooperative CET1).

*What it depends on.* Contract law, a subordination that courts will honour, an investor base willing to buy non-voting perpetual paper, and (for regulatory-capital credit) a regulator applying CRR-style criteria — which Nigeria does not, for a cooperative society.

*Does it transplant?* **The instrument can be synthesised by contract; its regulatory-capital status cannot arise at the cooperative level.** The founder can create, in the CAMA operating company (not the society), a class of non-voting, perpetual, deeply-subordinated, discretionary-distribution shares — CAMA 2020 permits multiple share classes and shareholders' agreements can fix their terms. That instrument reproduces the Rabobank Certificate's economics-without-control logic. The weakest links: (i) **investor appetite** — Nigerian investors have little history with perpetual non-voting cooperative-adjacent paper, so the instrument may price expensively or not sell; (ii) **enforceable subordination and discretion** — the distribution must be genuinely discretionary and the instrument genuinely perpetual/subordinated, or it becomes a disguised debt that fails both the capital test and, potentially, the deposit-boundary test of Part V; (iii) **the capital only counts where it sits** — it strengthens the CAMA company's balance sheet (which is what a CBN licence cares about), not the society's. This is fine, because it is the CAMA company that needs regulatory capital. Verdict: transplantable *as a private instrument in the operating company*, moderately weaker than the originals because of thin investor appetite and no regulatory-capital recognition, but exactly the right tool for capitalising the licensed subsidiary without ceding member control.

### VI.2.7 The two-tier listed-vehicle control lock (Crédit Agricole's SAS Rue La Boétie ≥50%)

*What it is.* The device by which a cooperative group raises investor equity on a public market while keeping absolute control: the cooperative core holds its stake in the listed operating company through a dedicated holding vehicle that owns a controlling majority. In Crédit Agricole's case, the 39 Regional Banks hold their stake in the listed Crédit Agricole S.A. through **SAS Rue La Boétie, which held 62.45% of the capital and voting rights as at 31 December 2024** (up from 56.69% in 2022). The vehicle's express purpose, per the Crédit Agricole 2013 Registration Document, is "to hold enough shares to ensure that it always owns at least 50% of the share capital and voting rights of Crédit Agricole S.A." — a permanent controlling block that lets the listed entity access public capital while effective control rests with the Regional Banks.

*What it depends on.* Company law that recognises a holding company, a controlling shareholding, and a shareholders' agreement — all of which Nigeria has under CAMA 2020.

*Does it transplant?* **Yes, and it is the structural spine of this Part's recommendation.** The Nigerian equivalent: the cooperative society (or a wholly-owned holding company beneath it) holds a controlling majority of the CAMA operating company that carries the CBN licence(s); outside investors hold the minority (economic-rich, control-poor) and/or the non-voting permanent instruments of VI.2.6. The **NPF Microfinance Bank precedent proves the structure works in Nigeria**: the Nigeria Police Co-operative Society holds about 62.6% of a CBN-licensed, NGX-listed bank (VI.2.9). The Crédit Agricole lesson the reader must internalise from Part III: **the control lock must be built *before* investors hold the economic majority — retrofitting is not available.** A controlling stake and a golden share are cheap to create at incorporation and impossible to reclaim after dilution. The weakest link is not legal but disciplinary: a controlling cooperative can *misuse* its control (the mirror risk), which is why the reserved-matters and governance provisions of VI.6 matter.

### VI.2.8 The CUSO model (US)

*What it is.* A Credit Union Service Organization — a separately-incorporated, for-profit company owned by one or more credit unions, which supplies services (IT, lending back-office, payments, compliance) that no single credit union could afford alone. NCUA Regulation §712.1(d) defines it; a CUSO "may be wholly owned by a single credit union or may be owned by multiple, mixed organizations." It is the US answer to the shared-infrastructure problem — pooling cost and capability across cooperatives.

*What it depends on.* Company law (which Nigeria has) and a *cluster of cooperatives willing to co-own it* (which Nigeria largely lacks — Part IV: no CUSO tier exists).

*Does it transplant?* **The vehicle transplants; the ecosystem does not (yet).** A cooperative-owned CAMA services company serving several societies is straightforwardly constructible: the society (or a group of societies) incorporates a CAMA company to provide shared IT, payments rails, compliance, and the jointly-commissioned audit/inspection function of VI.2.3. The statutory footing is the same investment power discussed at VI.2.9. What does not exist is the *network*: in the US a new CUSO plugs into an established collaborative culture and dozens of potential co-owners; in Nigeria the founder must *be* the ecosystem's first mover, co-owning with whatever handful of aligned societies he can assemble. So the CUSO *form* is available and useful — indeed the two-tier operating company is itself a species of single-owner CUSO — but the *cost-pooling benefit* is thin until other cooperatives join. This is a chicken-and-egg problem the founder can only solve by building the vehicle and inviting others in. Verdict: transplantable in form, weak in benefit until a cluster forms; worth building anyway because the operating company must exist regardless.

### VI.2.9 Cooperative ownership of a licensed bank (the NPF Microfinance Bank precedent)

*What it is.* A registered cooperative society owning and controlling a CBN-licensed, CAMA-incorporated bank. Part V established, and this Part confirms, that **this is not a hypothesis — it already exists in Nigeria.**

*The precedent, verified.* NPF Microfinance Bank Plc (formerly NPF Community Bank Ltd, RC 220824) was incorporated on 19 May 1993 under CAMA as a limited company, holds a CBN microfinance licence, is insured by the NDIC, and has been listed on the Nigerian Exchange (ticker NPFMCRFBK) since 2010 — the only microfinance bank so listed. Its controlling shareholder is the **Nigeria Police Co-operative Society Limited, holding about 62.6% of issued shares** (per NGX filings for the year ended 31 December 2024), with the Nigeria Police Welfare Insurance Scheme holding a further ~15.6%; the bank describes itself as "a subsidiary of Nigerian Police Co-Operative Society Limited." It reported shareholders' funds of about ₦13 billion and over 1.5 million customers by early 2026, and — importantly for the deposit-boundary analysis — it serves "the general public," not only police personnel, lawfully, *because the CAMA bank (not the society) holds the deposit-taking licence.* Its free float was reported at 21.51% as at 31 December 2024, satisfying the NGX 20% minimum.

*What it depends on.* A statutory power for a cooperative to invest in / own equity in a CAMA company. **This is the legal hinge of the entire recommendation, and it must be stated with precision.** The Nigerian Co-operative Societies Act does *not* contain an itemised "may hold shares in any body corporate" clause, and it imposes *no* Director/Registrar approval requirement for investments. The operative authority is a chain of three provisions:
- **§33 "Investment of funds":** "A registered society may invest or deposit its funds— (a) in a Co-operative Bank, or any other bank approved for the purpose by the committee of that society; (b) in any securities issued and guaranteed by the Federal Government; and **(c) in any other manner approved by the Committee of the society.**" The equity investment rests on the open residual clause §33(c) — approval by the society's *own Committee*, governed by its bye-laws — not on any express body-corporate clause.
- **§6 "Effect of registration":** registration makes the society a body corporate able to hold property "of any description," contract, sue and be sued, and "do all things necessary for the purpose of its constitution" — the corporate capacity to acquire and hold shares.
- **§11(2)(e):** the bye-laws must state "the purposes to which its funds may be applied and the mode of custody and investment of its funds" — so the *scope* of permissible investment is set by the society's own registered bye-laws.

Two further provisions confirm the structure is contemplated: **§27** exempts a "registered society" from the one-fifth (20%) shareholding cap (so a society may hold a controlling majority), and **§55(4)** provides that CAMA "shall not apply to a registered society" — meaning the society itself stays outside CAMA while the *company it owns* remains a distinct CAMA person. (SCHOLARLY/PRACTITIONER CONSENSUS, used with caution: legal commentators read §33(c) plus the bye-laws as authorising equity holdings, and note that cooperatives are tax-exempt on income including "shares or interest held in other entities.")

*Does it transplant?* **It is not a transplant at all — it is a domestic precedent, CONFIRMED, and it is the strongest single fact in the reader's favour.** The whole architecture recommended below is, in effect, "do what the Nigeria Police Co-operative Society did." The one caveat the reader must carry: the statutory footing is *general, not itemised* — it rests on §33(c)'s "any other manner approved by the Committee," reinforced by well-drafted bye-laws that expressly authorise the investment and the formation of a subsidiary. The reader's lawyer should draft §11(2)(e) fund-purpose and investment clauses that explicitly name the power to hold shares in, and to promote, a CAMA company, so that the §33(c) authority is put beyond argument. (The Lagos State Co-operative Societies Law, Cap C14 Laws of Lagos State 2015, mirrors the federal scheme, but the exact section number of its investment provision could not be verified from free primary sources and should be confirmed by counsel — UNKNOWN, flagged.)

### VI.2.10 Political-capture resistance

*What it is.* Whatever protects a cooperative from being captured for patronage — turned into a vehicle for a politician, a union boss, a "big man," or a faction. Part III's seven-part capture taxonomy (managerial, board/insider, factional, staff, regional, external, and **political**) named political capture as **the dominant risk in Kenya and Nigeria.**

*What it depends on.* This is the hardest question in the Part, because political-capture resistance is overwhelmingly *culture- and institution-dependent* (category (c) and (b)), and Nigeria is weak on both. There is no strong external supervisor to appeal to (unlike Germany's BaFin-plus-Prüfungsverband), no entrenched norm of cooperative independence, and an environment where cooperatives are frequently sponsored by, and captured through, the state (the Government-Sponsored Co-operative Model that Part V described cuts both ways — it enables the NPF structure but also invites patronage).

*What, if anything, protects the reader?* No single device; a layered, mostly self-contained defence:
1. **A tight, genuine common bond** (VI.4, VI.6). Political capture is easiest where membership is diffuse and members do not know or monitor each other. A homogeneous membership with real shared interest is the first line — this is the Raiffeisen/Netherlands lesson (segmentation and a real common bond) applied to politics.
2. **The asset lock and non-withdrawable shares.** A captor's prize is the pot; lock the pot and the prize shrinks.
3. **Contested elections with real rules** (VI.6): term and tenure limits, staggered boards, a nominations process that cannot be monopolised, and a separated supervisory committee that the management committee cannot appoint.
4. **The purpose-lock** (VI.6): a constitutional object clause that ties the cooperative to its defined member-service mission, giving members and auditors a standard against which to challenge diversion.
5. **Distance from the state's fiscal levers**: resist grant-dependency and state-directed lending, which are the classic conduits of political capture.
6. **The reflected discipline of a licensed subsidiary**: CBN fit-and-proper requirements on the *directors of the CAMA company* import an external screen that the society itself lacks.

Verdict: **political-capture resistance transplants worst of all, because it is the most institution- and culture-dependent feature and Nigeria's environment is the most hostile.** The reader cannot buy it or draft it in a single clause; he can only *assemble* a partial defence and *select* a membership in which the culture of independence already exists. This is the deepest reason the membership-definition choice (VI.4.1) is the most consequential decision in the whole design.

## VI.3 What Nigeria has that the others lack

Even-handedness requires the other ledger. Nigeria is institution-poor, but it is not advantage-poor. Several genuine assets exist, and some are things the reference jurisdictions would envy.

**A radically permissive statute.** The Nigerian Co-operative Societies Act's great weakness — that it supplies almost nothing mandatory — is also, for a sophisticated founder, its great strength: it *forbids* almost nothing either. Withdrawability, audit, asset locks, reserve policy, governance architecture and investment powers are all left to the bye-laws (§11). A founder who knows what a good cooperative looks like can *build* one, unconstrained by a prescriptive statute. In Germany you inherit the Prüfungsverband; in Nigeria you may design your own governance from a blank sheet. For most founders this permissiveness is a trap (they build nothing, and degenerate); for a founder armed with Parts I–V it is a workshop.

**A very low formation threshold and cost.** A savings-and-credit society needs only 20 members (ten for most other types; fifty for agricultural), and registration is administratively cheap and quick at the state Registrar — far below the capital and process burden of chartering a bank or even forming many foreign cooperatives. The reader can *stand up the cooperative shell almost immediately* and begin building membership and capital while the expensive licensed entities are still years away.

**Favourable tax treatment.** Nigerian cooperative societies enjoy income-tax exemption on their cooperative activities, including — per practitioner commentary — income from shares or interests held in other entities. This materially improves the economics of retaining surplus into indivisible reserves (the France/Italy insight: tax-favouring the lock accelerates capital formation).

**An enormous diaspora remittance market.** The CBN stated that "remittance flows into the Nigerian economy rose by nine percent to $20.98 billion in 2024, marking the highest level in five years," with IMTO inflows that "surged by 43.5 per cent to $4.73bn, up from $3.30bn in 2023" (CBN statement, April 2025). This is the demand-side reason the reader's eventual remittance product is commercially serious — and it is precisely the "underserved niche with real demand" that made the Dutch transplant succeed where the Irish (a saturated market) failed. On the Colvin–McLaughlin dimension, Nigeria looks Dutch.

**High mobile and digital penetration**, which lowers the cost of the shared-infrastructure and delivery problem and makes a digital transfer market for non-withdrawable shares (VI.2.2) and a delegate-democracy communications layer feasible.

**A deep, old, indigenous cooperative culture — ajo/esusu/adashe.** This is the most interesting asset and the one the reference literature would most recognise. Rotating savings and credit associations — *ajo* (Yoruba), *esusu* (Igbo/Yoruba), *adashe* (Hausa) — are ubiquitous, ancient, and built on exactly the *social-collateral* mechanism whose absence doomed the Irish transplant: members contribute a fixed sum at fixed intervals, the pot rotates, and the whole edifice runs on kinship-like solidarity, mutual trust, and the members' willingness to enforce against defaulting neighbours. Where Guinnane found rural Irish "reluctant to force their neighbours" to repay, Nigerian ROSCA culture is built on the opposite norm — the *alajo* collector, the clan-like membership, the reputational cost of default. The 2023 EFInA Access to Finance survey confirms informal providers "continue to play an important role," especially among women and in rural and south-eastern Nigeria. **The Raiffeisen social-collateral machine that could not be transplanted to Ireland already exists, indigenously, in Nigeria.** This is a profound advantage — *if* the founder builds on it rather than against it.

**The NPF precedent** (VI.2.9), proving cooperative control of a licensed bank is achievable in fact, not just theory.

### Is the informal savings-group tradition an asset to build on, or a competitor?

**Assessment: primarily an asset, with a real but manageable competitive edge, and a latent regulatory trap.** The honest answer has three parts.

*As an asset:* ajo/esusu supplies the reader with (i) a population already habituated to periodic contribution, rotating benefit and social enforcement — the behavioural substrate a cooperative fintech needs; (ii) ready-made *common bonds* (existing ajo groups are pre-formed homogeneous clusters the reader can formalise into cooperative membership units); and (iii) the social-collateral norm that makes small-ticket lending safe without formal collateral. A cooperative fintech that *digitises and formalises* existing ajo groups — giving them escrow, records, transparency and a bridge to formal credit and remittance — is building on the strongest cultural asset Nigeria offers. A visible cohort of Nigerian fintechs already targets exactly this space — Technext (July 2025) names Thrifto (founded 2024 by Sulaimon Biodun Durojaiye), CircleFunds (CEO Oluwasogo Ogundowole), Alajo, and LibertyPay, whose 2023 AJO program reportedly saw ~150% growth in collection volumes — which both validates the demand and warns of competition.

*As a competitor:* informal ROSCAs are free, trusted, interest-light and frictionless; they will not vanish, and a formal cooperative that offers *only* what ajo already offers, at higher cost and more paperwork, will lose. The reader cannot beat ajo at being ajo. He must offer what ajo *cannot*: safety of larger balances (escrow, insurance, deposit protection via the licensed subsidiary), access to remittance and formal payments, credit that scales beyond the pot, records that build a financial identity, and returns on permanent share capital. The competitive frontier is *complementarity*, not substitution.

*The regulatory trap:* the very feature that makes ROSCAs attractive — open, informal, trust-based membership — is fatal if imported naively into the formal structure. Part V's deposit boundary is unforgiving: **members-only dealing is lawful; soliciting or accepting deposits from the general public triggers BOFIA §2 and is criminal under §2(2).** An "open to everyone" ajo-style membership collapses the member/public distinction and turns the cooperative into an illegal deposit-taker. So the tradition must be *formalised into bounded membership*, not replicated as open access. This is the hinge between VI.3 and VI.4.1.

## VI.4 The design space — options laid out fairly

Here the design choices are presented neutrally. Each is a genuine fork; each has a real case on both sides. Section VI.5 then commits.

### VI.4.1 Membership definition

*The fork:* open common bond vs tight common bond; individuals vs businesses; geographic vs affinity vs employer-based.

*Case for a tight/affinity/employer bond:* Homogeneous members share preferences, which — following the Hart–Moore insight that Part III relied on (heterogeneity erodes the cooperative advantage and raises the cost of collective decision-making) — minimises factional and influence-cost capture and keeps governance cheap. A tight bond also preserves the social-collateral mechanism (VI.3) and, decisively, keeps the cooperative comfortably inside the member/public deposit boundary (Part V). It resists political capture (VI.2.10). This is the Raiffeisen/Rabobank recipe: a real, bounded common bond.

*Case against:* A tight bond caps scale and therefore caps the capital that member shares can raise (Part II's g\* = b × ROE ceiling bites harder with fewer members) and the customer base for the eventual remittance product. It may exclude exactly the diaspora senders and recipients the remittance business needs.

*Individuals vs businesses:* Individual membership fits the ajo/esusu culture, spreads risk, and keeps one-member-one-vote meaningful. Business membership concentrates capital (each member can contribute more), can be more sophisticated, and suits a cooperative of, say, market associations or SMEs — but business members are more heterogeneous in size and appetite (sharpening influence costs), and a cooperative dominated by a few large business members drifts toward oligarchy.

*Geographic vs affinity vs employer:* Employer-based bonds (the NPF model — police personnel) are the tightest and the easiest to administer (payroll deduction, natural common bond, low default), which is why they dominate successful Nigerian and Kenyan credit cooperatives; but they tie the cooperative's fate to one employer. Affinity bonds (a profession, a faith community, a trade association, a diaspora-origin community) are looser but larger. Geographic bonds are largest and loosest, and shade toward the "open" membership that endangers the deposit boundary.

### VI.4.2 Share design

*The fork:* withdrawable vs non-withdrawable; par value; minimum holding; the effect of the §27 20% cap.

*Withdrawable shares* are attractive to members (liquidity) but are a *liability* under IFRIC 2 and make the capital base fragile — a run redeems the cooperative to death. *Non-withdrawable shares* (VI.2.2) are permanent equity but illiquid to the member; they require a transfer market to be tolerable. *Par value and minimum holding* trade accessibility against capital depth: a low minimum admits the ajo-scale member but raises little; a high minimum raises capital but excludes. The *§27 20% cap* (no member other than a registered society may hold more than one-fifth of share capital) is a *pro-democracy feature* — it prevents a single member buying control — but it also caps how much any one committed member can capitalise the society, pushing the cooperative toward either many small members or the two-tier structure (where outside capital enters the *company*, not the society, and the cap does not bind).

### VI.4.3 Reserve policy

*The fork:* distributable vs indivisible; and what retention rate.

*Distributable reserves* reward current members and ease recruitment but activate the horizon problem (members prefer payout to reinvestment) and leave nothing locked against demutualisation. *Indivisible reserves* (VI.2.1) build permanent capital and deter conversion but ask current members to forgo value for the institution and posterity. *Retention rate* reference points: France mandates ≥15% of surplus to indivisible reserves; Italy effectively channels the large majority of surplus to indivisible reserves (70% tax-exemption threshold; historically up to 100% under Basevi), plus a 3% development-fund levy. A high retention rate is the single most powerful organic capital lever, but it is a tax on the present generation.

### VI.4.4 Governance architecture

*The fork:* plenary vs delegate assembly; unitary management committee vs separated supervisory committee; board composition and competence requirements.

*Plenary democracy* is legitimate and cheap while small; it fails at scale (Part III; German §43a's 1,500 trigger). *Delegate assembly* (VI.2.5) scales but risks laundering capture. *Unitary committee* is simple but concentrates power; a *separated supervisory committee* (the German two-board model) creates internal check but adds cost and can deadlock. *Competence requirements* on the board are essential for a body that will own a bank — the Co-operative Bank of the UK is the cautionary tale: Lord Myners' interim governance review (May 2014) found the Co-operative Group's elected directors had overseen "breathtakingly value-destructive" decisions (the Britannia and Somerfield takeovers) and that the Group suffered a significant "democratic deficit in which ordinary members have surprisingly weak constitutional rights"; Myners also called the Group "manifestly dysfunctional," and the episode — a £2.5bn 2013 Group loss and the Reverend Paul Flowers affair — is Part III's proof that *democratic boards can govern banks only with competence-preserving scaffolding.* But competence requirements can also be a Trojan horse for disenfranchising ordinary members in favour of a professional clique — the balance is delicate.

### VI.4.5 Capital strategy

*The fork:* member-only vs outside investors; where to sit on Part II's capital–control frontier.

*Member-only* capital preserves purity and control absolutely but caps growth at g\* = b × ROE — Part II's arithmetic that **₦200m at 20% ROE takes ~18 years to reach ₦5bn**, far too slow to fund a National MFB (₦5bn) or PSB (₦5bn) organically. *Outside investors* break the ceiling but threaten control unless the instrument is designed (VI.2.6) to give economics without votes. The frontier is real: no instrument escapes the trade-off entirely, but non-withdrawable member shares (cheapest, zero control cost) and the two-tier investor structure (economics to investors, control to members) *bend* it.

### VI.4.6 Corporate architecture

*The fork:* single cooperative vs two-tier vs multi-entity; and if two-tier, which entity holds what.

*Single cooperative:* simplest, cheapest, but hits a wall — a cooperative society "can never itself hold a CBN or IMTO licence" (Part V), so a single-entity design can never offer regulated deposit-taking, payments or remittance. *Two-tier:* the society owns a CAMA company that holds the licence(s) — the NPF structure, legally proven (VI.2.9). *Multi-entity:* the society owns a holding company, which owns separate licensed subsidiaries (Super-Agent, MFB, PSB) and a shared-services CUSO — cleaner risk-ring-fencing and cleaner investor entry, but more cost, more governance surface, and more places for control to leak.

### VI.4.7 Licensing path (Part V's Paths A/B/C/D)

*The fork, as Part V framed it:*
- **Path A — Agent/partnership:** operate as naira-payout agent/sub-agent of a licensed IMTO, and as agent of a sponsor bank. Fast, cheap, low-capital; but every capability is borrowed and every lever handed to an outsider (Part IV).
- **Path B — Super-Agent subsidiary:** the CAMA company obtains a Super-Agent licence (₦50m). Own agent network, still no deposit licence.
- **Path C — MFB:** Unit (Tier 2 ₦50m / Tier 1 ₦200m), State (₦1bn), or National (₦5bn) microfinance bank — real deposit-taking, real balance sheet, real prudential supervision (the reflected substitute monitor of VI.2.3).
- **Path D — PSB or IMTO-adjacent:** Payment Service Bank (₦5bn) for wide payments/deposit reach, or positioning as the naira leg of remittance. The 2024 IMTO guidelines (released 31 January 2024) set minimum operating capital at US$1 million and a non-refundable ₦10 million application fee, and **barred fintechs from holding IMTO licences** — so the reader cannot realistically obtain his own IMTO licence and must ride a licensed IMTO principal.

### VI.4.8 Infrastructure

*The fork:* build vs buy vs sponsor. *Build* (own core banking, own rails) maximises control and data ownership but is expensive and slow. *Buy* (commercial vendor) is fast but "every capability bought rather than federated hands a lever of degeneration to an outsider" (Part IV); mitigation is contractual (data ownership, exit rights, source-code escrow, multi-homing). *Sponsor* (ride a sponsor bank's licence and rails) is fastest and cheapest but deepest in dependency. Since no cooperative apex infrastructure exists in Nigeria (no Atruvia, no DZ BANK equivalent), the "federation" is, realistically, a vendor or a sponsor bank — the choice is which outsider, on what contractual terms, and how reversibly.

## VI.5 THE RECOMMENDATION — commit and defend

A decision document must decide. Here is what I would do.

### Base-case assumptions (labelled)

Three venture parameters were requested and not fully supplied. Per the brief:
- **(b) Remittance is an *eventual* product, not the entry wedge.** This is treated as *established*, on the reader's own consistent framing. The entry product is member savings and credit (the ajo/esusu-native business); remittance is a later addition once the licensed subsidiary and scale exist.
- **(a) Membership — ASSUMPTION: individuals**, bound by a *tight affinity or employer/association common bond* (e.g. a profession, a trade/market association, a faith or diaspora-origin community, or an employer group), not an open geographic public. Labelled as an assumption; sensitivity run below.
- **(c) Year-three scale — ASSUMPTION: mid-hundreds to low thousands of members** (base case ~2,000–5,000 members by year three), i.e. large enough to matter and to trigger delegate governance, small enough that the common bond and social collateral still bind. Labelled; sensitivity run below.

### The recommended architecture

**A two-tier (evolving to three-tier) structure, built in a defined sequence, with the control lock installed at inception.**

**Entity 1 — The Cooperative Society (the member core).** Register, immediately, a savings-and-credit cooperative society under the Nigerian Co-operative Societies Act (state Registrar; 20-member minimum; use Lagos or another capable state), with a *tight common bond* and *individual members*. Its bye-laws are the load-bearing document (VI.6): non-withdrawable member shares (unconditional, IFRIC 2 equity), a separate withdrawable member-deposit account (§19), an indivisible reserve with disinterested devolution and a high mandatory retention rate, a delegate-assembly trigger, a separated supervisory committee, contested-election rules, mandatory rotating external audit, insider-lending limits, a purpose-lock, super-entrenchment of the protective clauses, and — critically — an express §11(2)(e)/§33(c) power to invest in and promote a CAMA company and to hold a controlling stake in it. The society deals *only with its members* (deposit boundary preserved).

**Entity 2 — The Operating Company (the licensed vehicle).** Incorporate, under CAMA 2020, a company that the society controls with a permanent majority (≥60%, mirroring SAS Rue La Boétie's 62.45% and NPF's ~62.6%), held directly or through a wholly-owned holding company. This is the entity that obtains CBN licences, employs professional management, holds the technology, and — eventually — carries the remittance-agency and payments business. Its constitution and a shareholders' agreement install the control lock *before any outside investor enters*: cooperative majority, a golden share over defined reserved matters (change of control, change of purpose, disposal of the licence, related-party dealings, amendment of the control provisions), cooperative board-appointment rights sufficient to control the board, and a class of *non-voting, perpetual, subordinated, discretionary-distribution shares* (VI.2.6) reserved for future outside capital — economics to investors, control to members.

**Entity 3 (later) — The shared-services / CUSO vehicle**, if and when a cluster of aligned cooperatives can co-own it and a jointly-commissioned audit/inspection function (VI.2.3) can be stood up.

**Capital and share design.** Member capital is raised as *non-withdrawable shares* (permanent equity) plus *withdrawable deposits* (liability, for member liquidity). Set a modest minimum shareholding (accessible to ajo-scale members) but encourage additional voluntary shares. Retain a high proportion of surplus (recommend ≥50% to the indivisible reserve as a bye-law floor, exploiting the tax exemption) to maximise organic capital. Recognise Part II's ceiling honestly: organic member capital alone cannot fund a National MFB or PSB on any reasonable horizon, so outside capital enters *the company* (never the society), through the non-voting subordinated instrument, once the control lock is proven and the business case is real.

**Governance.** Plenary general meeting while small; automatic conversion to a *delegate assembly* on crossing the bye-law threshold (default 1,500 members, per §43a GenG's judgement). A *separated supervisory committee* independent of the management committee. Competence requirements for directors of the *operating company* (satisfying CBN fit-and-proper), balanced by reserved member seats so the professionalisation does not disenfranchise the base — the Co-operative Bank scaffolding lesson applied without the Co-operative Bank's democratic deficit.

**Licensing sequence (integrating Part V's Paths).** As Part V concluded: **register the cooperative → operate in partnership (Path A: agent of a sponsor bank and naira-payout sub-agent of a licensed IMTO) → stand up a Super-Agent subsidiary (Path B, ₦50m) → become an IMTO payout agent at scale → then MFB (Path C) or PSB (Path D) if and only if scale justifies the ₦1bn–₦5bn capital.** Remittance enters at the agent/sub-agent stage (Path A/B) — lawfully, without an IMTO licence the reader cannot realistically obtain — and deepens as the licensed balance sheet grows.

**Timeline (base case).** Year 0–1: register society, build membership on existing ajo/affinity clusters, raise non-withdrawable share capital, incorporate the operating company with the control lock, launch member savings/credit and (via partnership) basic payments. Year 1–2: Super-Agent licence; agent network; begin remittance payout as sub-agent of a licensed IMTO; commercial core-banking vendor on protective contract terms (data ownership, escrow, multi-homing). Year 2–4: Unit or State MFB licence as membership and balance sheet justify; deepen remittance. Year 4+: National MFB or PSB, and outside capital via the subordinated instrument, only if scale is real. (Costs and gates are detailed in VI.7.)

### Why this architecture and not the alternatives

- **Not a single cooperative (VI.4.6):** because a society "can never itself hold a CBN or IMTO licence" (Part V). A single-entity design is a dead end for regulated remittance. The two-tier structure is the *only* lawful route, and it is domestically proven (NPF).
- **Not investor-controlled or member-withdrawable capital (VI.4.5, VI.4.2):** because that surrenders the cooperative advantage and reopens the demutualisation and horizon problems the whole study warns against. The control lock + non-withdrawable shares + non-voting outside instrument keep economics flowing to capital while control stays with members.
- **Not an open/geographic membership (VI.4.1):** because it destroys the common bond (Hart–Moore heterogeneity cost), invites political capture (VI.2.10), and *collapses the deposit boundary* (Part V, BOFIA §2). The tight bond is the cheapest simultaneous defence against three failure modes.
- **Not build-everything infrastructure (VI.4.8):** because the capital is better spent on the licensed balance sheet; buy/sponsor on protective contract terms, and federate later via the CUSO.
- **Remittance later, not first (established assumption b):** because the reader cannot get an IMTO licence, the naira-payout-agent route requires an existing licensed partner and an agent network, and the member business must generate the trust, data and capital that make the remittance business safe and cheap.

### What it costs, and what it forecloses

*Costs:* the discipline of locking capital (members forgo liquidity and payout — the price of permanence, VI.2.2/VI.2.3); the cash cost of two/three entities, professional directors, mandatory rotating audit, and progressively larger regulatory capital (₦50m → ₦200m → ₦1bn → ₦5bn along the path); and the slower growth that member-first capital implies.

*What it forecloses:* the fast, capital-light pure-fintech route (barred anyway — fintechs cannot hold IMTO licences and an open-deposit model is criminal); a quick investor-led scale-up (the control lock deliberately makes the reader un-sellable, which is the point); and the option of retrofitting control later (Crédit Agricole/Part III: not available — which is why the lock is installed at inception).

*The central trade-off the reader must accept:* **permanence and control in exchange for speed and liquidity.** This design is built to *last and stay a cooperative*, not to exit fast. If the reader's true goal is a quick sale, this is the wrong design — and he should say so now, because almost every recommendation here is calibrated to prevent exactly that outcome.

### Sensitivity analysis

*If members are businesses, not individuals:* Concentrate more on influence-cost and oligarchy defences — cap voting weight, tighten related-party rules, and consider a graduated (still bounded) share structure. Capital is easier (business members contribute more, easing the g\* ceiling and possibly deferring outside capital), so the non-voting investor instrument becomes less urgent. The deposit boundary is *easier* (business members are fewer and clearly bounded). The core two-tier architecture is unchanged.

*If scale is much larger (tens of thousands by year three):* Trigger the delegate assembly earlier and harden it against capture; accelerate the MFB/PSB timeline (scale justifies the capital sooner); and lean harder on the outside-capital instrument, because member capital cannot keep pace. Watch the deposit boundary and common bond most carefully — large scale is where the bond frays and political capture enters (VI.2.10). *If much smaller (few hundred):* stay on Path A/B longer, defer or skip the MFB (a Unit MFB's ₦50m–₦200m may still be reachable; a National MFB is not), and rely on partnership for remittance indefinitely. The society-plus-Super-Agent may be the terminal state, and that is fine.

*If remittance must come earlier:* it does not change the *structure* (the reader still cannot get an IMTO licence), but it front-loads Path A/B — secure a licensed-IMTO principal and a sponsor bank in year 0–1 and build the agent network first, before deepening member credit. The risk is doing remittance-agency before the member base and controls are mature, importing AML/CFT and operational risk early; mitigate with the sponsor's compliance scaffolding and conservative limits.

*If the RH-CRRP reforms land* (the Cooperative Bank of Nigeria, ₦200bn mobilisation, CVN/CoopID, NCSA review — currently **policy intention, not operating fact**): this is upside, not a dependency. If a genuine cooperative apex and central institution emerge, the reader gains the very apex infrastructure (VI.2.3–VI.2.4, VI.2.8) that Nigeria now lacks — a real substitute monitor, a possible protection scheme, and shared rails. The design should be *apex-ready* (build the CUSO and mutual-support vehicles so they can plug in) but must not *assume* the apex, because it does not yet exist. If RH-CRRP delivers a cooperative bank the reader can join as a member-owner, revisit the build-vs-federate calculus in VI.4.8 decisively toward federate.

## VI.6 The bye-law specification

*This is a specification for a Nigerian lawyer to draft; it is not legal advice, and every clause below must be settled by counsel against the current Nigerian Co-operative Societies Act, the relevant State Co-operative Societies Law, CAMA 2020, and CBN/BOFIA requirements.* Because the statute supplies so little, the bye-laws must supply the institution. Each provision below states its purpose and the failure mode it defends against.

1. **Membership and common bond.** Define the common bond tightly and objectively (the affinity/employer/association test), the admission criteria and process, and the grounds for cessation. *Purpose:* preserve homogeneity (Hart–Moore), social collateral, and the member/public deposit boundary. *Defends against:* factional/political capture; degeneration; BOFIA §2 criminal exposure.

2. **Share non-withdrawability (drafted to be unconditional).** "Member shares shall not be redeemed, repaid or refunded in cash under any circumstances while membership subsists; on cessation, shares may only be transferred to an eligible member." No liquidity or capital-adequacy conditions. Establish a member transfer register and a discretionary buyer-of-last-resort funded from a designated pool. *Purpose:* IFRIC 2 equity classification; permanent capital. *Defends against:* insolvency (run risk); horizon problem.

3. **Withdrawable member-deposit account (separate).** A distinct member-savings instrument, repayable, clearly *not* share capital, dealt only with members. *Purpose:* member liquidity without contaminating equity. *Defends against:* IFRIC 2 liability-reclassification of shares; deposit-boundary breach.

4. **Indivisible reserve and dissolution devolution.** A general reserve that is non-distributable in life and, on dissolution, devolves to another registered cooperative society or a cooperative development fund (disinterested devolution), never to members. *Purpose:* asset lock (Part I master variable). *Defends against:* demutualisation; horizon/portfolio problems.

5. **Mandatory retention rate.** A bye-law floor (recommend ≥50% of annual surplus; France's 15% and Italy's up-to-70% as reference points) compulsorily allocated to the indivisible reserve before any patronage or dividend. *Purpose:* organic capital formation, tax-favoured. *Defends against:* under-capitalisation; horizon problem.

6. **Delegate-assembly trigger and composition.** Automatic conversion of the general meeting to an elected delegate assembly on crossing a defined membership threshold (default 1,500, per §43a GenG), with delegate constituencies, terms, and election rules. *Purpose:* workable democracy at scale. *Defends against:* Olson non-monitoring; managerial/board capture.

7. **Separated supervisory committee.** A supervisory/audit committee elected by and reporting to members (or the delegate assembly), independent of and not appointed by the management committee, with defined inspection powers. *Purpose:* internal substitute monitor. *Defends against:* managerial and insider capture; the KUSCCO failure pattern.

8. **Nomination and election rules for genuine contestation.** Open nominations not monopolised by incumbents; term limits and staggered terms; disclosure of candidate interests; secret ballot; a bar on concurrent conflicting offices. *Purpose:* contestable control. *Defends against:* board/insider and political capture; degeneration.

9. **Mandatory external audit with rotation.** Annual audit by an independent, ICAN-registered firm with compulsory rotation (e.g. every 3–5 years) and a direct reporting line to the supervisory committee, not management. *Purpose:* the minimum substitute monitor. *Defends against:* insolvency; fraud (the forged-dead-auditor pattern).

10. **Related-party and insider-lending limits.** Hard caps on lending to directors, committee members, staff and their affiliates (adopt the SASRA red-flag benchmark: aggregate insider lending not to exceed a defined percentage of core capital — SASRA treats ~20% of core capital as the supervisory red line), with disclosure and supervisory-committee approval. *Purpose:* block the classic looting channel. *Defends against:* insider capture; insolvency.

11. **The purpose-lock.** An object clause binding the cooperative to its defined member-service mission, breach of which is a governance and audit standard. *Purpose:* mission integrity. *Defends against:* political capture; degeneration; diversion.

12. **Amendment entrenchment.** Ordinary bye-laws amendable by normal majority, but the *protective* clauses (2, 4, 5, 7, 10, 11, and this clause) amendable only by a super-majority (recommend ≥90% of *all* members, not merely those voting), across two consecutive general meetings with a cooling-off period. *Purpose:* make the protections hard to strip. *Defends against:* capture-by-amendment; demutualisation. (*Caveat, flagged UNKNOWN:* enforceability of bye-law entrenchment against a later duly-registered amendment is untested in Nigeria; reinforce via the operating company's constitution where entrenchment law is firmer.)

13. **Investment and subsidiary power (the §33(c)/§11(2)(e) hinge).** An express clause stating the purposes to which funds may be applied and authorising the society to invest in, promote, and hold a controlling stake in a CAMA company, subject to Committee approval. *Purpose:* put the two-tier structure's statutory footing beyond argument (the NPF precedent rests on §33(c) + §6 + §11(2)(e)). *Defends against:* legal challenge to the structure.

14. **Reserved matters over any subsidiary.** A bye-law and shareholders'-agreement schedule of matters in the operating company requiring cooperative (golden-share) consent: change of control, change of purpose, disposal of any licence, related-party transactions above a threshold, issuance of voting shares, and amendment of the control provisions. *Purpose:* the control lock (VI.2.7). *Defends against:* absorption; demutualisation via the subsidiary.

## VI.7 The sequencing plan

A dated, costed, staged plan from formation to remittance. Figures are indicative planning estimates in 2026 terms; all regulatory capital figures are from Part V. Each stage states what is built, its cost, licences held, governance, leading risks, and **what must be true to proceed.**

**Stage 0 — Formation (Months 0–6).**
*Build:* register the cooperative society (state Registrar; 20+ members; a low-hundreds-of-thousands-of-naira spend on registration, drafting and professional fees for a well-drafted bye-law set — the drafting is where money should be spent, not saved); adopt the full VI.6 bye-laws; open member share (non-withdrawable) and deposit accounts; recruit founding members from existing ajo/affinity clusters.
*Licences held:* none (cooperative registration only).
*Governance:* plenary general meeting; management committee + supervisory committee elected.
*Leading risks:* weak bye-laws (the single biggest avoidable error); premature public dealing (deposit-boundary breach).
*Proceed when:* bye-laws registered; a functioning membership and a growing non-withdrawable-share capital base exist; supervisory committee operating.

**Stage 1 — Partnership operations (Months 6–18) [Path A].**
*Build:* incorporate the CAMA operating company with the control lock and reserved matters installed at inception; sign a sponsor-bank agency agreement and a naira-payout sub-agency agreement with a licensed IMTO; launch member savings/credit and basic payments; adopt a commercial core-banking/payments vendor on protective contract terms (data ownership, exit rights, source-code escrow, multi-homing).
*Cost:* company incorporation and agreements (modest); vendor and working capital (the main spend); no large regulatory capital yet.
*Licences held:* none directly; operates under partners' licences.
*Governance:* company board controlled by the society; professional MD/compliance hires begin.
*Leading risks:* dependency on partners (Part IV — every bought capability is a lever handed to an outsider); AML/CFT exposure via remittance payout; thin controls.
*Proceed when:* member base and transaction volumes justify owning the agent network; controls and compliance are demonstrably adequate; capital sufficient for the next licence.

**Stage 2 — Super-Agent subsidiary (Months 18–30) [Path B].**
*Build:* the CAMA company obtains a **Super-Agent licence (₦50m capital)**; build/roll out the proprietary agent network; deepen remittance payout as an IMTO sub-agent at scale.
*Cost:* ₦50m regulatory capital + network build.
*Licences held:* Super-Agent.
*Governance:* fit-and-proper directors; formal risk and audit functions in the company.
*Leading risks:* operational and agent-fraud risk; scaling faster than controls.
*Proceed when:* the agent network is profitable and controlled; membership/balance sheet supports MFB capital; a genuine deposit business case exists.

**Stage 3 — Microfinance bank (Years 3–5) [Path C].**
*Build:* obtain a Unit (Tier 2 ₦50m / Tier 1 ₦200m) or **State MFB (₦1bn)** licence as scale dictates; the MFB becomes the group's deposit-taking, prudentially-supervised core — and the reflected substitute monitor (VI.2.3).
*Cost:* ₦200m–₦1bn regulatory capital.
*Licences held:* MFB (+ Super-Agent).
*Governance:* full CBN prudential governance on the MFB; cooperative control preserved via the holding structure.
*Leading risks:* the capital ceiling (Part II — organic member capital cannot fund this alone, so outside capital via the non-voting instrument likely enters here); managing a regulated bank with a cooperative parent (the Co-operative Bank scaffolding lesson).
*Proceed when:* the MFB is safely capitalised and supervised; remittance and payments volumes justify a wider licence.

**Stage 4 — National MFB or PSB, and remittance at depth (Year 5+) [Path C/D].**
*Build:* **National MFB (₦5bn)** or **PSB (₦5bn)** if and only if scale justifies; remittance deepens across the licensed balance sheet; consider the CUSO/shared-services and mutual-support vehicles as a cooperative cluster forms; become apex-ready for any RH-CRRP cooperative bank.
*Cost:* ₦5bn regulatory capital (outside capital essential, via the non-voting subordinated instrument — control lock intact).
*Licences held:* National MFB or PSB (+ Super-Agent).
*Leading risks:* dilution pressure on control (resist via the lock); scale-driven fraying of the common bond and political-capture risk (VI.2.10); the mirror risk of the cooperative parent misusing control.
*Proceed when:* only when the business genuinely needs national reach and can hold ₦5bn safely — not for prestige.

## VI.8 Early-warning indicators — the degeneration dashboard

The four failure modes (demutualisation, absorption, degeneration, insolvency) announce themselves in metrics the reader can collect himself. Track these monthly/quarterly; each has a threshold that should trigger board and supervisory-committee action.

- **Member participation and contestation rate.** AGM/delegate-election turnout; number of contested board seats. *Threshold:* if contested seats fall toward zero or turnout below ~10–15%, degeneration/capture is advancing. Uncontested elections are the first symptom of capture.
- **Board and committee tenure.** Average and maximum director tenure; frequency of turnover. *Threshold:* any director beyond the term limit, or an average tenure that keeps rising, signals entrenchment.
- **Insider-lending ratio.** Aggregate insider/related-party loans ÷ core capital. *Threshold:* the SASRA red line ~20% of core capital; well before that (say 10%), scrutinise. Rising insider lending is the classic looting signature (KUSCCO).
- **Member/non-member revenue mix.** Share of income from members vs non-members (and, for the company, member-linked vs external business). *Threshold:* drift toward majority non-member business signals both degeneration *and* deposit-boundary danger; investigate above ~50% non-member (and note Italy's 50% test for cooperative tax status as a conceptual benchmark).
- **Operating-company pricing vs member interest.** The gap between the company's prices/terms to members and the best available market terms. *Threshold:* if the company systematically prices *against* members to benefit outside shareholders, the cooperative advantage is being expropriated — a leading indicator of absorption/demutualisation in substance.
- **Reserve growth vs redeemable claims.** Indivisible reserve trend vs withdrawable deposits and any redeemable instruments. *Threshold:* reserves must grow at least in line with the balance sheet; if redeemable claims outrun locked capital, insolvency/run risk rises.
- **Retention-rate compliance.** Actual surplus allocated to the indivisible reserve vs the bye-law floor. *Threshold:* any year below the mandated floor is a governance red flag.
- **Audit findings on the purpose-lock and controls.** Number and severity of audit and supervisory-committee findings; auditor-rotation compliance; any audit-independence concern. *Threshold:* repeat findings, deferred remediation, or resistance to rotation are precursors of the KUSCCO pattern — treat as urgent.
- **Common-bond integrity.** Share of members who genuinely meet the common-bond test. *Threshold:* erosion here is the upstream cause of most of the above and of political capture.

## VI.9 What would falsify this design

Intellectual honesty requires naming what would show the recommendation wrong, and what to do in each case.

- **If a Nigerian court holds that cooperative bye-law entrenchment (VI.6 cl.12) is unenforceable against a later duly-registered amendment.** Then the asset lock and control provisions are only as strong as an ordinary majority allows, and the whole self-entrenchment strategy is undermined. *Response:* relocate the protections decisively into the operating company's constitution and shareholders' agreement (where CAMA entrenchment and contract law are firmer), and treat the society as a control vehicle rather than the primary locus of the lock.
- **If the CBN or the courts hold that the two-tier structure impermissibly circumvents the deposit boundary — i.e. that a society-controlled licensed entity dealing with the public re-attributes the public deposits to the society.** This would invalidate the core route. *Response:* the NPF precedent strongly suggests otherwise (the licensed CAMA bank lawfully serves the public), but if the position changes, ring-fence more aggressively (independent minority, arm's-length governance) or reconsider a non-controlling stake.
- **If §33(c)/§11(2)(e) are read narrowly to bar a controlling equity stake in a CAMA company.** *Response:* seek an express Registrar approval/comfort, or restructure through an intermediate holding company; but the NPF precedent (62.6%) makes this unlikely.
- **If the assumed common bond cannot be maintained at scale** (membership becomes de facto open). Then the deposit-boundary and capture defences fail. *Response:* segment into multiple bonded cooperatives (the Rabobank/Netherlands religious-segmentation model) rather than one diffuse body.
- **If outside investors will not buy non-voting, control-poor instruments at a viable price.** Then Path C/D (₦1bn–₦5bn) is unfundable without ceding control. *Response:* stay at Stage 2/3 (Super-Agent + Unit/State MFB) as the terminal state, and grow more slowly — do not cede control to fund scale.
- **If remittance economics collapse** (margins compressed by CBN policy, or the naira-payout-agent channel is closed to cooperatives). *Response:* the design does not depend on remittance (it is the *eventual*, not entry, product); fall back on member credit/savings/payments, which stand alone.
- **If RH-CRRP creates a genuine cooperative apex bank.** This does not falsify the design but *changes the optimum:* federate rather than build (VI.4.8), and join the apex for the substitute-monitor, protection-scheme and shared-rail benefits Nigeria currently lacks.

## VI.10 Ten propositions on transplanting cooperative institutions

1. **A cooperative feature transplants only if the institutions it depends on already exist, can be built, or can be substituted.** The form always travels; the function often does not. This is the Guinnane–Colvin law, and it governs everything.

2. **Classify before you copy.** Self-contained features (adopt by drafting), institution-dependent features (need a federation/supervisor/statute), and culture-dependent features (need homogeneity/trust) demand entirely different strategies. Confusing the three is how founders reproduce the Irish failure.

3. **The features you most need transplant worst.** The asset lock, the substitute monitor and the protection scheme are the strongest cooperative defences and the most institution-dependent — and Nigeria's institutions are the thinnest. Plan to *reconstruct their functions from self-contained parts.*

4. **Nigeria's permissive statute is a workshop, not a wall.** Because the Act mandates little and forbids little, an informed founder can build, by bye-law, a stronger cooperative than a prescriptive statute would allow — and a naive founder will build nothing and degenerate.

5. **The cheapest win is real: non-withdrawable member shares, drafted unconditionally, convert fragile liability into permanent equity at zero control cost.** Nigeria achieves by charter what Kenya achieves by regulation, and IFRIC 2 does the rest.

6. **What you cannot lock in the society, lock in the company.** Nigeria's asset lock rests on self-entrenchment and is weaker than Quebec's or Italy's; reinforce it by relocating value and control into the CAMA operating company, where company law and shareholders' agreements are firmer.

7. **Install the control lock before investors hold the economics — retrofitting is not available.** Crédit Agricole's SAS Rue La Boétie (62.45% at end-2024) and Nigeria's own NPF precedent show the two-tier "economics to investors, control to members" structure works; but only if the majority, golden share and reserved matters exist at inception.

8. **Cooperative ownership of a licensed bank is not a hypothesis in Nigeria — it is a fact.** The Nigeria Police Co-operative Society's ~62.6% of NPF Microfinance Bank, resting on §33(c) + §6 + §11(2)(e), is the template; the reader's task is to repeat it deliberately and with better governance.

9. **Build on ajo/esusu, do not fight it — but formalise the bond, never the openness.** Nigeria already possesses the Raiffeisen social-collateral culture that Ireland lacked; the design must capture it through a tight, bounded membership, because the same openness that makes informal ROSCAs work makes formal deposit-taking criminal.

10. **Political capture is the failure mode you cannot draft away — you can only select against it and layer defences.** A tight common bond, locked capital, contested elections, a separated supervisor, a purpose-lock and the reflected discipline of a licensed subsidiary together buy resistance; none alone suffices, and no clause substitutes for choosing a membership in which the culture of independence already lives.
