# APPENDIX A — JURISDICTION MATRIX

*This appendix is a lookup instrument. Six jurisdictions — Germany, the Netherlands, Quebec/Canada, the United States, Kenya, Nigeria — are compared across nine variable-sets. France, Italy and the UK appear only where the analysis requires. Prose is confined to short interpretive notes beneath each table.*

**Note.** Only Nigeria treats the cooperative as expressly *outside* the general companies statute while providing almost no prudential apparatus of its own — the exemption from CAMA (confirmed by multiple Nigerian legal commentaries citing the Act) **[CF]** removes filing and charge-registration discipline without substituting a bank-grade regime. This is the single most consequential structural fact for the reader: the Nigerian cooperative-society form is lightly supervised by design, which is why Part VI recommended the operating fintech be a CAMA company with the cooperative sitting above or beside it.

## A.2 Member rights

| Variable | Germany | Netherlands | Quebec | US | Kenya | Nigeria |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| What the member owns | Geschäftsguthaben (paid-in share balance) | membership + certificates where issued | qualifying + permanent shares | the "share" = the deposit/par account | share capital (ownership) distinct from deposits | shares per bye-law |
| Deposits vs ownership | sharply separated | separated | separated | **fused** — the share IS a deposit | sharply separated (BOSA share vs FOSA deposit) | separated by bye-law |

**Note.** The US fusion of share and deposit is the analytically decisive oddity: because the member's ownership stake *is* an insured deposit, the "capital" is redeemable on demand and offers almost no permanent loss-absorbing base — which is precisely why the US had to build subordinated debt/secondary capital (Appendix B, instrument 7) as a bolt-on.

## A.3 Exit and redemption — the decisive matrix

| Variable | Germany | Netherlands | Quebec | US | Kenya | Nigeria |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Share redeemable or transfer-only? | redeemable (paid out on exit) | depends on instrument | permanent shares non-redeemable except at issuer discretion | redeemable (=withdraw deposit) | **non-withdrawable share; transfer-only** | bye-law default (usually redeemable) |
| Notice period | to year-end, extendable to 5 yrs by charter | per statute/charter | n/a for permanent shares | on demand (subject to par) | membership duration; transfer only | bye-law |
| Board discretion to defer/refuse | limited; §73(2) six-month payout of Geschäftsguthaben | yes for certificates | yes (discretionary redemption) | limited | must find transferee | bye-law |
| What departing member receives | paid-in share balance only | par/market per instrument | par (permanent shares not redeemed on exit) | par (the deposit balance) | par via transfer | par per bye-law |
| Claim on accumulated reserves on exit | **NONE — §73(2) GenG** | none (default) | none | none | none | none (default) |
| Claim on reserves on dissolution | possible per charter (no statutory lock) | per charter | disinterested devolution (locked) | net worth locked procedurally (NCUA) | to statutory reserve rules | per bye-law/registrar |

**Note — answer the five-second question.** *What happens to a member's stake on exit in Kenya versus Germany?* In **Kenya**, the ownership share is **non-withdrawable**: the member cannot demand cash; he must find someone to transfer it to, and receives at most par. Kenya Law's text of the Non-Deposit-Taking Regulations 2020 defines core capital as "fully paid up members' shares... not meant to be expended unless on liquidation" **[CF]**. In **Germany**, the member is paid out his *Geschäftsguthaben* (paid-in balance) within six months under §73(2) GenG, but — critically — "the member has no claim to the reserves and other assets of the Cooperative" (§73(2) GenG, confirmed in the statutory text and academic commentary) **[CF]**. Both jurisdictions protect the reserve; they differ on whether the par stake itself is liquid. Kenya freezes the stake and protects reserves; Germany liberates the stake but ring-fences the reserve. This is the cleanest illustration in the whole study of the horizon problem being solved two different ways.

## A.5 The asset lock — the master variable

| Variable | Germany | Netherlands | Quebec | US | Kenya | Nigeria |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reserves indivisible? | **No statutory lock** | charter-dependent | **Yes — strongest** | procedurally, via net-worth rules | statutory reserve, not full lock | weak/charter |
| Source of any lock | charter only | charter | statute (disinterested devolution) | NCUA conversion rules | SACCO Act reserve rules | bye-law |
| On conversion | no statutory bar to distributing | charter | reserves cannot be appropriated | NCUA polices net worth | n/a (no conversion route) | registrar discretion |
| On dissolution | charter governs (§§91–92 GenG) | charter | devolution to another co-op/charity | net worth to members/successor | to reserve/other co-ops | registrar |
| Disinterested devolution rule | No | No | **Yes** | No | partial | No |

**Note — carrying forward Part I's counter-intuitive finding.** Germany, the jurisdiction with the *lowest* demutualisation rate, has **no statutory asset lock** (§§91–92 GenG leave dissolution proceeds to the charter) **[CF]**, while Quebec, with the *strongest* lock, achieves its protection by statute. The US locks net worth only **procedurally**, through NCUA's control of the conversion vote and disclosure, not through an indivisible-reserve rule. The lesson Part I drew — and this appendix confirms — is that the asset lock is neither necessary nor sufficient to prevent demutualisation; the substitute monitor (A.7) does more work. France and Italy (indivisible-reserve jurisdictions, not treated as full columns here) show the lock can coexist with either survival (France) or legislated conversion (Italy 2015), reinforcing that the lock alone does not determine outcomes.

## A.7 Audit and supervision — the substitute monitor

| Variable | Germany | Netherlands | Quebec | US | Kenya | Nigeria |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Who audits | **compulsory audit federation (Prüfungsverband)** | external auditor | external + AMF | external + NCUA exam | SASRA-approved auditor | registrar/external |
| Audit federation membership compulsory? | **Yes** | No | No | No (NCUA exam instead) | No | No |

**Substitute-monitor strength ranking (strongest → weakest), carrying forward Part III:**
1. **Germany** — compulsory Prüfungsverband audit + BVR mutual protection + BaFin. The gold standard.
2. **United States** — NCUA examination + NCUSIF federal insurance.
3. **Quebec** — AMF + Desjardins internal federation monitoring + security fund.
4. **Netherlands** — DNB/ECB for the bank; strong but centralised on one institution (Rabobank).
5. **Kenya** — SASRA covers deposit-taking SACCOs credibly, but **KUSCCO sat outside the perimeter** (C.4) — a coverage gap that proved catastrophic.
6. **Nigeria** — effectively no prudential monitor for cooperatives; registrar capacity is thin. **Weakest by a wide margin.**

This ranking is the study's best single predictor of degeneration/insolvency risk. The Nigerian founder is operating in the weakest-monitor jurisdiction of the six.

# APPENDIX B — CAPITAL-INSTRUMENTS CATALOGUE

## B.0 How to read this catalogue

Part II dissected eleven instruments on a **nine-point template**: (1) legal character; (2) who may issue; (3) who may hold; (4) redeemability; (5) IFRIC 2 / IAS 32 accounting classification; (6) regulatory-capital treatment; (7) cost in money (coupon/dividend); (8) **cost in control** (governance transferred); (9) Nigerian availability. The organising idea is the **capital–control frontier**: every instrument trades off external capital raised against member control surrendered. The art of cooperative capital structuring is to move up the capital axis while giving away as little of the control axis as possible. The Rabobank Certificate is the frontier's most celebrated point because it raises billions with near-zero governance transfer; the listed vehicle over a mutual base (Crédit Agricole) raises more but concedes a permanent investor constituency.

## B.2 The eleven instruments — one-page entries

**1. The ordinary member share (baseline).** *Mechanism:* a subscribed par-value stake conferring one vote regardless of size. *Worked example:* a Nigerian multipurpose society requiring ₦10,000 minimum share subscription per member; 1,000 members = ₦10m paid-in capital, no member above the 20% cap. *Drafting point:* to count as CET1/equity under IFRIC 2 the co-op must have an **unconditional right to refuse redemption**; if the member can demand cash, it is a liability. *Nigerian verdict:* **available now**, but redeemable-at-par shares are weak permanent capital.

**2. Non-withdrawable member shares (Kenya) — Part II's cheapest win.** *Mechanism:* the ownership share cannot be redeemed for cash; the exiting member must transfer it to an incoming member. Kenya Law defines core capital as "fully paid up members' shares... not meant to be expended unless on liquidation" **[CF]**. *Worked example:* Kenyan deposit-taking SACCOs build core capital from non-withdrawable shares distinct from withdrawable FOSA deposits; the share funds the balance sheet permanently while deposits fund liquidity. *Drafting points:* (i) IFRIC 2 requires an **unconditional prohibition** on redemption — a discretionary refusal is not enough; (ii) a **transfer market** must exist or the share is illiquid and unattractive. *Nigerian verdict:* **achievable by bye-law** — the single highest-value, lowest-cost reform for the reader, converting redeemable shares into permanent capital at the stroke of a drafting pen.

**3. Permanent shares (Quebec parts permanentes / parts de capital).** *Mechanism:* member-held shares redeemable only at the issuer's discretion, designed as perpetual capital. *Worked example:* Desjardins launched a permanent-share issue targeting CAD 750m in 2009, distributed across its caisse network, paying 4.25% for 2009 (La Presse, 2009) **[CF]** — attractive relative to term deposits then paying 0.4–2.1%. *The Basel III point:* the older redeemable design **failed Basel III's permanence test** and had to be re-engineered as non-redeemable/discretionary; Desjardins' filings show permanent-share redemptions (e.g., $275m redeemed in 2017) managed at the institution's discretion, and F-capital shares issued as the compliant successor instrument, with $244m of F capital shares issued in 2017 net of certain items **[CF]**. *Nigerian verdict:* **synthesise by bye-law/contract** — Quebec proves permanence must be engineered against the accounting/regulatory test, not assumed.

**4. Cooperative preference shares (parts privilégiées; CoBank's US$300m 2001 preferred).** *Mechanism:* preferred equity paying a fixed return, usually non-voting, issued to members or (in some systems) outside investors. *Worked example:* CoBank, a US Farm Credit System cooperative bank, issued **US$300 million of preferred stock in 2001** (amount and year corroborated by secondary sources but not confirmed from a primary CoBank filing — flag **[CL]**), part of a repeated pattern of preferred issuance whose well-documented later series include a $375m Series I in April 2016 at 6.25% and a $300m Series L in April 2024 at 7.25% **[CF]**. *Drafting point:* preference shares can carry capital credit without votes, but dividend cost is real and cumulative-preferred terms can bind. *Nigerian verdict:* **by contract in a CAMA company** — not available in the society itself.

**5. Rabobank Certificates — the frontier's showcase.** *Mechanism:* deeply subordinated perpetual participations, €25 nominal each, issued via the Stichting Administratiekantoor Rabobank Certificaten, qualifying as **CET1** with fully discretionary distributions. *Worked example with figures:* listed on Euronext Amsterdam on **27 January 2014** (Euronext press release; De Brauw Blackstone Westbroek acted as counsel on a listing of up to 237,961,365 certificates) **[CF]**; the January 2017 tap issued **60 million new certificates at 108% of nominal, raising €1.5bn, 2.4× oversubscribed** (Rabobank Investor Relations) **[CF]**; later counts put roughly **313 million certificates** outstanding (~€7.8bn), with Rabobank itself holding a portion after a 2024 tender. Distribution policy referenced the 10-year Dutch state loan yield plus a spread with a **6.5% floor**. *The point:* certificate-holders get an attractive, tradable, loss-absorbing instrument and **almost no governance rights** — the purest example of moving up the capital axis without moving along the control axis. *Nigerian verdict:* **not available** — requires a listing venue and a central cooperative bank; aspirational model only.

**6. Subordinated member deposits (Kenyan BOSA structure).** *Mechanism:* deposits ranking below ordinary creditors; **not** an ownership stake. *Worked example:* Kenyan SACCO BOSA (back-office) balances function as quasi-capital but remain **refundable on exit**, creating the fragility Part II flagged — the "capital" can walk out the door when members leave. *Drafting point:* to count as Tier 2 the subordination and minimum maturity must be explicit and the refund right constrained. *Nigerian verdict:* **by contract** — usable, but the refundability trap must be closed in drafting.

**7. Subordinated debt / secondary capital (US, NCUA 12 CFR Part 702 Subpart D).** *Mechanism:* subordinated notes counting toward regulatory capital for eligible credit unions. *Regulatory history:* the final rule was adopted December 2020, effective **1 January 2022**, permitting LICUs, complex credit unions (≥$500m assets) and new credit unions to issue **[CF]**. *Uptake:* by end-2022, **154 credit unions had issued $3.4 billion** in subordinated debt (NCUA Chairman Harper) **[CF]**; the figure had grown further by 2025. *Drafting point:* undercapitalised issuers are barred from paying interest — the instrument is loss-absorbing by design. *Nigerian verdict:* **by contract in the CAMA company** — the closest off-the-shelf template for the reader's operating vehicle.

**8. A listed vehicle over a mutual base (Crédit Agricole).** *Mechanism:* a listed holding company (Crédit Agricole S.A., "CASA") sits over the mutual regional banks, which retain control through a holding vehicle. *Worked example with figures:* **SAS Rue La Boétie held 62.45% of CASA at year-end 2024** (shareholder register) **[CF]**; the 2001 IPO priced at **€16.60** under COB visa 01-1434 and was **predominantly a secondary sell-down** — of the ~€4.8bn transaction only a modest slice (~€244m) was genuinely new CASA equity **[CL]** (figure carried from Part II; the headline IPO size is well documented, the new-money split less so). *The real value:* not the one-off injection but the creation of a **repeatable capital-raising machine** — a permanent listed currency the group can tap again. *Cost in control:* **high** — a permanent public investor constituency and market discipline enter the group forever. *Nigerian verdict:* **by contract via a CAMA company that could later list** — the long-horizon aspiration, with the Crédit Agricole caution that control must be locked in a holding vehicle *before* listing.

**9. Retained patronage and revolving funds (US agricultural model).** *Mechanism:* surplus allocated to members in proportion to patronage but retained as equity, revolved (paid out) on a cycle. *Worked example:* US agricultural cooperatives distinguish **qualified** allocations (member pays tax now, co-op deducts) from **non-qualified** (co-op pays tax, member later), and run **base-capital plans** sizing each member's equity to current use. *Drafting point:* the revolving promise must not create an unconditional redemption obligation or it becomes a liability under IAS 32. *Nigerian verdict:* **by bye-law** — a strong, culturally-familiar way to build permanent capital from surplus rather than external investors.

**10. Investor / non-user members (Germany's 2006 GenG reform).** *Mechanism:* the 2006 reform permitted "investing members" (investierende Mitglieder) who provide capital without being users. *Governance guard-rail:* **§8(2) GenG caps investing members at no more than one quarter of the supervisory board** — capital admitted, control ring-fenced **[CF]**. *Worked example:* an eG can admit a strategic investor for capital while statute prevents that investor from dominating the Aufsichtsrat. *Nigerian verdict:* **not available** — Nigerian cooperative law has no investing-member category; would require a CAMA vehicle or statutory reform.

**11. Institutional protection schemes as a capital substitute (Germany's BVR).** *Mechanism:* a network mutual-guarantee scheme where members stand behind each other, so intra-network exposures carry **0% risk weight** (CRR Art. 113(7)) and the network can be treated on a consolidated basis (Art. 49(3)). *Worked example:* the BVR institutional protection scheme is described by the sector as "the oldest bank protection scheme in Germany," backing 735 primary banks in 2022 (falling to 697 by 2023) **[CF]**; it is the reason German cooperative banks almost never fail *individually* — the network absorbs them. *Nigerian verdict:* **not available** — requires a dense network of institutions and a credible mutual fund; a long-horizon collective project, not an individual founder's tool.

## B.3 The capital–control frontier, plotted

Ordered from **maximum control / minimum external capital** to **maximum external capital / control surrendered**:

1. **Retained patronage / revolving funds** (9) — capital from surplus, zero external control. *US ag co-ops.*
2. **Non-withdrawable member shares** (2) — permanent internal capital, one-member-one-vote intact. *Kenyan SACCOs.*
3. **Permanent shares** (3) — member capital, discretionary redemption. *Desjardins.*
4. **Subordinated member deposits** (6) — member liability capital, no votes. *Kenyan BOSA.*
5. **Subordinated debt / secondary capital** (7) — external liability capital, no votes. *US credit unions.*
6. **Cooperative preference shares** (4) — preferred equity, usually non-voting. *CoBank.*
7. **Institutional protection scheme** (11) — capital *substitute* via mutual guarantee; control shared across network. *BVR.*
8. **Rabobank Certificates** (5) — large external CET1, **near-zero** governance transfer. *Rabobank.*
9. **Investor / non-user members** (10) — external equity, control capped by statute (¼ supervisory board). *German eG.*
10. **Listed vehicle over mutual base** (8) — largest external capital, **permanent** investor constituency and market discipline. *Crédit Agricole.*

The instrument's position is not fixed by type but by drafting: a badly-drafted non-withdrawable share (refundable in practice) collapses toward a deposit; a well-drafted certificate holds CET1 status with no votes.

| Can use **today** (in the society) | Must **synthesise by contract** (in the CAMA operating company) | **Unavailable** (needs a listing, a network, or law reform) |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary member share (1) | Cooperative preference shares (4) | Rabobank-type listed certificate (5) |

**Carrying forward the 20% cap and Part VI's architecture:** because no single member may hold more than 20% of the society's shares **[CF]**, and because the society is a weak-monitor vehicle, the recommended structure is a **two-body architecture**: (i) the cooperative society holding member ownership and votes, using non-withdrawable shares and retained patronage to build permanent internal capital; and (ii) a **CAMA operating company** — the licensed fintech — that raises preference shares, subordinated debt and (eventually) listed equity, with control locked in a holding vehicle before any external capital enters. The society governs; the company raises.

# APPENDIX C — FAILURE CATALOGUE

*The most important appendix. Cooperative literature is written by advocates; this is the counterweight — a systematic catalogue of how member-owned financial institutions actually die, coded so the mechanisms are legible rather than merely lamented, and tested against Cook's five property-rights problems.*

## C.0 Method

Every case is coded on three axes:
- **Failure mode** (Prologue §0.3.8): **demutualisation** (event) · **absorption** (transaction) · **degeneration** (process) · **insolvency** (event).
- **Capture type** where relevant (Part III's seven): managerial · board/insider · factional (net-saver vs net-borrower) · staff/employee · regional/constituency · external · **political**.
- **Cook's five property-rights problems** (Cook, *American Journal of Agricultural Economics* 77(5), 1995, pp. 1153–1159): **free-rider (common property), horizon, portfolio, control, influence costs.**

**Selection criteria and admitted biases.** Cases are selected for documentary depth and analytical value, not representativeness. This produces two biases the reader must hold in mind. First, **survivorship bias**: failed cooperatives leave thinner records than survivors, so the catalogue understates failure frequency. Second, and more serious, **reporting bias by jurisdiction**: failures in well-documented jurisdictions (UK, US, Germany, Italy) are richly recorded, while **Nigerian cooperative failures are systematically under-recorded** — there is no NCUA-equivalent dataset, no compulsory audit federation, and little forensic journalism. The catalogue therefore **over-represents Anglo-American and European cases and under-represents the reader's own jurisdiction**. This is not a minor caveat: it means the base rate of failure in Nigeria is almost certainly *higher* than the visible record suggests, and the reader should treat the absence of documented Nigerian cases as ignorance, not safety. Say it plainly: **we do not know how often Nigerian cooperatives fail, and that ignorance is itself a risk factor [U].**

## C.1 Demutualisation cases (the event)

**The UK building society wave.** Following the Building Societies Act 1986, which permitted conversion on a 75% member vote, a cascade of conversions occurred: **Abbey National (1989)** — the first and the trigger; **Cheltenham & Gloucester (1994–95, into Lloyds)**; **Halifax, Woolwich, Alliance & Leicester, Northern Rock and Bristol & West (all 1997)**; and **Bradford & Bingley (2000)** **[CF]**. Speculative "carpet-baggers" opened accounts purely to force conversion votes and collect windfalls.

- *Coding:* demutualisation event; **factional capture** (short-horizon members voting to liquidate accumulated reserves for a windfall) and **external capture** (carpet-baggers); Cook problems: **horizon** (members with no long stake voting to cash out) and **free-rider/common-property** (accumulated reserves built by generations appropriated by a transient membership).
- **The critical follow-up finding.** *No demutualised UK building society survived the 2007–09 crisis as an independent institution.* Abbey National and Alliance & Leicester were absorbed by Santander; Woolwich by Barclays; **Northern Rock and Bradford & Bingley failed outright and were nationalised** (their shareholders wiped out). Meanwhile **Nationwide, which refused to convert (rejecting demutualisation attempts in 1998 and 2001), came through the crisis without state capital [CF].** Vince Cable called the wave "one of the greatest acts of economic vandalism in modern times." *The Prologue's selection-bias caveat applies:* this is a striking correlation, but the converters may have differed systematically from Nationwide in risk appetite, not merely in ownership form — the ownership change and the risk change are confounded **[CL]**.

**US mutual-to-stock conversions.** US mutual savings banks and mutual insurers demutualised in large numbers across the 1980s–2000s; and **US credit unions convert to mutual savings banks under 12 CFR Part 708a**. The scale is modest but instructive. Per the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco (Economic Letter, June 2007, *Credit Unions, Conversions, and Capital*), **33 credit unions with total assets of $7.7 billion converted or merged with non-credit unions from 1995 through June 2007 — "comprising only about 1% of total credit union assets"** **[CF]**; and of the 20 straight conversions from 1995 through 2003, "17 have already issued some stock or have merged with another stock-issuing institution" **[CF]**. (NCUA's own 2005 tally of a narrower window — 27 conversions/mergers 1995–2004, of which 20 became stock banks or merged into existing institutions — is consistent, differing only in period and definition **[CF]**.) The **"second-step windfall" problem** is the mechanism NCUA has repeatedly flagged: conversion to a mutual savings bank is "often the first step in a two-step process to convert to a stock-issuing bank," at which point, in NCUA's mandated disclosure language (Federal Register, 71 FR 36946, 2006), "the executives of the institution profit by obtaining stock far in excess of that available to the institution's members" **[CF]**. NCUA's 2005 legal opinion is blunter: the mutual-thrift voting method "allows insiders and others with large deposits to control a conversion vote that will reap monetary rewards for them as individuals" **[CF]**.
- *Coding:* demutualisation event; **managerial and board/insider capture**; Cook problems: **control** (managers exploiting weak member oversight) and **influence costs** (insiders steering the vote).
- *Current development to flag:* as of early 2026 NCUA had *proposed* rolling back several Subpart A disclosure requirements — a deregulatory move not yet finalised **[CF, pending]**.

**Legislated demutualisation — a distinct mechanism.** Italy's reform of **20 January 2015** (decree-law, converted by Parliament March 2015) **compelled the largest banche popolari — those with assets above €8bn — to convert to joint-stock form within 18 months**, affecting **exactly 10 banks** (US export.gov *Italy — Banking Systems*; Co-operative News) **[CF]**. This is categorically different from a member vote: the members did not choose to demutualise; the *state* abolished the one-member-one-vote form by statute, citing governance dysfunction (outvoting incumbent management could require the support of as many as 100,000 members).
- *Coding:* demutualisation event by **political capture** (the state as external actor); Cook problems: **control** and **influence costs** (the one-head-one-vote rule had entrenched management and blocked accountability, which is *why* the state intervened). The lesson: the cooperative form's own governance weakness can invite its legislative abolition — degeneration and demutualisation can be causally linked.

## C.2 Absorption cases (the quiet failure)

**The quantitative story first.** Absorption — merger into a larger institution — is the modal end of a sub-scale cooperative, and it is almost never coded as "failure."
- **US credit unions fell from a peak of 23,866 in 1969 to 4,287 at year-end 2025**, even as membership *rose* to **144.7 million** (NCUA Q4 2025 System Performance Data; 1969 peak per FRBSF and the peer-reviewed literature) **[CF]**. That is an ~82% decline in institutions alongside massive membership growth — consolidation, not sectoral collapse, but the disappearance of thousands of individual cooperatives.
- **German cooperative banks** consolidated from **771 in 2021 to 735 in 2022** (BVR Consolidated Financial Statements 2022), then to **697 in 2023** (Deutsche Bundesbank, *Changes in bank office statistics in 2023*: "a drop of just over 5%"), and ~669 by 2024 (IMF/FRED) **[CF]**, at roughly 4–5% annual consolidation.
- The **UK Building Societies Association** represents a much-reduced sector after decades of mergers and the conversions of C.1.

**Named transactions.**
- **Britannia into the Co-operative Bank (2009)** — the merger that planted the bad-loan book which detonated in 2013 (C.3). Absorption here *caused* a later near-death.
- **Nationwide / Virgin Money (completed 1 October 2024)** — a £2.9bn cash acquisition of a *bank* by a *mutual*, creating the UK's second-largest savings-and-mortgage provider; notably, Nationwide's board maintained a member vote was not required despite member petitions **[CF]**.
- **Coventry Building Society / The Co-operative Bank (completed 1 January 2025)** — a £780m acquisition returning the Co-op Bank to mutual ownership **[CF]**.

*Coding:* absorption transaction; **managerial capture** (management pursuing scale/self-preservation) and, in the Nationwide case, a **control** problem (members denied a vote on a transformative deal); Cook problems: **portfolio** (heterogeneous members unable to agree on risk/scale, so the board resolves it by merging) and **horizon**. Absorption is the *quiet* failure mode: it is reported as "expanded services" or "stronger together," rarely as the end of a cooperative. For the reader, the danger is that sub-scale is a slow death sentence — a small digital cooperative that cannot reach efficient scale will be absorbed, and the absorption will be described as a success.

## C.3 Degeneration cases (the process)

**The paradigm: the UK Co-operative Bank, 2013–14.** The bank revealed a **£1.5bn capital shortfall** (PRA assessment, June 2013, reaffirmed August 2013) **[CF]**, driven largely by loan losses inherited from the Britannia merger (~£900m), a scrapped IT system (~£300m), PPI (~£200m) and the collapsed Lloyds-branches ("Verde") deal (~£100m). The resolution was a **bondholder bail-in**: **the Co-operative Group's equity stake fell to 30%, "with the remaining 70% being owned by the former holders of the Bank's lower tier 2 debt"** (DBRS/Morningstar) **[CF]**, and the Group's stake subsequently fell to nil, with US hedge funds (Silver Point, GoldenTree, York, Perry) taking control — **all without any member vote to demutualise**.
- *The reviews.* The **Kelly Review (30 April 2014)** dissected the Britannia merger and IT failures. **Lord Myners' Report of the Independent Governance Review (7 May 2014)** found a "democratic deficit" — ordinary members had "surprisingly weak constitutional rights" — and branded the Britannia and Somerfield acquisitions "breathtakingly value-destructive," noting the Group had lost roughly £3.5bn of net worth in four years and calling the board "manifestly dysfunctional" **[CF]**. The **Treasury Select Committee's "Project Verde" report (HC 728, 21 October 2014)** concluded, in the words of Chairman Andrew Tyrie MP, that the bank's board structure "was an accident waiting to happen and badly let down Co-op members and customers... Co-op Bank's governance structure was not fit for purpose for any bank, let alone one bidding to become a major challenger in the UK market" **[CF]**.
- *Coding:* degeneration process culminating in loss of member control; **board/insider capture** (an unqualified, self-perpetuating board) and **managerial capture**; Cook problems: **control** (members structurally unable to monitor a three-tier governance system) and **influence costs** (regional-constituency politics producing directors "without the necessary qualifications"). This is the case the reader should internalise most deeply: **a cooperative can lose everything that makes it a cooperative — member ownership and control — without ever holding a demutualisation vote.** Degeneration is death by a thousand governance cuts, and it is the mode most invisible to members until it is complete.

**The contested Rabobank 2016 centralisation.** Rabobank's 2016 reorganisation merged its 100-plus local member banks into a single cooperative entity (Coöperatieve Rabobank U.A.). *One reading* (degeneration): local member control was hollowed out in favour of a central bureaucracy. *The counter-reading:* centralisation was a rational response to regulatory capital and efficiency pressures that preserved the cooperative form while professionalising it. The literature does not agree; carry this forward as **[CL]**.

**The US "large credit unions behave like banks" evidence.** Jordan van Rijn and co-authors find credit unions *do* retain distinct objectives — per van Rijn, Zeng & Hueth, *Do credit unions have distinct objectives?* (*Annals of Public and Cooperative Economics* 94(1), 2023), credit union CEOs receive **"approximately two-and-a-half times less performance-based compensation relative to CEOs of similarly sized community banks,"** with bank CEOs earning **"approximately 15% to 20% more total compensation on average,"** and credit unions took less portfolio risk through 2007–2017 **[CF]** — but a strand of the literature argues that the *largest* credit unions become behaviourally hard to distinguish from banks as they grow, adopt community charters and expand fields of membership **[CL]**.
- *Coding:* degeneration process; **managerial capture**; Cook problems: **horizon** and **portfolio** (as membership becomes heterogeneous and transient, the institution optimises like a bank).

**The worker-cooperative degeneration literature.** The classic degeneration thesis (Ben-Ner; and the survey work of Cornforth) holds that cooperatives tend over time to revert toward conventional capitalist governance — hiring managers, hiring non-member labour, concentrating control. But **Cornforth's important counter-argument is that regeneration is possible**: cooperatives can consciously renew democratic participation and arrest degeneration through deliberate governance design, education and structure **[SC/CL]**. This matters for the reader because it means degeneration is a *tendency*, not a *destiny* — the defences in Part VI are the regeneration mechanisms.

## C.4 Insolvency cases (the event)

**KUSCCO (Kenya) — in full.** The Kenya Union of Savings & Credit Co-operatives, the apex body for Kenyan SACCOs, collapsed in a fraud exposed by a **PwC forensic audit** (submitted to national security agencies, February 2025). The findings: **~KSh 13.3bn lost**; the body rendered **insolvent by ~KSh 12.5bn** (assets of KSh 5.2bn against liabilities of KSh 17.7bn); **KSh 24.8bn taken from 247 SACCOs** as deposits now at risk; financial statements overstated by ~KSh 9.3bn through understated costs and phantom profits; and, most luridly, **the forged signature of external auditor Alfred Basweti of Omenye and Associates — who had died before the 2022 accounts he supposedly signed** (Business Daily; multiple Kenyan outlets) **[CF]**. Executives named include former MD George Ototo, finance manager George Owino and chairman George Magutu.
- *The structural finding.* **KUSCCO sat entirely outside SASRA's prudential perimeter** — as an apex/umbrella body it was not a licensed deposit-taking SACCO, so the regulator that credibly supervises Kenyan DT-SACCOs had no authority over the institution holding their money **[CF]**. This is the single most important lesson in the whole appendix for the reader: **a regulatory perimeter is only as good as its coverage, and the most dangerous institution is the one that everyone assumes is supervised but is not.**
- *Coding:* insolvency event driven by **managerial and board/insider capture** (with staff collusion); Cook problems: **control** (no effective member or regulator monitoring of the apex) and **free-rider** (247 SACCOs each assumed someone else was watching). A near-perfect illustration of the substitute-monitor thesis: remove the monitor, and fraud runs for years.

**Northern Rock and Bradford & Bingley — post-demutualisation insolvencies.** **Northern Rock** (demutualised 1997) suffered the first UK bank run since 1866 in **September 2007** and was **nationalised in February 2008**; **Bradford & Bingley** (demutualised 2000) was **nationalised/broken up in 2008**, its shareholders wiped out **[CF]**.
- *Coding:* insolvency event; **the ownership conversion (C.1) was the precondition** — the originate-to-distribute, wholesale-funded model that killed them was enabled by the equity-raising freedom demutualisation conferred. Cook problems: **horizon** and **portfolio** (short-horizon shareholder pressure driving aggressive growth). This is the empirical link between demutualisation and insolvency: converting removed the conservatism that mutual ownership had imposed.

**US corporate credit union failures, 2009–2010.** Five wholesale ("corporate") credit unions failed: **US Central and WesCorp** (both placed in conservatorship 20 March 2009), followed by **Members United, Southwest and Constitution** **[CF]**. The cause was concentrated holdings of private-label mortgage-backed securities. NCUA created the **Temporary Corporate Credit Union Stabilization Fund (May 2009)** to spread the losses across surviving credit unions over time; NCUA issued more than **$28bn** in Guaranteed Notes and the system faced losses estimated at the crisis peak in the tens of billions **[CF]**. Remarkably, by the program's wind-down (2021) NCUA returned funds to former capital holders — the losses proved smaller than feared.
- *Coding:* insolvency event; **managerial capture** at the wholesale institutions (chasing yield with members' liquidity); Cook problems: **portfolio** (concentration risk) and **free-rider** (retail credit unions trusted the corporates and did not monitor them). The parallel to KUSCCO is exact: an *apex/wholesale* body, poorly monitored by its member institutions, took on risk that then flowed back to the members.

**The Italian cooperative bank collapses, 2015–2017.** Two waves. First, **November 2015**: four banks including **Banca Etruria** (Banca Popolare dell'Etruria e del Lazio) were resolved, with a good-bank/bad-bank split and junior investors bailed in. Second, and more instructive, **June 2017**: **Banca Popolare di Vicenza and Veneto Banca** were declared "failing or likely to fail" by the ECB (23 June 2017); the Single Resolution Board declined resolution; and both were put into **compulsory administrative liquidation** under Italian law (Banca d'Italia, 25 June 2017 decree-law), their good assets sold to Intesa Sanpaolo for a token sum while shareholders and junior bondholders were wiped out **[CF]**.
- *The member-share-as-capital catastrophe.* These banks had, for years, **sold their own shares to their own depositors** — retail customers who believed they were saving, not buying risk equity. When the shares were wiped out, ordinary savers lost their capital. Veneto Banca's attempted January 2017 buy-back offered just **15% of the original share price**, and ~72.6% of shareholders (54,374 people) accepted, for €248.5m **[CF]**.
- *Coding:* insolvency event; **managerial capture** with **external/political** dimensions; Cook problems: **portfolio** (savers holding undiversified equity in their own bank) and **control** (member-shareholders with no effective oversight). **This is the specific danger of member-share-as-capital producing consumer catastrophe**, and it is directly relevant to any Nigerian cooperative fintech that funds itself by selling shares to the same people it serves: the ownership stake that looks like member commitment can become the instrument of member ruin.

**Kenyan SACCO failures beyond KUSCCO.** SASRA supervision reports document individual DT-SACCO distress and de-licensing; the **Metropolitan National SACCO** matter is among documented cases of governance/liquidity trouble **[CF, thin detail]**. SASRA gazettes ~176 licensed DT-SACCOs and periodically places some on restricted status or removes them — routine attrition that the regulated perimeter makes *visible*, in contrast to KUSCCO's invisibility.

**Irish credit union failures.** A number of Irish credit unions failed or were wound down/transferred after 2008 amid loan-book deterioration and weak governance; the Central Bank of Ireland restructured the sector **[CF, summary level]**.

**Nigerian cooperative fraud.** The evidence is **thin and case-study-based [U/CF]**. Nigerian cooperative fraud is real and frequently reported anecdotally — treasurers absconding, phantom loans, thrift-society ("esusu"/"ajo") collapses — but there is **no systematic, audited dataset** comparable to NCUA or SASRA data. The honest position: *we know Nigerian cooperatives fail, often by insider fraud in a weak-monitor environment, but we cannot quantify the rate.* This is itself the finding — the absence of data is a symptom of the same monitoring vacuum that causes the failures.

**Which of Cook's five problems is most frequently implicated?** Counting across the table, **control** appears most often, closely followed by **horizon** and **portfolio**; **free-rider** clusters in the apex/wholesale insolvencies (KUSCCO, US corporates); **influence costs** appear where governance politics drive bad decisions (Co-op Bank, Italy). *Testing the Prologue's assertion that the horizon problem is "the single most important concept for a founder":* the case record only **partly** supports it. The horizon problem is decisive in the *demutualisation* cases (short-horizon members cashing out) and in the *risk-taking* insolvencies. But the *most destructive* individual cases — KUSCCO, the Co-op Bank, the US corporates — are primarily **control** failures (monitoring collapse), not horizon failures. **[AI — a partial correction to the Prologue:** for a founder in a *weak-monitor* jurisdiction, the **control problem** is more immediately dangerous than the horizon problem, because the horizon problem destroys value slowly while the control problem permits catastrophic fraud quickly. The Prologue's emphasis on horizon is right for *mature, well-monitored* cooperatives; for a *young Nigerian* one, control comes first. Stage 8 should reconcile this.]**

**Does the asset lock actually prevent demutualisation?** The evidence says **no, not on its own.** Asset-locked Italy suffered *legislated* demutualisation; asset-locked France survived. Non-locked Germany had essentially *no* demutualisation; non-locked UK building societies demutualised en masse. The variable that tracks the outcome is not the lock but the **substitute monitor and institutional environment**. **Germany is the anomaly the Prologue flagged — no statutory asset lock (§§91–92 GenG), yet no demutualisation — and the explanation offered in Parts I–III holds up under this test:** the compulsory **Prüfungsverband** audit federation, the **BVR institutional protection scheme**, and a dense network make conversion practically pointless and individually unnecessary (a struggling bank is absorbed within the network rather than sold out of it) **[SC]**. The lock is a *belt*; the monitor is the *braces*, and the braces do the work.

**Does the substitute monitor predict degeneration?** Ranking by monitor strength (from A.7: Germany > US > Quebec > Netherlands > Kenya > Nigeria) against the failure record: the strongest-monitor jurisdictions (Germany, US, Quebec) show **absorption/consolidation** (the benign mode) and few catastrophic member-wiping failures; the weakest (Kenya's *perimeter gap*, Nigeria's *absence*) show **fraud-driven insolvency** (KUSCCO; undocumented Nigerian cases). The US corporate-CU failures are the apparent exception — a strong-monitor jurisdiction with a large failure — but they occurred precisely at the **wholesale tier that members did not monitor**, proving the rule from the other side. **The substitute monitor is confirmed as the best single predictor [SC/AI].**

**What distinguishes cases that regenerated from cases that failed?** The regenerators (Nationwide refusing conversion; Cornforth's regenerating worker co-ops; the German network absorbing weak banks) share three features: (i) a **credible external monitor or federation** that caught problems early; (ii) **conscious governance renewal** — deliberate defence of member control rather than passive drift; and (iii) **structural constraints** (charter clauses, network membership) that made the degenerative path costly. The failures share the opposite: monitoring vacuum, governance drift, and no structural brake.

**Which failure mode is most dangerous for a small, young, digitally-delivered cooperative in a weak-institution jurisdiction? (The reader's actual question.)** **Insolvency by insider fraud, enabled by a control failure, is the clear and present danger — the KUSCCO pattern.** Demutualisation is a rich-country, mature-institution phenomenon and is remote for a young Nigerian venture. Absorption is a *later*-stage risk (once the venture has something worth absorbing). Degeneration is a slow risk that matters over decades. But **insolvency via managerial/board capture can happen in year two**, before any external monitor exists, and in Nigeria there is no Prüfungsverband, no NCUSIF, no effective SASRA-equivalent to catch it. **[AI — the ranking for the reader:** (1) insolvency by insider fraud/control failure; (2) member-share-as-capital consumer catastrophe (the Italian Vicenza/Veneto pattern, acute for a remittance venture funded by its own users); (3) absorption from sub-scale; (4) degeneration; (5) demutualisation (remote).**]**

## C.6 The Nigerian risk profile

Applying the catalogue to the reader's cooperative fintech (eventually offering remittance), ranked by likelihood/severity, with Part VI defences mapped:

| Rank | Failure mode / capture type | Why it applies to the reader | Part VI defence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Insolvency via managerial/board-insider capture (KUSCCO pattern)** | Weakest-monitor jurisdiction (A.7); no compulsory audit federation; single management committee with no fit-and-proper test (A.6). A dishonest treasurer/MD can run for years undetected. | Import the **substitute monitor by contract**: mandatory annual external audit by a reputable firm; segregation of duties; real-time reconciliation; an independent audit committee; and — critically — do **not** let the apex/holding body sit outside supervision as KUSCCO did. |
| **2** | **Member-share-as-capital consumer catastrophe (Vicenza/Veneto pattern)** | A remittance cooperative funded by selling shares to the same low-income users it serves fuses saver and shareholder — the exact structure that ruined Italian depositors. | Keep **ownership and deposits sharply separate** (the German/Kenyan model, A.2); use **non-withdrawable shares** as loss-absorbing capital but disclose their risk honestly; raise real risk-capital in the **CAMA operating company** from investors who understand equity, not from remittance users. |
| **3** | **Absorption from sub-scale** | A small digital cooperative that cannot reach efficient scale becomes an acquisition target (the modal US/German outcome). | Build toward **efficient scale deliberately**; consider **network/federation membership** early (the German lesson) so absorption, if it comes, is *into a cooperative network* not *out of the movement*. |
| **4** | **Degeneration (Co-op Bank pattern)** | Over time, professional managers and a drifting board can hollow out member control without a vote. | **Conscious governance renewal** (Cornforth's regeneration): member education, transparent delegate structures, term limits, competence requirements, and structural brakes in the bye-laws. |
| **5** | **Demutualisation (remote)** | No live conversion route in Nigerian law; the reader is unlikely to face a carpet-bagger vote. | Adopt Nationwide-style **charter defences** anyway (minimum-holding periods; charitable devolution of any windfall) as cheap insurance; carry forward the **20% cap** (Appendix B.4). |
| **6** | **Political capture (Italy 2015 pattern)** | Lower near-term probability, but Nigerian cooperatives are historically vulnerable to state/political interference and registrar discretion. | Keep the operating company at arm's length in **CAMA** with clean governance; maintain regulatory relationships proactively; avoid dependence on political patronage. |

**The bottom line for the reader.** The catalogue's dominant lesson is not that cooperatives fail because of their *form* but because of **missing monitors**. Every catastrophic case — KUSCCO, the Co-op Bank, the US corporates, the Italian populars — is a monitoring failure at a point where members assumed, wrongly, that someone was watching. In Nigeria, where no external monitor exists by default, **the founder must manufacture the monitor**: by contract, by audit, by structural separation, and by governance design. Do that, and most of this catalogue does not apply to you. Fail to do it, and you are the next entry.
