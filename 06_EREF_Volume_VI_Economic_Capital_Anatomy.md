## VI.1 THE GOVERNING-METRIC CHECK *(perform first)*
**Before any analysis, validate that revenue means what it appears to mean.**

Establish whether any material revenue is recognised gross on a principal basis at near-zero margin — commodity resale, cryptocurrency, travel, advertising pass-through, agency arrangements booked gross. Where it is, state the amount, the margin and the share of total revenue.

Then confirm the governing metric declared in §0 and **use it as the spine of every subsequent section.** One prior subject booked a commodity at full sale value at roughly a three per cent margin, making it about forty-two per cent of reported revenue and a rounding item in gross profit; any analysis anchored on revenue would have been internally consistent and wrong by a factor of three.

## VI.2 Multi-year financial history
Five to ten years where useful: revenue, segments, gross profit, operating expenses, earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, operating income, pre-tax income, tax, net income, earnings per share, operating cash flow, capital expenditure, free cash flow, cash, debt, shares, dividends and buybacks, and industry-specific operating metrics.

**Flag every restatement and rebuild a consistent series where the disclosure permits.** Note explicitly where a series crosses a segment or basis change.

## VI.3 Revenue taxonomy
For each stream: payer, segment, product, trigger, pricing, contract, geography, volume driver, recognition, direct costs, margin, recurrence, cyclicality, concentration, sustainability.

## VI.4 Revenue flow
Trace a representative commercial activity into accounting revenue and into cash.

## VI.5 Pricing economics
Price against volume, realised pricing, discounting, mix, inflation pass-through, elasticity, cross-subsidies.

## VI.6 Cost architecture
Direct and product costs, labour, supply, logistics, technology, service, risk and loss, sales and marketing, research and development, corporate. Classify variable, fixed, semi-variable and step-fixed.

## VI.7 Unit economics
Identify the correct unit — customer, subscriber, order, store, route, loan, account, product, project, factory, tonne, megawatt or industry equivalent.

Calculate revenue per unit, margin per unit, acquisition cost, servicing cost, lifetime value, utilisation, yield, payback and incremental margin.

**Price the monetisation ladder from III.3**: what is a customer worth at each rung, and what is the multiple between the entry rung and the top?

## VI.8 Capital per unit of volume *(price the velocity finding)*
**Using III.8, establish how much capital a unit of annual volume actually consumes.**

Work it through explicitly: the balance-sheet commitment per unit; the duration; the turnover; and therefore the capital tied up per unit of annual throughput. Compare against the category the market places the subject in.

**Where velocity differs materially from peers, this is frequently the most under-appreciated economic fact about the business.** State it plainly and quantify it.

## VI.9 Segment economics
By product, geography, customer, channel, facility, maturity and project type. **Where the enterprise runs materially different businesses, present them separately and treat the consolidated view as a reconciliation** — a consolidated income statement hides divergence, which is usually the finding.

## VI.10 Income-statement teardown
Every material line and its operational cause.

## VI.11 Balance-sheet teardown — in bands
Analyse in four bands rather than as one list:

- **Money that is not the enterprise's** — customer funds, client money, settlements receivable and payable, float, deposits held on behalf of others.
- **Any regulated entity's balance sheet** consolidated within the group, with its own capital requirement.
- **The operating asset base** — receivables, inventory, loans, property, plant and equipment.
- **The parent's own assets** — cash, investments, goodwill and intangibles, deferred tax, treasury holdings.

**Then compute genuine leverage and returns with the first band excluded.** Leverage computed on reported totals is meaningless for any business holding material sums of other people's money, and this error is extremely common.

## VI.12 Working capital
Receivable days, payable days, inventory days, deferred revenue, advances, progress payments, seasonality.

## VI.13 Capital intensity
Separate maintenance from growth capital expenditure, working capital, regulatory capital, facilities, fleets, stores, data centres, inventory and research.

## VI.14 Cash flow and its quality
Reconcile accounting profit to shareholder free cash flow. Address the distortions explicitly: **share-based compensation as a non-cash add-back that is nonetheless a real cost**; movements in customer funds that flow through operating cash flow but are not the enterprise's money; balance-sheet growth that consumes cash; and fair-value remeasurement.

**State how much cash is genuinely available to shareholders**, which is frequently well below the headline free-cash-flow figure.

## VI.15 Debt and funding
Maturities, rates, covenants, revolvers, leases, securitisations, deposits, project finance, supplier finance, customer advances. **Verify the debt position against the filings rather than a summary** — reported and actual have diverged.

## VI.16 Capital allocation
Organic investment, research, capital expenditure, mergers and acquisitions, divestitures, debt repayment, dividends, buybacks, cash accumulation, venture investments.

**Classify each major decision: value creating · strategically necessary · questionable · too early to judge.** Establish the net effect on shares outstanding, and whether buybacks are returning capital or offsetting dilution.

## VI.17 Return on capital
Return on invested capital, equity and assets; incremental returns; segment and acquisition returns. **Normalise for one-time tax items, fair-value swings and restatements**, and say what the adjustment does to the picture.

## VI.18 One unit of revenue
Show where one currency unit of revenue goes, end to end.

## VI.19 Economic driver tree
The five to ten variables explaining most financial outcomes.

## VI.20 Non-GAAP measures assessed
Define every adjusted measure the enterprise reports. Reconcile to statutory figures where disclosure permits.

**Assess each adjustment on its merits.** State plainly which measures retain all real economic costs and which do not — particularly share-based compensation. **Where the enterprise promotes a self-selected composite metric, scrutinise it rather than repeat it.**

## VI.21 Scenario model and sensitivities
Strong growth, slow growth, price competition, cost inflation, demand shock, operating leverage, sector-specific shocks. Test the most important drivers.

## VI.22 Revenue quality
Score predictability, recurrence, retention, pricing power, concentration, cyclicality, margin, capital intensity, regulatory risk, disruption risk. **Rank the revenue lines from highest to lowest quality and say why.**

## VI.23 Normalised profitability
Separate structural economics from temporary macroeconomic and accounting effects. **Where provisioning is front-loaded on a growing book, distinguish a growth signal from a deterioration signal** and establish the proportions.

## VI.24 Valuation-relevant economics
Without forcing a view: growth durability, margin ceiling, reinvestment, capital intensity, cash-flow quality, cyclicality, dilution, optionality, long-term returns.

## Final questions
- What is the real economic engine?
- Which revenue stream is highest quality?
- Where does operating leverage come from?
- How much capital does a unit of volume actually consume?
- What is genuinely available to shareholders after real costs?
- Is growth capital-light or capital-intensive?
- Does incremental capital earn attractive returns?

**Completion test:** a reader should understand precisely how activity becomes revenue, profit, cash and return on capital — and should not be able to be misled by the headline figures.
