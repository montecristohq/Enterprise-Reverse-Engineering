## VII.1 Founders and historical architects
Backgrounds, motivations, operating philosophy, capital allocation, risk tolerance, long-term orientation, ownership, governance influence. **Establish revealed priorities from where capital and personal attention have actually gone**, not from stated philosophy.

## VII.2 Current management
Roles, tenure, experience, responsibilities, compensation, incentives, ownership, succession, strategic influence. Assess leadership depth, turnover and **key-person risk**, including any concentration of multiple senior functions in one individual.

## VII.3 The management system
Strategic planning, budgeting, forecasting, performance metrics, resource allocation, hiring, promotion, performance management, investment approval, risk approval, mergers and acquisitions, geographic expansion.

**Compare the formal structure with where power actually sits**, and establish whether any function holds a genuine veto.

## VII.4 Declared versus revealed culture
Separate the declared culture from the revealed one. Assess customer orientation, quality, frugality, transparency, autonomy, accountability, bureaucracy, speed, risk tolerance, innovation, long-termism and internal politics.

Test declared culture against: the enforcement record; the abandonment record; the treatment of the original constituency; the framing of workforce changes; the handling of adverse allegations; and disclosure choices.

## VII.5 THE CREDIBILITY VERDICT
**Where the enterprise's public claims have required correction, that pattern is itself the finding — and this volume must reach a settled position on it.**

Catalogue the instances. For each: what was claimed, what was independently established, whether the enterprise later qualified it, and what the correction was worth.

Then **state plainly which categories of statement are reliable and which are not.** Two prior studies reached the same structural verdict from different evidence: the audited financial disclosure was reliable; the operational and strategic narrative was not. That is a priceable conclusion.

**Distinguish promotional exuberance — common, and capable of being discounted — from misrepresentation of audited figures, which is far graver. Do not conflate them in either direction**, and where the evidence supports neither, say so.

Close with the practical instruction: **what should an analyst do with this enterprise's statements in future?**

## VII.6 Incentives
Founders, executives, business-unit leaders, sales, product, engineering, operations, finance, compliance and risk, frontline workers, partners, shareholders.

Identify conflicts and gameable metrics. **Ask what behaviour the system actually rewards** — and whether anything rewards risk and compliance outcomes.

## VII.7 Ownership structure as a causal variable
Where control is concentrated by structure — multi-class shares, a controlling holder, a cooperative or mutual form, state ownership — treat it as a **cause of outcomes rather than a governance footnote.**

Establish what the structure bought: long-horizon patience, resistance to short-term pressure, the ability to pivot. And what it cost: decisions that no one could stop, and the pattern of those decisions.

**Reach a verdict for this enterprise specifically**, avoiding both governance orthodoxy and founder worship, and state the conditions under which the calculus would change.

## VII.8 Competitive universe
Segment: direct competitors, adjacent competitors, substitutes, vertical entrants, new technologies, low-cost entrants, premium entrants, and public or governmental alternatives. **Explain why each category belongs.**

## VII.9 Competitor teardowns
Compare customer, product, price, quality, distribution, geography, technology, assets, supply chain, brand, regulation, acquisition, economics, capital, scale, switching costs and moat.

**Give the deepest treatment to the closest structural analogue** — the competitor running the most similar model, which is the cleanest test of whether the subject executes it well. Be willing to conclude that a rival executes it better.

## VII.10 Why the enterprise wins
Decompose the mechanism rather than listing strengths: cost, product, quality, brand, trust, distribution, switching costs, scale, network effects, intellectual property, technology, data, regulation, relationships, physical scarcity, speed, service, capital access, culture.

**Separate structural advantage from management choice from temporary advantage**, and quantify where possible.

## VII.11 Moat analysis
Score 0–5 with mechanism, evidence, durability, replicability and weakening conditions: network effects, scale economies, brand, trust, switching costs, regulation, distribution, intellectual property, data, installed base, supply relationships, customer relationships, physical scarcity, cost position, ecosystem, learning curves, operational expertise, capital access.

**Apply discriminating scepticism.** Do not score uniformly where the evidence is not uniform. Where the evidence supports a genuine advantage, score it high; where a claimed advantage does not survive testing, score it low and say so. **A scorecard on which everything scores alike has said nothing.** State plainly which claimed moats survive testing and which do not.

## VII.12 Absence as evidence
**Where the enterprise has asserted an advantage for years and never published the metric that would demonstrate it, record the absence as a finding rather than as a data limitation.**

Establish what evidence would demonstrate each major strategic claim, then establish what the enterprise has actually disclosed. The gap is informative.

## VII.13 Replication test
Imagine the enterprise disappears. For each key capability estimate difficulty, time, capital, regulatory burden, technical burden, operational burden, talent burden and path dependence.

Classify: easy to buy · buildable · hard to reproduce · path-dependent · effectively non-replicable at comparable time and cost.

## VII.14 Natural experiments
**Identify where the enterprise ran the experiment itself** — two comparable businesses with divergent outcomes, markets entered and exited, products built and killed, a strategy applied in one division and not another.

State what each demonstrates that no external comparison could. *Where Volumes IX or X were commissioned, draw on them; otherwise establish the cases here.*

## VII.15 PESTLE
Only material political, economic, social, technological, legal and environmental factors.

## VII.16 Strategic flywheels
Positive compounding mechanisms, **each verified with evidence.** Reject the unsupported.

## VII.17 Negative flywheels
Adverse compounding mechanisms — regulatory ratchets, disintermediation, credit deterioration feeding funding cost, depressed equity impairing retention.

## VII.18 Strategic bottleneck
> If every other part improved by fifty per cent, what single constraint would most limit future value creation?

Identify the current bottleneck and the likely next one.

## VII.19 Risk register
Strategic, competitive, operational, supply, quality, financial, credit, liquidity, rate, currency, commodity, regulatory, legal, tax, cyber, data, intellectual property, environmental, political, geopolitical, reputational, key-person, governance, execution and concentration risk.

Score probability, severity, detectability, mitigation, residual risk and time horizon.

## VII.20 Stress tests
Revenue shock, margin compression, price war, loss of a major supplier or customer, regulation, operating-asset failure, cyber incident, management transition, substitute technology, sector-specific shocks, **and the execution risk of any in-flight restructuring.**

## VII.21 Obsolescence
What technology, regulation, business model, customer behaviour, lower-cost production, platform shift, automation or substitute could eliminate the advantage. For each: does it eliminate the customer problem, commoditise the advantage, can the enterprise adopt it, and does it strand capital?

## VII.22 Strategic optionality
Evaluate adjacencies. Classify: natural adjacency · plausible · stretch · strategic fantasy.

## VII.23 What is the enterprise becoming?
Develop three to six competing hypotheses. For each: evidence for, evidence against, the economics, management behaviour, capital allocation and product evolution. **Rank by probability.**

## VII.24 Five-year and ten-year strategic map
Base case, strong execution, margin compression, disruption, regulatory shock, strategic transformation. For each: revenue mix, products, customers, margins, capital needs, competitive position, moat.

## VII.25 What the market misunderstands
At least five. For each: the conventional view, the underlying reality, the evidence, why the misconception persists, the implication.

## VII.26 Management and capital-allocation judgement
Evaluate every major historical decision as value creating, strategically necessary, questionable, or too early to judge — **with the evidence for the classification.**

## Final questions
- Why does the enterprise win today?
- What is the strongest moat, and which is most overrated?
- What is hardest to replicate? What is easiest?
- How much weight should be placed on what management says?
- What is the bottleneck? What is the single greatest structural risk?
- Which competitor or substitute is most dangerous?
- What could make the enterprise obsolete?
- What is it most likely to become over five to ten years?

**Completion test:** a reader should understand leadership, culture, competitive advantage, risk, strategic constraint and future evolution — and should know which of the enterprise's own claims to believe.
