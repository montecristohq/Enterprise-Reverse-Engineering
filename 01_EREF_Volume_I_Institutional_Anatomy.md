## I.1 Corporate history
Reconstruct founding, original model, financing, acquisitions, divestitures, restructurings, geographic expansion, listing, leadership changes, crises, regulatory milestones, pivots, current state.

For each material event identify: **structural change · new capability · new dependency · legal or regulatory consequence · path dependence.** A chronology that does not explain the present shape has failed.

## I.2 Corporate group structure
Map parent, holding companies, operating subsidiaries, regional entities, licensed entities, IP entities, employment entities, manufacturing and distribution entities, financing entities, joint ventures, special-purpose vehicles, and materially relevant dormant entities.

For each: legal name, jurisdiction, ownership, immediate parent, function, assets, liabilities, employees, revenue responsibility, tax residence, regulated status, permits and licences, and major contracts where public.

## I.3 THE REGULATORY PERIMETER *(map this, not only the ownership tree)*
**An ownership chart is public and largely uninformative. The perimeter is what constrains the business.**

Organise every material entity into three rings:
- **Inside the licensed entity's ring-fence** — subject to prudential or sectoral supervision.
- **Regulated but outside it** — holding permissions of a different kind.
- **Unregulated commercial.**

For each entity state which ring, which regulator, and **what keeps it on that side of the boundary.** Where the group holds a volatile or commercially exotic asset, state which ring holds it and why.

*Where the subject holds no licences, record that and move on — do not manufacture a perimeter.*

## I.4 Intercompany architecture *(the contracts binding the rings together)*
Where a licensed entity sits inside a wider group, establish the instruments governing the boundary: capital and liquidity maintenance undertakings; affiliate-transaction limits and their statutory basis; source-of-strength obligations, including any binding a controlling individual personally; tax-sharing and whether tax assets are held in trust; intercompany service agreements and their pricing basis; governance separation, including any independent-director requirement at the subsidiary; and **the dividend position — what the parent may and may not extract, and whether it ever has.**

Establish plainly: **what can flow up, what must flow down, and what is trapped.**

## I.5 Ownership
Analyse founders, families, institutions, strategic holders, governments, employee ownership, public float, share classes, preferred rights, dilution, and ownership changes.

**Explicitly separate economic ownership from voting control.** Where a multi-class structure exists, state the ratio, the holder's economic stake against their voting share, the sunset provision and whether it is time-based or behaviour-based, and the record of any shareholder proposals to collapse it with their vote shares.

## I.6 Governance
Board, chair, chief executive, committees, independence, succession, tenure, executive committee, controversies, board expertise.

**Then answer directly: has any independent director ever visibly constrained the controlling party?** Cite the instance or record that none is evident. Where a subsidiary operates under a stricter governance regime than the parent — imposed by a regulator — describe the contrast; it is instructive.

## I.7 Decision rights
Who can make what decisions, and **who can stop them.** Map capital allocation, mergers and acquisitions, divestitures, executive appointments, debt, dividends and buybacks, geographic expansion, product and facility investment, risk, and regulatory authority.

## I.8 Executive compensation
Salary, bonus, equity, long-term incentives, performance metrics, pensions and perquisites, founder incentives, alignment, distortions. Where nominal pay is symbolic, establish what the individual actually holds and receives.

## I.9 Legal architecture
Customer and supplier contracts, distribution agreements, IP ownership, patents, trademarks, licensing, outsourcing, labour arrangements, intercompany agreements, related parties, litigation, antitrust, product liability, tax disputes.

## I.10 Regulatory architecture
Where material: regulators, licences, permits, certifications, concessions, product approvals, capital requirements, reporting duties, safety and quality obligations, geographic scope, renewal and termination risk.

*Where the subject is heavily licensed and permissions determine what the business can be, commission **Volume VIII — The Regulatory & Licensing Estate** and keep this section to a map.*

## I.11 Own capability versus partner dependency
For each material regulated or institutional capability, determine whether the enterprise owns, licenses, outsources, partners, uses a joint venture, or relies on government-granted access.

**Name the parties that are not in the group** — sponsoring banks, processors, networks, partner lenders, licence holders. In two prior studies the entity that appeared to own the customer did not.

## I.12 Regulation as cost, constraint or moat
Assess barrier to entry, trust, scarcity, capital burden, compliance burden, protected position, geographic advantage. **Reach a verdict: on net, for this subject at this scale, is regulation a cost or a moat?** The answer frequently differs for the incumbent and the entrant.

## I.13 Institutional dependencies
Governments, regulators, banks, lenders, insurers, critical suppliers, utilities, landlords, standards bodies, exchanges, strategic customers, infrastructure owners, major vendors. Rank by criticality and replaceability.

## I.14 Tax and intercompany economics
Where evidence permits: tax residence, transfer pricing, IP royalties, management charges, cost allocations, withholding tax, incentives, effective tax rate, uncertain positions. **Flag any one-time tax item that distorts a reported result**, since later volumes will need it normalised.

## I.15 Institutional risk
Licence loss, litigation, government intervention, tax disputes, IP risk, antitrust, partner termination, regulatory change, export controls, parent-subsidiary contagion.

## Final questions
- Where does actual control sit?
- Which legal entity is strategically most important — and is it the one the market names?
- What keeps the licensed entity ring-fenced, and what can the parent extract?
- Which permission or institutional relationship is hardest to reproduce?
- What is the most dangerous dependency?
- Which part of the institutional structure is most misunderstood?

**Completion test:** a reader should understand who owns, controls, regulates, legally constitutes and institutionally enables the enterprise — and where the boundary runs between the regulated and unregulated parts of it.
