## II.1 Market definition
Define the actual economic markets. Distinguish direct, adjacent, substitute, geographic and customer-specific markets.

## II.2 Market size and structure
Market revenue and volume, addressable-market estimates where meaningful, growth, concentration, fragmentation, cyclicality, regulation, geographic differences.

## II.3 Industry value pool
Where do industry profits accumulate — manufacturers, distributors, platforms, service providers, intermediaries, infrastructure, aftermarkets? **Establish where the subject sits in that pool and whether its position is improving.**

## II.4 THE PAYER INVERSION *(run this before segmentation, not after)*
**Establish who actually pays, which is frequently not the party the enterprise calls its customer.**

For each material offering ask: **who receives the value, who bears the cost, and are they the same party?** Where they are not, establish what the non-paying party actually is to the business — raw material, product, bait, distribution, or something else — and what the paying party is actually buying.

Prior studies produced four structurally different answers: the consumer as **raw material** (data furnished about them); the customer's **order** as the product, sold to a wholesale counterparty; the consumer as **bait**, with a merchant paying for conversion; and a **free wedge** subsidised by downstream monetisation.

**Where the enterprise charges everyone openly, answer the second form:** which customer subsidises which, and what happens if the subsidy is withdrawn?

*This is the sharpest instrument in the framework. Carry the answer explicitly into Volume III, which traces how the value actually moves.*

## II.5 Customer segmentation
Segment by consumer, business or government; geography; income or company size; industry; use case; sophistication; channel; value; profitability.

For each: needs, behaviour, frequency, spend, price sensitivity, service burden, retention. **Where the payer and the user differ, segment both.**

## II.6 Jobs to be done
For each segment: triggering event, functional job, economic job, emotional or trust job where material, current workaround, switching trigger, desired outcome.

## II.7 Buying process
Buyer, user, influencer, approver, procurement, budget holder, sales cycle, implementation cycle, renewal.

## II.8 Customer acquisition
Organic, paid, sales-led, partner-led, retail, channel, referral, brand, marketplace, government procurement. Determine acquisition cost or a useful proxy by channel.

## II.9 The acquisition subsidy *(where anything is free or below cost)*
**Establish what "free" actually costs and who funds it.**

Identify every product, service or hardware item offered free or below cost. For each: the direct cost of provision; the associated fraud, support or loss cost; the deferred cost, including any compliance or remediation bill arising from frictionless provision; and **the revenue line that funds it.**

Then reach a verdict: **is this a business or an acquisition subsidy?** Where it is a subsidy, establish what it acquires customers *for* — because that downstream thing is the actual business.

## II.10 Sales architecture
Self-service, small business, mid-market, enterprise, field sales, channel, distributors, account management, solution engineering, partner sales.

## II.11 Marketing
Brand, performance marketing, content, events, sponsorship, referrals, community, public relations. Separate acquisition from brand maintenance.

## II.12 Distribution
Stores, e-commerce, distributors, dealers, wholesalers, marketplaces, agents, brokers, banks, partners, app stores, integrators.

For each: economics, **customer ownership, data ownership**, bargaining power, scalability. **Establish whether the enterprise owns its distribution or rents it** — and if rented, from whom, on what terms, and how easily that party could disintermediate it.

## II.13 Pricing
List against realised price, discounts, rebates, bundles, subscriptions, usage pricing, dynamic pricing, enterprise contracts, financing, cross-subsidies.

## II.14 Willingness to pay and elasticity
Why customers tolerate the price, where they resist, how it compares with alternatives.

## II.15 Retention and churn
Repeat behaviour, contracts, switching costs, habits, integration, loyalty, service, quality, churn causes. **Distinguish retention that comes from satisfaction from retention that comes from friction.**

## II.16 Customer concentration
Top customers, revenue concentration, contract duration, bargaining power, renewal risk.

## II.17 Competitive market structure
Porter's five forces on rivalry, buyers, suppliers, substitutes and entrants. **Give supplier power particular attention where the enterprise rents its distribution or its rails.** Keep company-level competitor strategy for Volume VII.

## II.18 Commercial flywheels
Whether acquisition, distribution density, installed base, brand, marketplace liquidity or referral loops reinforce growth. **Verify each with evidence; reject the unsupported.**

## Final questions
- Who is the economically most important customer, and is it the party the enterprise calls its customer?
- What customer problem matters most?
- What is the strongest acquisition channel, and what does it cost?
- Where is there genuine pricing power?
- What causes customers to stay — satisfaction or friction?
- Does the enterprise own its distribution or rent it?
- What market assumption is most critical to growth?

**Completion test:** a reader should understand demand — market, payer, buying behaviour, pricing, acquisition, distribution, retention and commercial power — and know who is subsidising whom.
