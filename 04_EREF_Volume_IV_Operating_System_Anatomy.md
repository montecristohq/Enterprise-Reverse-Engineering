## IV.1 Operating model
Map procurement, manufacturing, operations, logistics, quality, service, support, treasury, compliance, finance, legal, sales operations, field operations, research operations, vendor management, internal services.

For each: mandate, inputs, processes, outputs, people, key metrics, cost, dependencies, bottlenecks, automation, outsourcing.

## IV.2 Core process maps
Trace the flows that matter: order-to-cash, procure-to-pay, concept-to-product, lead-to-sale, manufacture-to-delivery, application-to-service, transaction-to-settlement, and any industry-specific equivalent.

## IV.3 The critical operating path
**Identify the one process whose interruption would stop the business fastest**, and trace it end to end with its dependencies, its single points of failure and its recovery time.

## IV.4 Supply chain *(where relevant)*
Raw materials, components, suppliers, sourcing geography, contracts, qualification, lead times, logistics, concentration, dual sourcing, geopolitical exposure.

## IV.5 Procurement *(where relevant)*
Buying power, sourcing strategy, commodity exposure, contracts, supplier economics, procurement organisation.

## IV.6 Manufacturing or service delivery
Plants, facilities, capacity, utilisation, throughput, yield, downtime, maintenance, automation, labour, energy, safety, quality. For service businesses, the analogous capacity model.

Raw materials, work in progress, finished goods, safety stock, turns, obsolescence, shrinkage, seasonality.

## IV.9 Quality
Quality gates, defect rates, recalls, warranty, testing, certification, complaints, continuous improvement.

## IV.10 REGULATED OPERATIONS *(where the subject is licensed or supervised)*
**The operational functions a regulator requires, which conventional operations analysis omits and which have repeatedly proved to be where enterprises actually fail.**

Establish: identity verification and customer due diligence; transaction monitoring and reporting; sanctions and screening; conduct, complaints and dispute resolution; mandated service levels; record-keeping; and the internal audit and control-testing regime.

For each: who owns it, how it is resourced, how its capacity scales against volume, and **whether it can stop a product launch.** State explicitly whether risk and compliance hold a genuine veto, or only a voice.

## IV.11 The enforcement post-mortem *(where an adjudicated failure exists)*
**Where a regulator has investigated and published findings, treat that document as operational evidence rather than as a legal artefact.**

Enforcement orders routinely contain the most precise public description of an enterprise's internal systems — queue depths, alert backlogs, thresholds, staffing ratios, escalation paths — because the regulator had subpoena power and the company did not choose the disclosure.

Establish: what failed, in which system, over what period; the causal link to a design or growth decision; what was mandated as remedy; and **the permanent operating cost the remedy imposes.** A mandated service level is a cost line for ever.

## IV.12 Customer support and field service
Channels, staffing, self-service, escalation, repair, maintenance, warranties, service economics. Where support levels are regulatorily mandated, cost them.

## IV.13 Outsourcing
What is outsourced and why: manufacturing, logistics, support, professional services, contractors, business process, facilities. **Establish what the enterprise has outsourced that it cannot easily bring back.**

## IV.14 Facilities and physical footprint
Offices, factories, warehouses, laboratories, branches, stores, depots, service centres; owned against leased.

## IV.15 Workforce operations
Headcount by function and geography, labour intensity, shifts, productivity, contractors, unions and works councils, critical skills, turnover.

**Where a material headcount change has occurred, establish its true composition** — automation, attrition, hiring freeze, over-hiring correction, business-line closure — rather than accepting the stated cause. This is a COMPANY CLAIM until decomposed.

## IV.16 Process automation
Robotics, workflow automation, machine learning in production, scheduling, optimisation, self-service, straight-through processing. **Separate deployed capability from pilot from aspiration.**

## IV.17 Operating leverage
Classify processes as nearly automatic, sublinear, linear, step-fixed or disproportionate. **Establish the marginal cost of one additional unit of output**, and whether recent margin improvement is structural automation or a one-time cost reset.

## IV.18 Bottlenecks
> If every other operational component improved by fifty per cent, what single constraint would still cap output or growth?

Identify the current bottleneck and the likely next one.

## IV.19 Resilience
Stress supplier failure, facility outage, labour disruption, logistics breakdown, demand spike, demand collapse, utility failure, recall, regulatory shutdown, and **the execution risk of any in-flight restructuring.**

## Final questions
- What is the operating core?
- Which process constraint matters most?
- Where does operating leverage actually come from — and is it structural or a one-time reset?
- Can risk and compliance stop a launch?
- What is hardest to scale? What is hardest to replicate?
- Which external dependency could halt operations fastest?

**Completion test:** a reader should understand how resources become delivered outcomes, at what cost, through which bottlenecks, under whose supervision, with what resilience.
