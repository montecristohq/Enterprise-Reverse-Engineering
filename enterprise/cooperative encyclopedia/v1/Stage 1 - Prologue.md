# The Cooperative Encyclopedia: Legal Form, Capital, Governance and the Nigerian Path
## PROLOGUE — Why Cooperatives Exist, and Why They Fail

---

**TL;DR**

- **Cooperative financial institutions are durable and fragile for the same reason.** The alignment of owner and customer interests that makes them safe (low return volatility, conservative risk-taking, member loyalty) is inseparable from the "vaguely defined, non-tradable property rights" that make them unable to raise external equity and vulnerable to demutualisation, absorption, degeneration and — occasionally — insolvency. The durability and the fragility are two faces of one structural fact, and this study's organising thesis is that *which face shows depends on the legal and institutional environment* (homogeneous membership, indivisible reserves, protection-scheme networks, and a non-capital-intensive niche produce durability; their absence produces conversion).
- **A specific correction, verified: Cook identified FIVE property-rights problems, not four.** In Michael L. Cook, "The Future of U.S. Agricultural Cooperatives: A Neo-Institutional Approach," *American Journal of Agricultural Economics* 77(5) (1995), pp. 1153–1159, the canonical list is the **free-rider, horizon, portfolio, control, and influence-costs problems**. The reader's "four" was wrong. This five-problem taxonomy is fixed as canonical and structures the Failure Catalogue appendix.
- **Capital is the binding constraint on every financial cooperative, and it is a legal fact before it is an economic one.** Because member shares are redeemable at par, they are a liability in substance (IFRIC 2) and count as top-quality regulatory capital only insofar as the institution can refuse redemption (CRR Article 29) — the "redemption paradox." Retained earnings therefore cap growth at roughly the return on equity, while investor-owned rivals can issue equity and grow faster. The whole later study is a search for how to relax this constraint without ceasing to be a cooperative.

---

## Key Findings

1. **Ownership form is predictable from economics, not ideology (Hansmann).** Customer/cooperative ownership arises where market contracting with customers is costly (asymmetric information, lock-in, local market power) *and* customers are homogeneous — conditions that describe banking, insurance and agricultural marketing exactly. This single framework explains both the historical rise of cooperative banking and its retreat once deposit insurance and prudential regulation reduced the depositor's contracting problem.

2. **The property-rights critique is correct about mechanisms but wrong as a prediction of inevitable failure.** Ward's Illyrian firm, Furubotn–Pejovich's attenuated rights, Cook's five problems, Olson's collective-action decay, Hart–Moore's heterogeneity result and Rasmusen's risk-taking analysis together predict under-investment, control failure and conversion. Yet European cooperative banking is large, old and stable — because successful systems neutralise the mechanisms through legal asset locks and network institutions. This is one conditional theory, not two contradictory literatures.

3. **The empirical record on demutualisation is a warning.** Of the UK building societies that demutualised and floated after the Building Societies Act 1986, *not one survived the 2007–2009 crisis independently*; the mutual survivor, Nationwide, came through without state capital. The Co-operative Bank (2013–14) shows a cooperative can be lost to the capital constraint — passing to hedge-fund control — *without any member vote to demutualise*. Both facts are central evidence for the founder.

4. **The problems are diseases of scale, not of founding (Cook's lifecycle).** A new, small, homogeneous cooperative feels none of the five problems on day one; it feels them acutely if it succeeds and grows into a large, heterogeneous membership — precisely when a remittance-offering, technology-dependent institution most needs to invest long-term. Design choices made at founding determine whether that phase is survivable.

## Details

### §0.0 Conventions established here that govern all eight stages

Because this is the first stage, it fixes conventions every later stage inherits. These are epistemic commitments, not stylistic preferences.

**§0.0.1 Evidence-classification scheme.** Every material claim carries one of six tags. This scheme deliberately differs from the reader's prior *company* studies, where the salient distinction was company claim versus third-party estimate; in a *theory and institutions* study the salient distinction is the epistemic status of knowledge:

- **CONFIRMED FACT** — a matter of record (statute text, dated event, audited figure); verifiable and not seriously disputed.
- **SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS** — a proposition the peer-reviewed literature broadly accepts as a synthesis of evidence.
- **CONTESTED IN THE LITERATURE** — a proposition on which competent scholars genuinely disagree; the study presents the disagreement rather than resolving it artificially.
- **ANALYTICAL INFERENCE** — a conclusion this study draws by reasoning from facts and theory, with the reasoning shown so the reader can dissent.
- **HYPOTHESIS** — advanced as plausible and worth testing, not established.
- **UNKNOWN** — a question the available evidence does not answer, flagged rather than papered over.

**§0.0.2 Source hierarchy.** For this document the hierarchy is *inverted* relative to a company study, because cooperative law is constitutive — a cooperative is what its enabling statute says it is. Ranking: (1) **statute and regulation** (the primary evidence for most claims in Parts I–V); (2) **peer-reviewed academic literature** (the primary evidence for theory and empirical adjudication); (3) **regulator datasets and supervisory reports** (central banks, EBA, NCUA, WOCCU, IMF); (4) **audited accounts of named institutions**; (5) **cooperative-federation publications — used with caution because they are promotional** (their factual claims are treated as claims to be tested, not conclusions to adopt); (6) **reputable journalism** (for recent events and colour, not load-bearing analysis). Where sources conflict, higher-ranked sources prevail and the conflict is flagged.

**§0.0.3 Jurisdiction labels.** Six jurisdictions are carried throughout and named exactly: **Germany**, **the Netherlands**, **Quebec/Canada** (Quebec-specific where the *Loi sur les coopératives de services financiers* and the caisses populaires are in view; Canada-wide where federal credit-union law applies), **the United States**, **Kenya**, and **Nigeria**.

**§0.0.4 Terminology discipline.** The following usages are fixed and held to:
- **Cooperative** — a firm owned and democratically controlled by a defined class of *patrons* (customers, suppliers or workers) on a substantially one-member-one-vote basis, whose surplus is distributed by reference to patronage rather than capital. The genus.
- **Mutual** — a customer-owned firm with *no separate share capital*, membership arising automatically from the customer relationship (classic form: the mutual building society or mutual insurer). A species of customer-owned enterprise, used narrowly and never as a loose synonym for "cooperative."
- **Member share** — the subscribed, par-value ownership stake, typically redeemable at or near par on exit; the instrument at the centre of the capital problem.
- **Subordinated member deposit** — a term deposit or subordinated loan from a member ranking below ordinary creditors but *not* an ownership stake; a loss-absorbing liability.
- **Permanent capital instrument** — an instrument engineered to be non-redeemable (or redeemable only at the institution's discretion) so it qualifies as permanent, loss-absorbing regulatory capital (e.g. Rabobank Certificates).
- **Credit union** — a customer-owned depository whose members share a defined common bond, typically under a dedicated statute.
- **Cooperative bank** — a licensed bank whose ownership is cooperative, under the ordinary banking regime.
- **SACCO** — Savings and Credit Cooperative Organisation, the East African term of art (dominant in Kenya), some deposit-taking (DT-SACCOs), some not.
- **Cooperative society** — the generic Commonwealth legal-form term (including Nigeria's), registered under a Cooperative Societies law rather than a Companies Act.
- **Demutualisation** — the *formal* legal conversion of a cooperative/mutual into an investor-owned company, usually by member vote, distributing accumulated reserves. An event.
- **Degeneration** — the *substantive* erosion of member control while the cooperative form is formally retained. A process. Demutualisation is an event; degeneration is a process.

**§0.0.5 Currency and date conventions.** Monetary figures are reported in the currency of the jurisdiction discussed, with the year stated, and are not converted to a common numéraire in this Prologue; later Parts state per-jurisdiction conventions where comparison requires them.

### §0.1 The problem the Prologue must solve

Consider two facts, both true, that appear to contradict each other.

**The first fact: cooperative financial institutions are extraordinarily durable.** German cooperative banks have operated continuously since the 1860s. Their protection scheme, run by the Bundesverband der Deutschen Volksbanken und Raiffeisenbanken (BVR), is the oldest privately financed bank protection scheme in the world, established in 1934, and — on the BVR's own account — no member institution has gone bankrupt since. [CONFIRMED FACT, with the caveat that the "no member insolvency since 1934" claim is the BVR's own, and is a claim about the *scheme's* success at averting insolvency by pre-emptive intervention, not evidence that individual banks never became distressed — the source is a federation publication and is treated under §0.0.2 accordingly.] Rabobank (Netherlands), Crédit Agricole and Groupe BPCE (France), the Desjardins movement (Quebec), and the US credit-union system are all large, old and, by ordinary measures, successful.

**The second fact: a large fraction of the Anglo-American mutual sector demutualised or disappeared within a single generation.** The UK Building Societies Act 1986 permitted conversion to company form; between 1989 and 2000 almost every large building society converted, and the mutual share of the mortgage market collapsed. In the United States, thousands of mutual savings institutions and mutual insurers converted to stock form across the 1980s and 1990s.

Both facts are true. The Prologue's task is to explain *why both are true* — to show that durability and fragility spring from the *same* structural features of cooperative ownership, viewed from different angles. Durability comes from the alignment of owner and customer interests, the low-risk business model that alignment produces, and (in the successful systems) a collective architecture — protection schemes, central institutions, indivisible reserves — that solves the capital problem at the network level. Fragility comes from the fact that cooperatives cannot easily raise external equity, that members' claims are locked in and illiquid, and that the very reserves which make them safe also make them a standing temptation: a pot of accumulated, un-owned capital that a current generation can, under the right legal conditions, vote to cash out. The rest of this Prologue is the long-form answer.

### §0.2 Why cooperatives exist at all — the economics of ownership

**§0.2.1 Hansmann's framework: ownership as a cost-minimisation problem.** The indispensable starting point is **Henry Hansmann, *The Ownership of Enterprise* (Harvard University Press, 1996)**, building on his "Ownership of the Firm" (*Journal of Law, Economics, and Organization*, 1988). Hansmann converted a normative, often sentimental literature into a positive theory of *why particular ownership forms arise in particular industries*, treating the investor-owned corporation, the worker cooperative, the consumer cooperative, the mutual and the non-profit as one family explained by a single principle.

The principle: any firm transacts with several classes of *patrons* — customers who buy output, suppliers (including of labour and capital) who provide inputs. Ownership — defined by Hansmann as the possession of two rights, control and residual earnings — can in principle be assigned to any class. Hansmann's central claim is that ownership tends over time to be assigned to **the class of patrons for whom the sum of two costs is lowest**: the total cost is minimised when ownership goes to the class for whom **(a) the costs of market contracting** (borne if that class dealt at arm's length) **plus (b) the costs of ownership** (borne as owner) are together at a minimum. [SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS as to the framework; Hansmann 1996, chs. 1–3.]

**(a) The costs of market contracting** — the costs a non-owner patron bears protecting its interests through contract and market discipline alone — decompose into:
- **Market power / monopoly** — exposure to overcharging (customers) or underpayment (suppliers) where the firm faces little competition; ownership converts the monopolist's margin into a patronage refund.
- **Asymmetric information** — where the firm knows more about quality or price than the patron can verify (the quintessential financial-services problem: the depositor cannot assess the institution's safety, the lender cannot assess the borrower), inviting *ex post* exploitation through moral hazard and adverse selection.
- **Lock-in and transaction-specific investment** — where a patron must invest specifically in the relationship, the firm can hold it up *ex post* even if the market was competitive *ex ante*.
- **Long-term contracting problems** — where the future cannot be fully specified, the residual right to decide unforeseen matters is valuable, and assigning it to the exposed patron class economises on contracting.

**(b) The costs of ownership** — the costs a class bears *because* it owns:
- **Monitoring / agency costs** — dispersed owners monitor managers poorly (developed via Olson, §0.3.4). Hansmann incorporates Jensen and Meckling (1976) here.
- **Costs of collective decision-making** — Hansmann's genuine innovation. When owners have *heterogeneous* interests, voting satisfies the pivotal (median) voter rather than the average member, and factional rent-seeking dissipates value. The decisive proposition: **the more homogeneous the owning class, the lower these costs.**
- **Risk-bearing** — residual claimants bear residual risk; a class ill-placed to diversify bears a high ownership cost (reappears as Cook's portfolio problem).

**§0.2.2 What the framework predicts — and why banking fits.** The framework predicts **customer ownership** where **(i)** market contracting with customers is very costly (high asymmetric information, lock-in, local market power) *and* **(ii)** customers are homogeneous enough that collective decision-making is cheap. Banking, insurance and agricultural marketing satisfy both. Retail banking is an information-asymmetry business par excellence; a local base of farmers, tradesmen or one employer's staff is a homogeneous ownership class. The framework also explains the *decline* of customer ownership in banking: once government deposit insurance and prudential regulation reduced the depositor's contracting problem — the state now certifies the bank's safety — the historic rationale weakened and investor ownership could compete on equal terms. [ANALYTICAL INFERENCE from Hansmann 1996 — the through-line connecting §0.2 to the demutualisation wave in §0.5.]

**§0.2.3 Why credit specifically — information asymmetry and local lending.** **Stiglitz and Weiss, "Credit Rationing in Markets with Imperfect Information" (*American Economic Review*, 1981)** is foundational. Their result is counter-intuitive: with asymmetric information, the interest rate does not clear the market. Raising the rate has two adverse effects — **adverse selection** (safe borrowers drop out first, leaving a riskier pool: higher prices attract worse types) and **moral hazard / incentive effects** (a higher rate induces riskier project choices, since the borrower keeps the upside). Because the bank's *expected return* can *fall* as the rate rises past some point, the bank rationally sets a below-clearing rate and **rations credit by quantity** — some creditworthy applicants get no loan at any price. [CONFIRMED FACT as to the model; the empirical prevalence of Stiglitz–Weiss rationing is CONTESTED IN THE LITERATURE — Arnold and Riley (*AER*, 2009) show the result is fragile to assumptions.] The upshot: precisely the borrowers a development-oriented institution most wants to reach — the poor, rural, collateral-less — are rationed out; member-based institutions solve this by embedding information and enforcement in social relationships.

**The Raiffeisen model.** Friedrich Wilhelm Raiffeisen (1818–1888) founded rural credit cooperatives in the German Rhineland from the 1860s (the Heddesdorf Darlehnskassen-Verein, 1864, is the archetype), defined by **unlimited joint liability**, tightly bounded membership so "all members would know each other," conservative lending, unpaid management, and accumulation of an indivisible collective reserve rather than dividends on shares. Unlimited joint liability turns each member into a monitor of every other, because a member's entire personal wealth is at stake if a fellow member defaults; local membership supplies information no outside bank could obtain. The scheme thus converts the Stiglitz–Weiss problems — problems of information and incentives — into problems the members are structurally motivated to solve. Notably, limited liability was not extended to German cooperatives until 1889. [CONFIRMED FACT.]

**The microfinance rediscovery.** **Maitreesh Ghatak and Timothy W. Guinnane, "The Economics of Lending with Joint Liability: Theory and Practice" (*Journal of Development Economics*, 60(1), 1999, pp. 195–228)** analyse how joint-liability group lending addresses a lender's four problems — **screening** (adverse selection), **monitoring** (moral hazard in effort), **state verification** (auditing claimed defaults) and **enforcement**. Key results: joint liability induces *assortative matching* (safe borrowers pair with safe borrowers, screening "by the company you keep" — Ghatak, *Economic Journal*, 1999/2000); harnesses peer monitoring; and substitutes social collateral for physical collateral. Crucially, Guinnane is also the leading economic historian of the German credit cooperatives (see his "Cooperatives as Information Machines: German Rural Credit Cooperatives, 1883–1914," *Journal of Economic History*, 2001, and Yale EGC working papers), drawing the explicit line: the nineteenth-century Raiffeisen cooperative and the twentieth-century Grameen-style group are the *same institutional technology* for the *same information problem*. [SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS.] His "A Failed Institutional Transplant: Raiffeisen's Credit Cooperatives in Ireland, 1894–1914" (*Explorations in Economic History*, 1994) carries a direct warning: the same form, dropped into different social and legal soil, failed — a caution for the Nigerian design question in Part VI.

**§0.2.4 The historical record and why forms differed.** The cooperative form was invented several times, by different people solving different problems; the differences in problem explain the differences in form.
- **Rochdale Society of Equitable Pioneers (1844).** Twenty-eight Lancashire weavers opened a consumer cooperative store and codified the *Rochdale Principles* (open membership, one-member-one-vote, limited return on capital, patronage dividend, cash trading, neutrality, education, cooperation among cooperatives). The problem was *consumer* exploitation; the form was consumer-owned. [CONFIRMED FACT.]
- **Hermann Schulze-Delitzsch (urban credit cooperatives, from 1850).** A lawyer and liberal politician; his urban *Vorschussvereine* / people's banks (ancestors of the Volksbanken) served artisans and small traders, used **subscribed share capital**, paid dividends on it, employed **paid professional management**, operated broadly, and after 1889 moved to **limited liability**. The clientele had some means; hence a capital-oriented, business-like form. [CONFIRMED FACT.]
- **Friedrich Wilhelm Raiffeisen (rural, from the 1860s).** Rural, unlimited joint liability, minimal share capital, indivisible reserves, volunteer management, tightly local. The clientele was cash-poor; hence a form economising on capital (which farmers lacked) and substituting joint liability and social information (which they had). **The Schulze-Delitzsch/Raiffeisen contrast is the single most instructive comparison in cooperative history** — two credit-cooperative models born in one country within fifteen years, differing precisely because their memberships differed in wealth, homogeneity and information environment, exactly as Hansmann predicts. [SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS.]
- **Alphonse Desjardins and the caisse populaire (Lévis, Quebec, 1900).** A parliamentary reporter who synthesised the two German models into the North American *caisse populaire*, founding the first at Lévis on 6 December 1900 (first deposit: ten cents), and later helping establish the first US credit union — St. Mary's Cooperative Credit Association, Manchester, New Hampshire (opened 1908/1909) — for a Franco-American Catholic parish. The common bond was parish and ethnicity. [CONFIRMED FACT.]
- **Edward Filene and the US credit union movement; the Federal Credit Union Act (1934).** Filene, a Boston merchant, and the Massachusetts banking commissioner Pierre Jay secured the first US general credit-union statute (Massachusetts Credit Union Act, 1909); Filene funded and the attorney Roy Bergengren ran the Credit Union National Extension Bureau (from 1921). The **Federal Credit Union Act of 1934**, signed by Roosevelt in the Depression, created a national chartering framework for member-owned, common-bond, non-profit credit unions, to supply consumer credit and displace loan sharks. [CONFIRMED FACT.]

The lesson carried forward: **institutional form tracks the problem and the membership.** Where members were poor and homogeneous, forms economised on capital and leaned on joint liability; where members had means, forms used share capital and professional management. Nigeria's founder must ask which problem, and which membership, the new institution actually faces.

**§0.2.5 The ICA Statement on the Cooperative Identity (1995) — stated, then assessed.** The International Cooperative Alliance adopted the **Statement on the Cooperative Identity** at its centennial Congress in Manchester in 1995 (adopted at the 31st World Cooperative Congress before 1,210 representatives from 207 national and 8 international organisations across over 90 countries). It defines a cooperative as "an autonomous association of persons united voluntarily to meet their common economic, social and cultural needs and aspirations through a jointly-owned and democratically-controlled enterprise," and codifies **seven principles**: (1) Voluntary and Open Membership; (2) Democratic Member Control; (3) Member Economic Participation; (4) Autonomy and Independence; (5) Education, Training and Information; (6) Cooperation among Cooperatives; (7) Concern for Community (added in 1995). The Statement is doctrinally treated as *indivisible*. [CONFIRMED FACT as to text and adoption.]

The critical assessment the founder needs — because the seven are not equally binding:
- **Economically load-bearing:** Principles 2 (Democratic Member Control) and 3 (Member Economic Participation) *constitute* the cooperative as an economic form; they are the source of both its advantages (§0.2.1) and its pathologies (§0.3). If these go, the cooperative has degenerated in substance whatever the label.
- **Aspirational:** Principle 7 (Concern for Community) and much of the values language matter for legitimacy and loyalty (economically real assets, per §0.6) but are not the mechanism by which the form works; treating them as the essence invites the promotional framing this study resists.
- **Operationally constraining — and directly relevant to capital:** Principle 4 (Autonomy and Independence) and the "limited compensation on capital" and "common property" elements of Principle 3 bear *directly* on Part II. Read literally, a principle that members receive only limited return on subscribed capital, that reserves are common (indivisible) property, and that autonomy from outside investors must be preserved is a principle *against* raising ordinary external equity. The cooperative principles do not merely describe the capital constraint of §0.4 — in part they *impose* it. This is the single most important tension in the study, flagged here for Part II to dissect.

### §0.3 Why cooperatives fail — the property-rights critique

This is the part the reader most needs, because it structures the Failure Catalogue appendix. It runs from 1950s labour-managed-firm theory through 1970s property-rights economics to Cook's 1995 synthesis and 1990s incomplete-contracts models.

**§0.3.1 The labour-managed firm: Ward, Domar, Meade.** **Benjamin Ward, "The Firm in Illyria: Market Syndicalism" (*American Economic Review*, 48(4), 1958, pp. 566–589)** modelled a worker-managed firm maximising **net income per member** and derived the notorious **perverse supply response**: when output price rises, the firm may *reduce* output and shed members, because adding the marginal worker dilutes income per existing member. [CONFIRMED FACT as to the model.] **Evsey Domar (*AER*, 1966)** generalised to multiple inputs and showed the perversity is not robust; **James Meade (*Economic Journal*, 1972)** analysed the firm's aversion to new members and its distinctive investment behaviour. The mature Ward–Domar–Vanek verdict (Vanek, *The General Theory of Labor-Managed Market Economies*, 1970) is nuanced: **the perverse response holds only under restrictive assumptions and empirical support is weak** (Pencavel and Craig's plywood-cooperative studies). [CONTESTED IN THE LITERATURE; the modern view (Putterman, *New Palgrave*, 2008) treats perversity as a special case, not a general property.] The residue for this study is not the supply curve — which concerns worker cooperatives — but the *method*: changing who owns the firm changes what it maximises, in ways formal analysis can predict.

**§0.3.2 Attenuated property rights: Furubotn and Pejovich.** **Eirik Furubotn and Svetozar Pejovich** ("Property Rights and Economic Theory," *Journal of Economic Literature*, 1972; Furubotn, "The Long-Run Analysis of the Labor-Managed Firm," *AER*, 1976) supplied the underinvestment mechanism. In a cooperative a member's claim on assets is **attenuated** — the member cannot sell a share in the enterprise's capital and the claim lapses on leaving — so the member rationally prefers surplus **paid out now** rather than **retained and invested** in long-lived assets whose returns cannot be captured or sold. The result is a systematic bias toward under-investment in collective capital and toward debt over retained earnings. [SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS that the mechanism is real; its magnitude is CONTESTED.] This is the parent of Cook's horizon and portfolio problems.

**§0.3.3 Cook (1995) and the vaguely-defined property-rights problems — correction verified.** The reader repeatedly referred to *"Cook's four problems"* and doubted the number. The doubt was well founded. The primary source is **Michael L. Cook, "The Future of U.S. Agricultural Cooperatives: A Neo-Institutional Approach," *American Journal of Agricultural Economics*, 77(5) (December 1995), pp. 1153–1159.** Cook identifies **five** — not four — property-rights problems (later termed "vaguely/ill-defined property rights" in Cook and Iliopoulos, 2000). **The reader's "four" was wrong; the correct number is five, and the canonical names are: (1) the free-rider problem, (2) the horizon problem, (3) the portfolio problem, (4) the control problem, and (5) the influence-costs problem.** [CONFIRMED FACT — verified against Cook 1995 as reported in Cook & Iliopoulos 2000 and corroborated by multiple independent secondary summaries.] Because this taxonomy structures the Failure Catalogue, it is fixed as canonical. Each problem is explained with a *financial-cooperative* worked example so the transfer to the reader's problem is explicit.

**(1) The free-rider (common-property) problem.** Rights are non-tradable and equally distributed, so benefits leak to those who did not pay. Cook distinguishes an *external* free-rider problem (non-members/new members capture the benefit of past investment) from an *internal / common-property* problem (a new member acquires the same claim on accumulated reserves, per unit of patronage, as a founder who built them). *Worked example:* a credit union spends ten years and its retained surplus building an underwriting platform and reserve buffer; a member joining in year eleven pays the same nominal share (say ₦10,000) and enjoys the same access and the same claim on common reserves as a founder who effectively financed the build-out through years of forgone refunds. Because the founder's share redeems at par and cannot be sold at an appreciated value, the founder's incentive to have financed it is attenuated — so rational members under-invest in exactly the long-lived shared assets (technology, brand, reserves) a growing institution most needs. [ANALYTICAL INFERENCE from Cook 1995 — and directly relevant to why a cooperative fintech may struggle to fund a large up-front platform build.]

**(2) The horizon problem — the single most important concept for a founder.** A member will not support investments whose returns extend beyond expected remaining tenure, because the claim ends at exit and cannot be sold for present value; the residual claim on an asset's income is *shorter* than the asset's life. *Worked example:* a cooperative bank contemplates a five-year investment (new core system, or a remittance corridor) with positive NPV realised in years four to eight; a member expecting to leave within three years gets none of the payoff but bears the cost now. If the median voter has a short horizon, the value-creating investment is voted down. The cooperative is biased toward short-payback projects and against long-gestation infrastructure — **the concept the founder must internalise above all others**, because it explains chronic under-investment and why the successful systems had to invent capital instruments and indivisible reserves (§0.4) to defeat it. [SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS on the mechanism; its empirical bite is CONTESTED — see "The Horizon Problem in Agricultural Cooperatives – Only in Theory?" in Chaddad et al.'s Springer volume.]

**(3) The portfolio problem.** Because a member's investment is tied to patronage, non-tradable and non-diversifiable, the member cannot adjust risk exposure independently of use, driving **excessively conservative investment preferences**: unable to diversify away the cooperative's risk, members want the cooperative itself to take little risk. *Worked example:* a Nigerian cooperative member holds savings, borrowing relationship and ownership stake all in one institution — a concentrated, undiversified exposure — and will rationally oppose a profitable but volatile new line of business because they cannot offset the added risk by trading their stake. Note that this same conservatism is the *source* of cooperative stability in §0.6 — a pathology in the property-rights critique and a virtue in the stability literature. [ANALYTICAL INFERENCE.]

**(4) The control problem.** The principal–agent problem in cooperative-specific form: residual claimants are dispersed, each holds a tiny non-saleable stake, and there is **no share price and no takeover market** to discipline managers. Monitoring falls to a volunteer board and an apathetic membership. *Worked example:* in an investor-owned bank a falling share price and takeover threat discipline value-destroying management; in a cooperative there is no price to fall and usually no way to acquire control, so complacent or self-serving management can persist for years — part of the Co-operative Bank story (§0.5). [SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS; the basis for Part III's governance analysis.]

**(5) The influence-costs problem.** When one organisation contains members with divergent interests and decisions are collective, members expend real resources trying to influence decisions (lobbying, factions, committee capture) — pure deadweight loss (concept from Milgrom and Roberts, 1990) that also distorts the decisions. *Worked example:* a cooperative serving both net-savers (wanting high deposit rates) and net-borrowers (wanting low loan rates) has two factions with directly opposed interests over its single most important pricing decision; each lobbies, the board's decision reflects factional power rather than efficiency, and the energy spent fighting is wasted. As a cooperative grows and diversifies, influence costs rise — which is why heterogeneity is the recurring villain of the whole account. [SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS.]

The unifying thread: all five stem from residual claims being **non-tradable and ill-defined as to who bears which cost and receives which benefit.** Fix tradability and definition and you mitigate all five — exactly what §0.4's instruments attempt, at a cost in cooperative purity.

**§0.3.4 Olson: collective action and scale.** **Mancur Olson, *The Logic of Collective Action* (Harvard University Press, 1965)** supplies the theory behind the control problem's dependence on size: in a large group a rational individual will *not* contribute to a collective good — here, monitoring management and participating in governance — because their contribution is negligible while its cost is private, and the benefit is shared regardless. The free-rider logic applies to *governance itself*. As membership grows, the rational member abstains: AGM attendance falls, board elections go uncontested, management's *de facto* autonomy expands. [SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS.] **The cooperative's democratic legitimacy is strongest when small and homogeneous and decays predictably as it grows** — the same growth the horizon and free-rider problems also punish. This is the engine of §0.3.7's degeneration and of Part III.

**§0.3.5 Hart and Moore: when is cooperative ownership efficient?** **Oliver Hart and John Moore, "The Governance of Exchanges: Members' Cooperatives versus Outside Ownership" (*Oxford Review of Economic Policy*, 12(4), 1996, pp. 53–69)** and **"Cooperatives vs. Outside Ownership" (NBER Working Paper 6421, 1998)** compare two "constitutions": a members' cooperative (control shared one-member-one-vote) and outside ownership (control and residual income to an investor), treating ownership as the allocation of *residual rights of control* over non-human assets. Central results: under **perfect competition**, outside ownership tends to achieve the first-best while a cooperative typically does not (the cooperative uses its cost advantage to shield members from competition, and collective decisions track the **median** member, not the average); both forms are inefficient but for different reasons (outside ownership tailors decisions to the marginal customer; the cooperative to the median member); and, decisively, **outside ownership becomes relatively more efficient as (i) the membership becomes more heterogeneous and (ii) competition intensifies.** [SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS; the formal restatement of Hansmann's homogeneity intuition.] This is the theoretical hinge of the study's diagnosis: cooperatives are efficient and durable where members are homogeneous and competition muted (nineteenth-century rural Germany; a tight common-bond credit union) and come under conversion pressure as membership diversifies and markets become competitive (the deregulated 1990s UK mortgage market). **This single comparative-static — heterogeneity plus competition erodes the cooperative advantage — reconciles the durability and fragility of §0.1.** [ANALYTICAL INFERENCE, a direct reading of Hart–Moore.]

**§0.3.6 Rasmusen: mutual banks take less risk.** **Eric Rasmusen, "Mutual Banks and Stock Banks" (*Journal of Law and Economics*, 31(2), 1988, pp. 395–421)** argues the mutual form, lacking outside equity holders with a levered call option on the bank's assets, gives decision-makers weaker incentives to take risk: managers cannot capture the upside through equity appreciation, and depositor-owners are risk-averse (the portfolio problem again). Prediction: **mutual/cooperative banks take less risk than stock banks** — a double-edged prediction running through the study (less risk means more stability, §0.6, but potentially under-investment and slower growth, §0.3.2–0.3.3). [SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS that ownership form affects risk-taking; sign and size vary by regime and competition — inter-war UK building-society and Massachusetts savings-bank studies find the low-risk prediction holds mainly for smaller institutions insulated from competition, echoing Hart–Moore.]

**§0.3.7 The degeneration thesis.** Traceable to the Webbs and developed by **Avner Ben-Ner** ("Comparative Empirical Observations on Worker-Owned and Capitalist Firms," *International Journal of Industrial Organization*, 1988) and **Chris Cornforth** ("Patterns of Cooperative Management: Beyond the Degeneration Thesis," *Economic and Industrial Democracy*, 1995): cooperatives tend over time to *degenerate* — either converting, or retaining the cooperative shell while control migrates to a managerial elite or a shrinking privileged core. The mechanisms are those already assembled (Olson's decay, the horizon and free-rider problems, the temptation to appropriate accumulated reserves). [CONTESTED IN THE LITERATURE — Cornforth argues degeneration is a tendency, not a law, and "regeneration" is possible; Cook's lifecycle framework, §0.7, models both.] Whether it applies to *financial* cooperatives is genuinely open: the worker-cooperative version (hiring non-member labour) does not map cleanly onto a bank, but the *substantive-control* version maps precisely — a cooperative bank that remains a cooperative in law while members become passive and management or outside capital takes effective control has degenerated in Ben-Ner's sense. The Co-operative Bank (UK) is the study's paradigm case (§0.5). [ANALYTICAL INFERENCE.]

**§0.3.8 A taxonomy of cooperative failure modes.** The theory yields a taxonomy of *how* a cooperative ceases to be a functioning member-owned institution. The Failure Catalogue appendix will populate each cell with real cases.

| Failure mode | Definition | Formal? | Driving mechanism (from theory) | Paradigm case |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Demutualisation** | Legal conversion to investor ownership, usually by member vote, distributing reserves | Yes — an event | Members cashing out reserves (free-rider/horizon); capital constraint; managerial incentive | UK building societies 1989–2000 |
| **Absorption** | Loss of independent existence through merger/acquisition into a larger (often non-cooperative) institution | Yes — a transaction | Sub-scale in a competitive market (Hart–Moore); inability to raise capital | Small building societies / credit unions post-crisis |
| **Degeneration** | Formal cooperative status retained; member control lost in substance | No — a process | Olson decay; control problem; outside capital injection | Co-operative Bank (UK), 2013–14 |
| **Insolvency** | Failure to meet obligations; resolution, wind-up or rescue | Event | Excessive risk-taking (where Rasmusen fails); capital inadequacy; fraud | Northern Rock (post-demutualisation) |

Two analytical points. First, the modes are **causally linked**: the capital constraint (§0.4) pushes a cooperative toward absorption or demutualisation, and degeneration often *precedes and enables* demutualisation or a capital-driven loss of control. Second, **only insolvency is unambiguously "failure" in the ordinary sense** — demutualisation can enrich current members; absorption can be an orderly rescue. The study insists the interesting question is not "did the institution survive?" but "did the *cooperative form* survive, and was that good for members, for financial stability, and for the underserved population it was meant to serve?"

### §0.4 The capital problem, stated theoretically

Everything above converges on a single binding constraint. **A financial cooperative's fundamental difficulty is that it cannot easily hold permanent, loss-absorbing equity capital, because its natural ownership instrument — the redeemable member share — is, in economic and regulatory substance, a liability rather than capital.** Part II is this section's full development.

**§0.4.1 Why member shares are not permanent capital.** A member share is typically **redeemable at or near par, on demand or on exit**: the member paid in ₦10,000 and on leaving is entitled to ₦10,000 back. The share does not appreciate, is not traded, and embodies no claim on accumulated reserves at true value. Economically the aggregate of member shares behaves like a **demand liability**: in stress, members exit and demand redemption exactly when the institution can least afford to pay — a run on *capital* rather than on deposits. Permanent equity, by contrast, never has to be repaid and absorbs losses first. The member share fails both tests. [SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS.]

**§0.4.2 The regulatory consequence: Basel III, CRR Article 29, and the redemption paradox.** Under Basel III as implemented in the EU through the **Capital Requirements Regulation (Regulation (EU) No 575/2013, "CRR")**, the top-quality capital — **Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1)** — must be permanent and fully loss-absorbing. **CRR Articles 27–29** create a specific regime for capital instruments of "mutuals, cooperative societies, savings institutions and similar institutions." **Article 29**, elaborated by the **EBA Regulatory Technical Standards on Own Funds (Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) No 241/2014)**, sets when cooperative members' shares count as CET1. The pivotal condition: **the institution must be able to refuse or limit redemption** (Article 29 CRR; the RTS conditions for refusing redemption; and the redemption-permission regime under Article 78 CRR / Article 32 of the RTS, which generally caps advance permission for redemptions at up to 2% of CET1). [CONFIRMED FACT.]

This produces the **redemption paradox**, the fulcrum of the capital analysis: *to make member shares count as regulatory capital, the cooperative must curtail the very redemption right that makes membership attractive; the instrument is capital to the exact extent that it is not really the member's to reclaim.* [ANALYTICAL INFERENCE, a direct implication of Article 29 CRR / IFRIC 2.] The same tension appears in accounting dress. **IFRIC 2, "Members' Shares in Co-operative Entities and Similar Instruments" (IASB/IFRS Interpretations Committee, issued November 2004, effective 1 January 2005)**, interpreting IAS 32, provides: members' shares the member can demand to have redeemed are **liabilities**, *unless* the entity has an **unconditional right to refuse redemption**, or local law/regulation/charter unconditionally prohibits redemption — in which case they are **equity**. [CONFIRMED FACT.] Both the prudential regulator and the accountant say the same thing: a redeemable member share is equity only insofar as it is *not* redeemable at the member's option.

**§0.4.3 Retained earnings and the growth arithmetic.** If subscribed shares are unreliable capital, **retained earnings** become the primary reliable source of true cooperative capital — with a precise, constraining implication. For a bank held to a fixed capital-to-assets ratio, assets can grow only as fast as capital. If the only source of new capital is retained earnings, the maximum **sustainable growth rate of assets** is **g\* ≈ ROE × retention ratio**; if the cooperative retains *all* surplus, g\* ≈ ROE. A cooperative bank earning a 6% return on equity and retaining everything can grow its balance sheet at roughly **6% per year and no faster** without breaching its capital ratio; paying a patronage refund lowers g\* further. An investor-owned competitor can **issue new equity** and grow far faster. This is the arithmetic behind demutualisation: in the deregulated, fast-growing 1990s UK mortgage market, mutuals constrained to internal-growth rates watched equity-funded rivals expand and concluded they had to convert to compete. [CONFIRMED FACT as to the arithmetic; ANALYTICAL INFERENCE as to its causal role, developed in §0.5.] The horizon and portfolio problems compound the constraint: members may not even *want* to retain earnings to fund growth whose benefits they will not capture, so the achievable rate can fall below even the arithmetic ceiling.

**§0.4.4 Indivisible reserves: the strongest solution, and its cost.** Some jurisdictions solve the problem by law, making reserves **indivisible** — incapable of distribution to members even on dissolution. In **France**, the *réserves impartageables* rest on the framework statute **Loi n° 47-1775 du 10 septembre 1947**: Article 16 mandates a minimum allocation of surplus to reserves (not less than 15% while reserves are below share capital), and Article 19 (as amended) requires that on dissolution the net assets, after repayment of paid-up capital, be devolved to other cooperatives or social-economy bodies — *not* to members. In **Italy**, Civil Code Article 2514 requires "prevalently mutual" cooperatives to prohibit distribution of reserves to members and, on dissolution, to devolve the entire residual assets to the mutual-promotion funds; **Law No. 59 of 31 January 1992** created those funds (e.g. **Coopfond** for Legacoop) and requires a payment of **3% of annual net profits** into them, while cooperative banks (banche di credito cooperativo) must allocate at least **70% of net profits to the legal reserve** under Article 37 of the Consolidated Law on Banking (TUB, D.Lgs. 385/1993). [CONFIRMED FACT.]

The trade-off is exact. **Indivisible reserves produce strong, permanent, run-proof capital** — un-redeemable, un-votable, un-cashable on demutualisation, so they also function as an *anti-demutualisation asset lock* (Cliff Mills, "A Study of Indivisible Reserves in Cooperatives in EU Member States," *International Journal of Cooperative Law*, No. 3, 2025). **But they weaken the individual member's incentive to build them** — the free-rider and horizon problem in purest form: why forgo a refund today to build a reserve you can never personally claim? The indivisible-reserve jurisdictions answer the property-rights critique on the *capital* side at the cost of sharpening it on the *incentive* side. That the French, Italian and German sectors are among the world's most durable (§0.6) suggests the trade is often worth making — an ANALYTICAL INFERENCE Part II will test.

**§0.4.5 The instruments invented to escape the trap.** The Prologue names them; Part II and the Capital-Instruments Catalogue dissect them.
- **Rabobank Certificates (Netherlands).** The most subordinated capital instrument of Coöperatieve Rabobank U.A.; they began as internally-traded Member Certificates (*Ledencertificaten*) and were **admitted to trading on Euronext Amsterdam on 27 January 2014**, converting a member-only instrument into a freely tradable perpetual security of €25 nominal (held via the trust office Stichting AK Rabobank Certificaten). Rabobank states they **form part of its Common Equity Tier 1 capital**, with distributions fully discretionary. Per Rabobank's official investor-relations disclosure, the €25 nominal per certificate "corresponds to an outstanding nominal amount of **EUR 7.8 billion**. A total number of **313,005,461 Rabobank Certificates have been issued, of which Rabobank holds 36,652,880 for its own account**, following a tender offer in 2024." [CONFIRMED FACT.] This is the model of a *permanent capital instrument* raising external CET1 while leaving cooperative governance formally intact.
- **Cooperative preference shares and subordinated member deposits** — instruments engineered to sit in Additional Tier 1 or Tier 2, providing loss-absorbing capacity below CET1.
- **A listed vehicle over a mutual base (Crédit Agricole, France).** In **December 2001** the former central body (Caisse Nationale de Crédit Agricole) was renamed **Crédit Agricole S.A. (CASA)** and listed on Euronext Paris, atop a mutual pyramid: about 2,400 Local Banks (Caisses Locales) own the 39 Regional Banks (Caisses Régionales), which control CASA through the holding company **SAS Rue La Boétie**, whose purpose is to hold **at least 50%** of CASA's capital and votes (it holds roughly 60% today, with the balance a public float). This raises listed equity while the cooperative base retains locked-in control — the canonical "listed vehicle over a mutual base." [CONFIRMED FACT; the sum raised in the 2001 IPO is reported variously — contemporary reporting ~€3.5bn; some later summaries ~€4.8bn — flagged as a figure to pin against the CASA prospectus in Part II.]
- **Secondary capital / subordinated debt for US credit unions.** Under the **NCUA's Subordinated Debt final rule (approved December 2020, effective 1 January 2022; 12 CFR Part 702, Subpart D)**, low-income-designated and "complex" credit unions may issue subordinated debt counting toward the net-worth ratio (for LICUs) and/or the risk-based-capital ratio (for complex credit unions), with maturities of 5–20 years; pre-2022 "secondary capital" is grandfathered for 20 years. Per NCUA Chairman Todd M. Harper's 2023 statement, "by the end of 2022, **154 credit unions of all types had issued $3.4 billion in subordinated debt** … nearly double the number … a decade ago"; by 31 March 2025 this had risen to **167 credit unions with $4.1 billion** outstanding (Catalyst Corporate FCU). [CONFIRMED FACT per NCUA.]

The common feature: every instrument tries to **make a member-owned institution's capital tradable, permanent, or externally-sourced — i.e. to undo the non-tradable property rights Cook identified as the root of the five problems.** Each buys capital at some cost in cooperative purity. That trade-off is what Part II exists to map.

### §0.5 The demutualisation wave — what the record shows

The theory predicts pressure to convert; the record shows what conversion produced. It contains the single most-cited piece of evidence in the demutualisation literature.

**§0.5.1 The UK building societies.** The **Building Societies Act 1986** permitted conversion to bank form, subject to a member vote. **Abbey National converted first, in 1989.** Then a wave: **Cheltenham & Gloucester (acquired by Lloyds, 1995); and in 1997 Halifax (the largest, having merged with Leeds Permanent), Woolwich, Alliance & Leicester, Northern Rock, and Bristol & West (acquired by Bank of Ireland); Bradford & Bingley followed in 2000.** [CONFIRMED FACT.] The mutual sector shrank drastically. The **Building Societies Association today represents all 42 UK building societies** (June 2025), dominated by Nationwide — which decisively **did not convert**, rebuffing carpetbagger-led attempts in 1998 and 2001; the BSA notes that consolidation has continued (Virgin Money was acquired by Nationwide in October 2024, and the Co-operative Bank by Coventry Building Society in January 2025). [CONFIRMED FACT.] **Carpetbagging** — opening a small account solely to qualify for the "windfall" free shares on conversion, then voting to convert and cashing out — was a major driver; it is the free-rider/horizon problem made flesh: current "members," many with no genuine stake in the future, voting to distribute reserves generations of prior members had built. [SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS.]

**The striking empirical fact — stated carefully.** Of the building societies that demutualised and floated, **not one survived the 2007–2009 financial crisis as an independent institution.** **Northern Rock** suffered the first run on a British bank in over a century in September 2007 and was nationalised in February 2008; **Bradford & Bingley** was nationalised/broken up in 2008 (branches to Santander); **Halifax** (within HBOS) was rescued by merger with Lloyds in 2008–09 and then state support; **Alliance & Leicester** was sold to Santander in 2008; **Abbey National** and **Woolwich** had already been absorbed (Abbey into Santander in 2004; Woolwich into Barclays in 2000); **Bristol & West** into Bank of Ireland. Meanwhile the mutual survivor — **Nationwide Building Society, the world's largest building society, serving over 16 million members with total assets of £367.9 billion (2025)** — came through the crisis **without state capital**. [CONFIRMED FACT as to each institution's fate.]

The interpretive care the study demands: this pattern is *consistent with* the thesis that demutualisation drove the converts toward the riskier, wholesale-funded, "originate-to-distribute" model the crisis destroyed (Northern Rock is the archetype), and it is the empirical centrepiece of the pro-mutual literature (the Cass/Bayes Business School reports for the BSA find building societies exhibited consistently higher z-scores — greater distance from insolvency — than converted banks). **But correlation is not the whole story.** The converts were disproportionately the *most aggressive growers* (that is partly *why* they converted), so selection, not just ownership form, is at work; and the sample is small. The honest statement: **the demutualised UK societies as a class fared markedly worse in the crisis than the mutual survivors, powerful suggestive evidence that mutual ownership restrained the fatal risk-taking — but the sample is small and confounded by selection, so it is strong corroboration rather than clean proof.** [CONTESTED IN THE LITERATURE as to how much weight the pattern bears; the facts of each fate are CONFIRMED.]

**§0.5.2 US mutual-to-stock conversions.** The US saw a larger-numbers phenomenon: conversion of **mutual savings institutions (thrifts) to stock form** across the 1980s–1990s, and **demutualisation of mutual insurers** (life and property-casualty). A hybrid device features prominently: the **mutual holding company (MHC)** structure, where a mutual parent retains majority control while a minority stock issue raises outside equity — a halfway house and a direct analogue of the Crédit Agricole idea. The thrift experience fed the risk-taking literature (Masulis on conversion wealth effects, 1987; the Massachusetts savings-bank studies), which broadly finds conversion to stock form associated with increased risk-taking — consistent with Rasmusen. [SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS on direction; magnitudes CONTESTED.]

**§0.5.3 Partial and hybrid forms — and the Co-operative Bank as the degeneration case.** Three cases show the boundary is a spectrum, not a line:
- **Crédit Agricole (2001)** — listed vehicle over a mutual base: outside equity raised, cooperative control retained. Demutualisation of *capital* without demutualisation of *control*.
- **Rabobank Certificates** — external CET1 raised on a listed market with cooperative governance untouched: a still more conservative hybrid.
- **The Co-operative Bank (UK), 2013–14 — the paradigm of degeneration without a demutualisation vote.** Until 2013 the Co-operative Bank plc was wholly owned by the Co-operative Group (itself a consumer cooperative owned by millions of members). A **£1.5 billion capital shortfall** was discovered in 2013 (following the aborted acquisition of the Lloyds "Verde" branches and losses from the earlier Britannia Building Society merger). The rescue was a **debt-for-equity swap ("bail-in")** in which subordinated bondholders — led by US hedge funds — took equity. The result: **the Co-operative Group's ownership fell from 100% to about 30% (and later to nil), and the bank passed into hedge-fund control — without any member vote to demutualise.** [CONFIRMED FACT.] This is degeneration in the precise §0.3.7 sense: the institution kept the name and the ethical marketing while ceasing, in substance, to be member-controlled or even cooperatively-owned. It is the clearest demonstration that **a cooperative can be lost to the capital constraint (§0.4) without anyone ever voting to give it up** — a direct cautionary tale for a capital-hungry cooperative fintech.

**§0.5.4 Why does demutualisation happen? Adjudicating three accounts.** (1) **Managerial-expropriation**: conversion is driven by managers who gain pay, options and freedom, with the members' windfall as bait — post-conversion director remuneration rose sharply, and a 2005 UK cross-party inquiry found members were on average worse off within a few years through worse rates than the one-off windfall compensated. [SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS that managerial incentives mattered; the specific inquiry finding is drawn partly from advocacy sources and read with §0.0.2 caution.] (2) **Capital-constraint**: conversion is driven by genuine need for external equity to compete (the §0.4.3 arithmetic) — supported by the post-1986 timing and the converts' equity-funded expansion. (3) **Members-cashing-out**: conversion is driven by current members and carpetbaggers appropriating reserves they did not build — supported by the central role of the windfall. **Adjudication:** the three are complementary and reinforce each other — **demutualisation was over-determined: it required a genuine competitive/capital pressure to make it defensible, an appropriable reserve to make it attractive, and an agent (management or carpetbaggers) to organise the member vote, and was most likely where all three coincided, as in the large growth-oriented UK societies.** The strongest counter-evidence to the claim that demutualisation *created value for members* is §0.5.1: the converts did not, in the main, survive. [ANALYTICAL INFERENCE, well grounded.]

### §0.6 Why financial cooperatives nonetheless persist

The property-rights critique predicts pervasive under-investment, control failure and conversion, yet the German, Dutch, French, Quebec and US cooperative banking sectors are large, old and stable. **The persistence of European cooperative banking is the fact the property-rights critique does not straightforwardly explain, and any honest theory must confront it.**

**§0.6.1 Scale and survival.** The sectors are not marginal: the German cooperative financial network (BVR members plus DZ Bank) intermediates over a trillion euros; Crédit Agricole and Groupe BPCE are among Europe's largest banking groups; Rabobank is globally significant; Desjardins dominates Quebec; the US credit-union system serves over a hundred million members. These institutions survived world wars, the Depression, and 2008. [CONFIRMED FACT.] The IMF has noted that in developing countries cooperative finance held roughly a quarter (~24%) of banking-sector assets. [CONFIRMED FACT per IMF reporting.]

**§0.6.2 The stability evidence — Hesse and Čihák, and its critics.** The central reference is **Heiko Hesse and Martin Čihák, "Cooperative Banks and Financial Stability" (IMF Working Paper WP/07/02, January 2007)**. Using bank-level data (BankScope, 1994–2004) and the **z-score** (distance to insolvency, combining profitability, capitalisation and return volatility; higher means safer), they conclude, verbatim from the abstract: *"Contrary to some suggestions in the literature, we find that cooperative banks are more stable than commercial banks. This finding is due to the lower volatility of the cooperative banks' returns, which more than offsets their lower profitability and capitalization. This is most likely due to cooperative banks' ability to use customer surplus as a cushion in weaker periods."* [CONFIRMED FACT as to what the paper says.] They also find that in systems with a high cooperative presence, *weak commercial banks* are somewhat less stable, so the system-stability effect is positive on average but statistically insignificant in some specifications. Companion work: Wim Fonteyne, "Cooperative Banks in Europe — Policy Issues" (IMF WP/07/159, 2007).

**The critical reading.** First, the result is partly *definitional*: lower return volatility is close to what a z-score rewards, and cooperatives' lower volatility partly reflects their lower risk-taking (Rasmusen) and conservatism (the portfolio problem) — so the "stability" finding and the "under-investment" critique may be **two descriptions of the same conservatism**, virtue and vice at once. Second, it is an *average* over a pre-crisis window and does not license the federation claim that cooperatives are unconditionally safe (the Co-operative Bank, and assorted cooperative-bank and credit-union failures, refute that). Third, later work is mixed: Kick and von Westernhagen find German cooperative and savings banks less risky than commercial banks, while others question whether the advantage survives once network/support-scheme effects are stripped out. The defensible statement: **there is real, peer-reviewed evidence that cooperative banks exhibit lower return volatility and greater distance-to-insolvency on average — most plausibly because member ownership and the absence of a profit-maximisation mandate reduce risk-taking and let them smooth returns using customer surplus — but this is an average tendency, not a guarantee, and it is inseparable from the same conservatism the property-rights critique counts as a cost.** [CONTESTED IN THE LITERATURE at the margins; the core Hesse–Čihák finding is robust and much-cited.]

**§0.6.3 Institutional protection schemes and networks.** Much of the sectors' durability is *architectural*, not intrinsic to the form: the successful systems built **institutional protection schemes (IPS)** and central institutions that mutualise risk. Germany's **BVR** scheme (1934) is the exemplar — member banks jointly guarantee each other and the scheme intervenes pre-emptively to prevent any member's insolvency (the basis of the §0.1 "no member bankruptcy since 1934" claim). Under **CRR Article 113(7)** an IPS can be officially recognised, allowing members a 0% risk weight on intra-network exposures; and since 2015 the German cooperative and savings-bank IPSs are also recognised as statutory deposit-guarantee schemes. [CONFIRMED FACT.] The point for Parts III–IV: **much of what looks like the resilience of the cooperative *form* is really the resilience of a cooperative *network with shared infrastructure and mutualised solvency support*** — a distinction that matters enormously for a lone Nigerian start-up, which would enjoy the form without the network. [ANALYTICAL INFERENCE — a central design lesson for Part VI.]

**§0.6.4 Countercyclical lending and the ownership-risk argument.** Two further well-supported claims: **countercyclical lending** (not driven to protect a share price and able to lean on customer surplus, cooperative banks tend to maintain or expand lending in downturns when investor-owned banks retrench — surveyed in McKillop et al.), and the **ownership-risk argument** (member ownership dampens the risk-taking incentives that outside equity, a levered call option, creates in stock banks — Rasmusen; §0.5's crisis evidence). Both are economically real and both are double-edged: the same features that stabilise also slow growth and can entrench under-investment. [SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS on the direction of the effects.]

**§0.6.5 The synthesis the study carries.** Reconciling §0.3 and §0.6: the property-rights critique is *correct about mechanisms* but *wrong if read as a prediction of inevitable failure.* Cooperatives persist where the institutional environment neutralises the mechanisms — homogeneous membership (low influence and collective-decision costs, per Hart–Moore and Hansmann), indivisible reserves and legal asset locks (defeating the cash-out incentive), protection schemes and central institutions (solving capital and scale collectively), and a competitive niche not dominated by capital-hungry growth. They convert or degenerate where the environment activates the mechanisms — heterogeneous membership, distributable reserves, no network, and intense capital-intensive competition. **This is not two contradictory literatures; it is one conditional theory in which ownership form interacts with the legal and institutional environment.** [ANALYTICAL INFERENCE — the thesis that organises the whole study.]

### §0.7 The cooperative lifecycle

The property-rights problems are not present at birth; they emerge with age and scale. The best available temporal model is **Michael Cook's cooperative lifecycle framework**, stated most fully in **Cook, "A Life Cycle Explanation of Cooperative Longevity" (*Sustainability*, 10(10), 2018)** and **Cook and Molly Burress, "A Cooperative Life Cycle Framework" (2009)**, developed from interviews and fieldwork with (on Cook's account) more than 5,000 cooperative leaders. The framework has **five phases**: (1) **economic justification** (the cooperative forms to solve a market failure — the Hansmann/Stiglitz–Weiss conditions of §0.2; members homogeneous, value proposition clear); (2) **organisational design** (it builds rules, governance and capital structure — choices here, e.g. redeemable shares vs. indivisible reserves, determine how severely the property-rights problems later bite); (3) **growth, glory and heterogeneity** (success brings growth, growth brings **heterogeneity of membership**, which is the trigger: the five problems and the influence/collective-decision costs emerge and intensify — success itself plants the seeds); (4) **recognition and introspection** (leadership confronts the mounting ownership costs and conflicts); (5) **choice** (a decision point with a menu: **exit** — dissolve, sell, demutualise; **continuation** — muddle on; **conversion** — change form, the demutualisation of §0.5; or **reinvention/regeneration** — redesign the property-rights and capital structure to restore the alignment of phase 1). [SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS that this is the standard temporal model.]

Cook's distinctive, hopeful claim — distinguishing his framework from the deterministic degeneration thesis — is that a well-led cooperative can **re-invent** at phase 5 and begin a *new* lifecycle, so longevity is repeated regeneration, driven by "cooperative genius" (keeping ownership costs below perceived market-contracting costs). [The "reinvention vs. regeneration" refinement is an active debate — see the *Sustainability* 2023 exchange.] Other lifecycle models can be cross-referenced (Ferguson and McKillop's nascent/transition/mature typology for credit unions; the Mondragón-based degeneration-stage models of Meister and Cornforth); Part III will use them.

**Why this matters more than any other single point for a founder.** The lifecycle model tells the reader that **the pathologies of §0.3 are diseases of success and scale, not of founding.** A new Nigerian cooperative fintech will not feel the horizon or influence-cost problems on day one, when members are few and homogeneous and the mission is clear (phase 1). It will feel them acutely *if it succeeds and grows* into a large, heterogeneous membership (phase 3) — precisely when a remittance-offering, capital-hungry, technology-dependent institution most needs to invest for the long term. The design choices made at founding (phase 2) — capital structure, reserve rules, governance architecture, membership definition — determine whether phase 3 is survivable. That is why the theory must be settled *before* Part VI's design brief, and why this study exists.

## Recommendations

These are staged next steps for the reader as author-founder, with the thresholds that would change them. They are framed as what the theory obliges the *later Parts* to resolve; the Prologue itself makes no jurisdiction-specific design choice.

**Stage 1 — Adopt the corrected apparatus now (immediate).** Fix Cook's **five** (not four) property-rights problems as the canonical spine of the Failure Catalogue; fix the four failure modes (demutualisation / absorption / degeneration / insolvency); and fix the evidence-classification scheme and inverted source hierarchy (statute first). *Benchmark that would change this:* none — this is settled and verified against Cook (1995).

**Stage 2 — Treat capital as the design problem, not a downstream detail (Part II).** Because member shares are a liability in substance (IFRIC 2 / CRR Art. 29) and internal-only growth caps expansion near ROE, the founder must decide *at founding* how the institution will hold permanent capital. The menu is now defined: indivisible reserves + asset lock (strongest, but weak member incentive to build); a permanent capital instrument on the Rabobank-Certificate model (external CET1, governance intact); a listed-vehicle-over-mutual-base on the Crédit Agricole model (control retained, capital raised); or subordinated/secondary capital on the US-credit-union model. *Benchmark:* if the business plan's required asset-growth rate exceeds achievable ROE by a wide margin, an internal-only structure is disqualified and one of the external-capital instruments becomes mandatory — quantify this in Part II.

**Stage 3 — Design governance for phase 3 before you reach it (Part III).** Because Olson and Hart–Moore predict member control decays with scale and heterogeneity, the founding constitution should embed the scaling mechanisms the durable systems used (delegate/representative structures, regional tiers, professionalised but member-accountable boards) *before* growth erodes participation. *Benchmark:* falling AGM participation, uncontested board elections, or widening net-saver/net-borrower factional conflict are the leading indicators that phase 3 has arrived and governance must already be in place.

**Stage 4 — Do not attempt to enjoy the form without the network (Parts IV–VI).** The European resilience is largely the resilience of *networks with mutualised solvency support*, which a lone Nigerian start-up lacks. Part IV must decide how to substitute (a cooperative-of-cooperatives, shared IT, a private protection arrangement) and Part V must map the actual Nigerian regulatory perimeter (cooperative society vs. microfinance bank vs. PSP/mobile-money licence for remittance). *Benchmark:* if the Nigerian legal environment offers neither an asset lock nor a route to a protection network, the degeneration/absorption risk documented in §0.5 rises sharply and the design must compensate with conservative capital and tight common-bond membership.

**Stage 5 — Test transplantability explicitly (Part VI).** Guinnane's failed Irish transplant is the standing warning: the same form in different soil fails. Part VI's design brief must state, feature by feature, which elements of the successful systems depend on institutions Nigeria has and which depend on institutions it lacks.

## Caveats

- **This is Stage 1 of eight; work pauses at the natural boundary of the completed Prologue.** No Part I material is begun. Every convention, taxonomy and thesis set here is provisional to the extent later Parts, working against statute and data, revise it — but the Cook five-problem correction and the evidence/source conventions are final.
- **The reader's "Cook's four problems" was an error and has been corrected to five** (free-rider, horizon, portfolio, control, influence-costs), verified against Cook (1995) via Cook & Iliopoulos (2000) and multiple independent secondary confirmations. The primary text itself (Wiley/AJAE) is paywalled and was not read in full; the enumeration is nonetheless CONFIRMED by convergent secondary sourcing.
- **Cooperative-federation sources are promotional and were treated as claims to be tested.** The BVR "no member insolvency since 1934" and pro-mutual z-score claims come partly from federation/advocacy material and are labelled accordingly; the load-bearing stability claim rests on the peer-reviewed Hesse–Čihák (2007) IMF paper, not on federation assertion.
- **The UK crisis-survival evidence is strong but confounded.** That no demutualised society survived 2008 independently while Nationwide did is a CONFIRMED set of facts, but the sample is small and the converts self-selected as aggressive growers, so it is powerful corroboration of the mutual-stability thesis rather than clean causal proof.
- **Two figures remain to be pinned in Part II.** The 2001 Crédit Agricole IPO proceeds are reported variously (~€3.5bn contemporary vs. ~€4.8bn in later summaries); this should be verified against the CASA prospectus. The Rabobank outstanding figure (€7.8bn / 313,005,461 certificates, post-2024 tender) and NCUA subordinated-debt figures (154 CUs / $3.4bn end-2022; 167 CUs / $4.1bn by Q1 2025) are current as cited but move over time.
- **The theory diverges from the evidence in interesting places, and the study preserves the divergence rather than resolving it artificially** — most notably that the property-rights critique predicts fragility while European cooperative banking is durable. The Prologue's resolution (one conditional theory in which ownership form interacts with the legal-institutional environment) is an ANALYTICAL INFERENCE, defensible but not a settled consensus, and Parts II and III are the places it is tested against evidence.
