# PART VIII — THE BUREAU

## *The Cooperative Encyclopedia: Legal Form, Capital, Governance and the Nigerian Path*

*Stage 10 of the extended commission. Weight: heavy.*

---

## TL;DR

- **The founder's central premise — that owning the platform gives him a credit bureau's data by construction — is mostly an illusion.** Holding data as a processor is not permission to repurpose it for credit reporting; a bureau needs four things (lawful data supply, legal right to the new purpose, standalone predictive value, and paying lender demand), and the platform supplies at most one and a half. The reciprocity cold-start is only *partly* solved, and only if a specific data-rights chain is written into the platform and cooperative terms **at launch, not retrofitted**.
- **A cooperative-sector bureau can survive only as a *complement* to the three incumbents, never as a competitor** — the Quod-versus-Serasa natural experiment shows even Brazil's five largest banks, acting together with their own data and regulatory blessing, could not dislodge an incumbent bureau. The genuine niche is the thin-file population between Nigeria's ~64% formal financial inclusion and its ~13% credit-file coverage — but the state is already occupying that niche with **CoopCHECK (powered by CreditRegistry)** and **CREDICORP's NIN-anchored national scoring**, with more capital and a better identity rail.
- **The verdict: the bureau should be a *product of the platform*, not the fifth company.** The ₦500m minimum capital is ten times the founder's entire ₦50m starting capital; the revenue base is structurally thin; the founder-inside-every-layer conflict lattice is not curable by firewalls; and the value is capturable as a furnishing-and-scoring feature plus a partnership margin long before it is capturable as an owned licensed entity. Defer or abandon the standalone entity until six specific triggers (VIII.11) converge.

## Key Findings

1. **Legal foundation [CF].** The Credit Reporting Act 2017 (assented 30 May 2017), read with the CBN Guidelines for the Licensing, Operations and Regulation of Credit Bureaux (2008, revised 2013), governs. A CBN licence is mandatory (s.2); minimum capital is **₦500,000,000** (2008 Guidelines); the ₦10,000,000-per-incident penalty for inaccurate reporting and the 10-working-day dispute clock are the enforcement teeth.

2. **Cooperatives *can* be Credit Information Providers [SC]** — the legal category is open to them — but eligibility to furnish is not the same as an obligation to furnish, nor the same as the founder's bureau being permitted to *ingest* platform-held data. Those are separate questions with harder answers.

3. **The data-rights chain is the make-or-break issue [CF/AI].** Member → cooperative (controller) → platform (processor) → bureau (separate controller, new purpose). The operative lawful basis for the furnishing transfer is the cooperative acting as a CIP under the CRA's statutory permission — **not consent, not bare legitimate interest** — and it works only if the bureau is licensed and members were noticed at collection. Purpose limitation means ledger data is *not* automatically available for credit reporting.

4. **Reciprocity is the moat, and it is deep [CF].** The Quod experiment establishes that a new bureau cannot dislodge incumbents even with furnishers' own data. The founder's only defensible position is complementary thin-file coverage.

5. **The state is the real competitor [CF].** CoopCHECK and CREDICORP's national scoring occupy the exact niche, better capitalised and NIN-anchored — a state-blessed competitor, not a tailwind.

6. **The conflict lattice is uncurable by firewalls [AI].** Being vendor, competing-cooperative owner, apex operator and scorer at once produces conflicts at the level of *role*, not data. At minimum the founder must not own both the competing anchor cooperative and the bureau.

7. **Economics do not support a standalone entity [AI].** ₦500m capital floor, thin cooperative-query revenue, external-lender revenue gated behind coverage the founder cannot reach quickly. Best captured as a platform feature and partnership margin.

## Details

### VIII.1 What a credit bureau is, legally, in Nigeria

**The controlling statute is the Credit Reporting Act 2017 (CRA), assented on 30 May 2017, read together with the CBN's Guidelines for the Licensing, Operations and Regulation of Credit Bureaux (2008, revised 2013).** [CF] The Act is short and principle-based. Its objectives (s.1) are to facilitate access to credit and enhance risk management; to promote accurate, fair and reliable credit information and protect its privacy; to set standards for the establishment and operation of bureaux; to promote a fair and competitive credit-reporting system; and to promote responsibility in the credit market.

**Who may operate a bureau.** Section 2 provides that no person shall establish, operate or conduct the business of a credit bureau unless licensed by the Central Bank of Nigeria. [CF] The applicant must be incorporated in Nigeria as a company limited by shares under CAMA; must meet the minimum capital requirement; and must satisfy such other requirements as the CBN prescribes. The application goes in writing to the CBN Governor. The Act deliberately does *not* fix the capital figure — it delegates that to CBN regulation, a choice flagged at the 2017 public hearing by Dr Jumoke Oduwole (then PEBEC Secretary), who argued the number belonged in guidelines so it could be adjusted without amending the Act. [CF]

**The three-stage licence.** Under the 2008 Guidelines, licensing proceeds through (1) receipt and appraisal of application, (2) Approval-in-Principle (valid 180 days, within which all conditions must be met), and (3) a Final Operating Licence granted after on-site verification. [CF] A non-refundable application fee of ₦250,000 accompanies the application. The licence expires automatically if the bureau has not commenced business within six months of issue. Every licensee must add "Credit Bureau" to its corporate name, and may not use "Central," "Federal," "National," "Nigeria," "Reserve," "State" or various religious words in its name.

**Minimum capital.** The 2008 Guidelines set the minimum capital requirement at **₦500,000,000 (five hundred million naira)** — half released on grant of Approval-in-Principle, the balance with accrued interest on final licence. [CF] This is the most consequential single number in the Part for sequencing (see VIII.11). Whether the CBN has revised the figure upward since 2013 is **UNKNOWN** on the public record; ₦500m is the last published number and should be treated as a floor, not a ceiling, given naira depreciation since 2008. [U]

**Permitted and prohibited activities.** The bureau collects credit and related information, aggregates it, maintains a database, and issues reports and scores to authorised users for a *permissible purpose*. The permissible purposes are enumerated: an application for credit or to be a guarantor; review or restructuring of existing facilities; account opening as KYC; funds transfers of **₦1,000,000 and above**; employment checks; tenancy contracts; insurance underwriting; acceptance of guarantees; contract or post-paid service applications; court judgment; credit scoring; and written consent of the data subject. [CF] The bureau *may not* use information outside these purposes; *may not* disclose the database to any person other than the CBN or its nominee; and owes, through its shareholders, directors and officers, a perpetual duty of confidentiality. Breach of the confidentiality provisions attracts a ₦500,000 fine and, for a continuing offence, up to ₦10,000 per day, plus blacklisting of the officers involved.

**Obligations on providers and users.** A Credit Information Provider (CIP) furnishes data; a Credit Information User (CIU) queries it. Critically, **a CIP may disclose a data subject's credit information to a bureau *without* the subject's prior consent** — the Act deems the ordinary banker–customer confidentiality duty waived to the extent needed to perform obligations under the Act (this is the statutory-permission basis in VIII.5). [CF] By contrast, a CIU generally needs *either* the subject's written consent *or* a Data Exchange Agreement (DEA) with the bureau plus a permissible purpose. All financial institutions must hold DEAs with at least two bureaux and obtain a report from at least two before granting a facility. A CIU that declines an application on the basis of a report must, within **15 working days**, tell the subject the reasons and provide a free report copy — Nigeria's adverse-action rule and the local root of the explainability constraint the Experian study identified.

**Consumer rights.** A data subject has the right to one free credit report per year; the right to dispute inaccurate information; the right to be told the reason for an adverse decision and receive a free report copy; and privacy and confidentiality rights. [CF] Consent, where required, is valid only for the specific purpose granted and lapses when that purpose is satisfied — so each transaction requires its own consent, a point of friction with ongoing monitoring products.

**Penalties.** The Act imposes a **₦10,000,000** fine on a CIP or bureau (jointly or severally) for intentionally or negligently providing, disclosing, altering or tampering with credit information so as to misrepresent it, or for obtaining information under false pretence or for a non-permissible purpose. [CF] Data subjects who suffer loss may sue in tort and under the Act; directors and officers face personal liability.

**The CBN's role and the two public registries.** The CBN is licensor, supervisor and appellate body. Under s.13, a data subject whose complaint is not resolved by the bureau or CIP within 10 days may escalate to the CBN; if the CBN does not resolve it within a further 10 days, the subject may go to court. The CBN also operates its own **Credit Risk Management System (CRMS)** — a *public* credit registry, legally distinct from the private bureaux. The CRMS traces to the 1990 Presidential Budget Speech and was given legal backing by the CBN Act 1991 (ss.28 and 52, as amended); it originally captured all bank credits with an outstanding balance of ₦100,000+, later raised to ₦1,000,000+. [CF] The "redesigned" CRMS (Regulatory Guidelines of 27 February 2017) introduced a "submit before disbursement" rule and mandatory BVN/TIN tagging; from 2022 the CBN extended CRMS enrolment to Other Financial Institutions — DFIs, microfinance banks, primary mortgage banks and finance companies. The CRA (s.4) provides that the CRMS continues its regulatory and prudential functions and *shares its information with the licensed bureaux on request* — so the CRMS is both a competitor data source and a feeder. The **Global Standing Instruction (GSI)** (Guidelines of 14 July 2020) lets a creditor bank recover a defaulted amount by debiting the defaulter's accounts across all banks, keyed to the BVN — a recovery tool running off the CRMS. The **National Collateral Registry** (movable-assets registry) sits alongside as the registry for security interests in movable property; it is distinct credit infrastructure, not a bureau. [CF]

**The pivotal question: are cooperative societies Credit Information Providers?** The answer, so far as the evidence permits, is **yes — cooperatives fall within the class of entities that may be CIPs.** [SC] The leading practitioner commentary on the CRA (Greychapel Legal) states plainly that CIPs "typically include banks, microfinance institutions, **cooperative societies** and insurance companies," alongside the Act's express inclusion of utilities and post-paid service suppliers. [SC] The 2008 Guidelines list, among permitted data sources, "institutions that offer credit to medium, small and micro enterprises" and "other entities that have data and information that serves the purposes of the company" — language broad enough to cover a thrift-and-credit cooperative. So the legal *category* is open. But being *eligible* to furnish is not being *obliged* to furnish (cooperatives are not compelled as banks are), nor is it the same as the founder's bureau being permitted to ingest a cooperative's data merely because his platform processes it. Those distinct questions are answered in VIII.4 and VIII.5.

### VIII.2 The incumbents

**Nigeria has three licensed credit bureaux: CRC Credit Bureau Limited, FirstCentral Credit Bureau Limited, and CreditRegistry (CR Services (Credit Bureau) Plc).** [CF] All three predate the CRA — the private-bureau industry was introduced under the 2008 CBN Guidelines in 2008–2009 — and all three belong to the Credit Bureau Association of Nigeria (CBAN). The founder would enter a market that has had roughly fifteen to seventeen years to accumulate data depth and lock in furnisher reciprocity.

**CreditRegistry (CR Services (Credit Bureau) Plc).** Established in 2003, it is the oldest of the three; its parent is CreditRegistry Corporation, a software technology company headquartered in Washington State, USA — so it is, at root, foreign-founded, with a Nigerian private-equity minority investor, ARM Capital Partners (the PE arm of ARM), holding a board seat representing investor interest. [CF] Its flagship consumer score is the **SMARTScore** (Nigeria's first credit bureau score, 100–999). B2B products include portfolio monitoring, skip tracing and its "GetPaidFaster" late-payer product. Its CEO Jameelah Sharrieff-Ayedun is publicly reported (BusinessDay interview) to have "grown the company to profitability by 858%" with "5-year average YoY revenue growth of 202%" — growth percentages only; **no absolute naira revenue or profit figure is publicly disclosed.** [CF]

**CRC Credit Bureau Limited.** Established in 2008 and licensed under the CBN Act 2007, CRC was founded by a consortium of ten leading financial institutions together with Dun & Bradstreet; D&B remains a partner and CRC now runs a group structure including CRC Data and Analytics Limited. [CF] Its current shareholder register is not public, and there is no evidence of a change of control away from the founding bank consortium plus D&B. [U] CRC uses the FICO scoring model (300–850) and markets Self Enquiry, I-CONplus monitoring, Portfolio Monitoring Review, skip tracing, and identity/fraud tools. It is the source of the strongest concrete scale figures in the market: its CEO Dr Ahmed 'Tunde Popoola reported (BusinessDay, June 2024) that **"29.4 million searches...conducted in 2023, and more than 10 million in the first quarter of 2024 alone,"** with 33 million on CRC's own database; and later (Guardian Nigeria, "50 million Nigerians now have credit scores") that **"while about 70 million Nigerians currently enjoy credit, over 50 million now have credit scores,"** the figure having risen from 30 million in August 2024. [CF] A third-party data-broker estimate puts CRC's 2025 revenue at about US$8.5m, but this is from a sales-intelligence vendor, not audited accounts — treat as low-confidence. [H]

**FirstCentral Credit Bureau Limited.** Originally incorporated as XDS Solutions Limited (RC 623894) in May 2005 and granted its final operating licence on 28 May 2009, FirstCentral leveraged at inception the technical partnership of Xpert Decision Systems (XDS) Pty of South Africa. [CF] Its distinguishing governance feature is a self-imposed policy that no financial institution may own equity or participate in management, marketed as making it "Nigeria's only independent credit bureau." Its score is the X-Score; other products include iRemedy, snapcheck identity verification, and Portfolio Monitoring Review. Its scale claims have escalated: static marketing copy cites "over 1 billion records" and "3,000+ subscribers," while at its 20th-anniversary communication (October 2025) its MD Oladimeji Peters stated the bureau "now holds over 2 billion historic records and more than 40 million unique customer profiles." [CF] The internal inconsistency between "1 billion/3,000" and "2 billion/40 million" on the company's own materials is itself instructive about how to read bureau self-reporting.

**The "largest" claims.** CRC, CreditRegistry and FirstCentral each brand themselves, in current materials, as "Nigeria's largest" bureau — self-reported, mutually inconsistent, and unverifiable, because **none publishes public financial statements** (CRC and FirstCentral are private limited companies; CR Services, though a Plc, is not NGX-listed). [CF] The absence of audited accounts is itself a finding: the sector's true unit economics are opaque, which should temper any projection the founder builds. [AI]

**CBAN.** The Credit Bureau Association of Nigeria was set up in 2012 by the three licensed bureaux to promote credit reporting and advocate favourable policy. [CF] It co-designed, with the CBN and the IFC, the **Common Data Template** launched in 2016, standardising the four categories of data submitted (demographic; credit/facility; enquiries; collateral/litigation). CBAN is small, but it is the institutional expression of the reciprocity cartel — where the incumbents coordinate the data-sharing standards a fourth entrant would have to join or replicate.

**Coverage.** The World Bank's Doing Business series recorded Nigerian **private credit bureau coverage at 7.8% of adults in 2017**, up from a record low of 4.9% in 2013. [CF] By 2025, CREDICORP publicly stated that **only about 13% of Nigerians have credit bureau records** — MD/CEO Uzoma Nwagba, as reported by Nairametrics (9 May 2025, "Only about 13% of Nigerians have records with credit bureaus – CREDICORP CEO"); CREDICORP's own account frames it as "Only 13% of Nigerians have credit bureau records, but that's about to change." [CF] Set that against EFInA's 2023 Access to Financial Services survey: **formal financial inclusion rose from 56% (2020) to 64% (2023), 74% including informal, with 26% — "28.8 million adults...still excluded from accessing finance"** (EFInA General Manager Oluwatomi Eromosele, reported by TheCable). [CF] The gap between ~64% formally included and ~13% credit-file coverage is the thin-file population — tens of millions of adults who transact but have no history a lender can price against. That gap is simultaneously the market opportunity and the reason it is hard: these are people the incumbents have *not* found it economic to cover.

**The competitive reality, stated honestly.** Three incumbents with fifteen-plus years of accumulated files, DEAs with essentially every regulated lender, a shared data template, a common trade body, and — in CRC's case — tens of millions of searches a year (29.4m in 2023). A fourth general-purpose bureau would be attempting exactly what Quod attempted in Brazil, and the Quod result (VIII.4) is not encouraging. The only defensible entry is *differentiated*: a bureau covering what the incumbents do not.

### VIII.3 The national credit push — tailwind or competitor

Two state-led initiatives bear directly on the founder's niche, and both are double-edged in exactly the way Part VII found the cooperative-digitisation programme to be.

**CREDICORP (the Nigerian Consumer Credit Corporation).** Established in April 2024 as a federal-government development finance institution, CREDICORP's mandate is to accelerate access to consumer credit to **50% of all working Nigerians by 2030.** [CF] It does *not* lend directly; it is a wholesale financier and risk-sharing platform, providing capital lines and credit guarantees to banks, MFIs, fintechs **and cooperatives**, plus a "cultural re-orientation" advocacy arm. Its three-pillar mandate is Infrastructure (credit-scoring systems), Capital (guarantees and wholesale funding) and Cultural Re-orientation. Its *first* pillar is explicitly about credit reporting: "Strengthening Nigeria's credit reporting systems, ensuring every economically active citizen has a dependable credit score."

In June 2025, CREDICORP's MD Uzoma Nwagba announced that the government would **link every citizen's credit score to their National Identification Number (NIN) and consolidate loan data into a "centralised national credit bureau,"** with mandatory reporting from all lenders and "real consequences" for defaulters (Nwagba floated tying defaults to passport, driver's-licence and tenancy processes). [CF] At the State House "Meet The Press" (17 June 2025) he put the scale of the financing gap directly: **"Nigeria needs about ₦183 trillion in credit to effectively develop the country... No government has that kind of money"** (reported by Daily Post and Nairametrics). [CF] CREDICORP's logic is to de-risk private lending to close that gap.

The critical ambiguity — a genuine gap in the public record — is **whether CREDICORP intends to build its own bureau infrastructure or to aggregate/partner with the three existing licensees.** [U] The 2025 language ("we are building a system that will assign credit scores to every Nigerian," a "centralised national credit bureau") reads as though CREDICORP could displace or sit above the private bureaux. No 2025–2026 statement resolves this, and CBAN has issued no located public response. For the founder, the unresolved question is not academic: if the Federal Government is standing up a NIN-anchored national scoring utility with mandatory furnishing, then the reciprocity cold-start the founder thinks his platform solves may be about to be solved for the *entire market* by fiat — which simultaneously destroys his differentiation and removes his reason to build.

**The RH-CRRP's proposed CoopCHECK Credit Bureau.** This is the sharper threat, because it occupies the founder's exact niche. Under the Renewed Hope Cooperative Reform and Revamp Programme (RH-CRRP), the Federal Ministry overseeing cooperative affairs announced (June 2026, in Kaduna, alongside a ₦200 billion capital-mobilisation campaign for a proposed Cooperative Bank of Nigeria) a **National Cooperative Digital Architecture Platform (NCDAP)** whose components include the National Cooperative Smart Registry (NCSR), a Cooperative Verification Number (CVN), a CoopID, and a **"CoopCHECK Credit Bureau powered by CreditRegistry."** [CF] Read carefully, this is the founder's entire fifth-entity thesis — a cooperative-sector bureau integrated with a cooperative identity number — being built by the state in partnership with an *existing licensed incumbent*. The founder proposed to be the new entrant solving the cooperative cold-start; the government has proposed to solve it with the oldest incumbent.

**Verdict: state-blessed competitor, not tailwind — with a narrow caveat.** Mirroring Part VII, these initiatives are far more competitor than tailwind for a *founder-owned* cooperative bureau. CoopCHECK is the same niche, occupied by a better-capitalised, already-licensed player with government backing and a national identity rail. CREDICORP's national-scoring ambition threatens to commoditise the very reciprocity advantage the founder counts on. The narrow caveat is that both are, as of this writing, **policy intention rather than operating fact** — Nigeria's record of announced-but-unbuilt digital-identity and credit infrastructure is long, and CoopCHECK may stall. But planning a five-to-seven-year build on the bet that a government programme *fails* is not a strategy; it is a hope. The defensible posture is to treat CoopCHECK as the thing most likely to foreclose the founder's fifth entity, and plan accordingly.

### VIII.4 The reciprocity cold start — does the platform actually solve it?

This is the most important analytical section, because the entire economic case rests on one sentence: *"owning the platform gives me furnisher relationships by construction."* The sentence is seductive and mostly wrong, in four distinct ways, each of which must be taken separately.

**(1) Does processing a cooperative's transactions give the *platform owner* the right to use that data in a separate bureau? No — not without a specific, purpose-built legal basis.** [CF/AI] This is the load-bearing legal point, developed fully in VIII.5. In outline: under the NDPA 2023 the tenant cooperative is the **data controller** and the platform is the **data processor** (Part VII). A processor may process only on the controller's documented instructions and *for the controller's purposes* — running the ledger. The bureau, by contrast, is a **separate controller for a new purpose** (credit reporting to third parties). The platform owner does not acquire a personal right to repurpose the data because his software touches it; the lawful basis for onward transfer must be established through the cooperative (as CIP) under the CRA's statutory permission, *plus* explicit contractual authority in the processor agreement. The intuitive answer — "I already have the data" — describes operational capability, not legal permission. The Experian study's central distinction applies with full force: **the founder will have the capability long before he has the permission, and acting on the capability without the permission is the failure mode.**

**(2) Even with full rights, is cooperative repayment data valuable to the lenders who would pay for it? Partially — and less than the founder assumes.** [AI] Cooperative thrift-and-credit loans are characteristically salary-deducted at source (check-off), guarantor-backed, small, and historically unreported. Each cuts against predictive value for an outside lender: *salary-deduction* means repayment reflects payroll discipline as much as the borrower, so a clean record overstates standalone creditworthiness; *guarantor-backing and social enforcement* mean default is suppressed by peer pressure that does not travel to an anonymous fintech loan, weakening the out-of-sample "good payer" signal; *small and short* facilities say little about how a borrower handles larger, market-priced obligations. But the data is not worthless. For a genuinely thin-file borrower with *no* bank history, even a salary-deducted cooperative record is more than the zero a lender otherwise has, and it establishes identity, address stability and a payment cadence. Honest assessment: cooperative data has real value **as a thin-file supplement** and modest value **as a standalone predictor of market-rate repayment.** That distinction dictates the whole business model (VIII.9).

**(3) Would the incumbents' lenders query a fourth bureau? Rarely, and only for coverage the incumbents lack.** [AI] A Nigerian bank already holds DEAs with at least two of the three incumbents (the CRA effectively requires two) and already pulls reports on any borrower with a file. It has no reason to add a fourth integration, subscription and underwriter retraining unless the fourth bureau returns a hit the others miss. For the thick-file banked population the fourth bureau returns nothing new; for the thin-file cooperative member it might return the *only* file that exists. So the fourth bureau is queried, if at all, precisely and only for the population the incumbents do not cover — confirming its position is **complementary, not competitive.**

**(4) The Quod natural experiment, applied directly.** In 2016 Brazil's five largest banks — Banco do Brasil, Bradesco, Caixa, Itaú and Santander, each holding 20% — founded Quod (Gestora de Inteligência de Crédito) with the explicit purpose of reducing dependence on Serasa, with antitrust blessing and using their own data. [CF] A decade later Serasa (Experian-owned since 2007, taken to 99.6% by 2012) still held roughly 60% market share, and the more consequential competitive event was Equifax buying the number-two bureau (Boa Vista), not Quod's rise. The companion study's lesson is stark: **even the furnishers themselves, acting together, with regulatory blessing and their own data, could not readily dislodge an incumbent bureau.** The reciprocity moat is that deep.

Now apply it. The founder is not five giant banks; he is one entrepreneur with one platform, a handful of tenant cooperatives, and ₦50m. If Quod could not dislodge Serasa, a founder-owned cooperative bureau cannot dislodge CRC, FirstCentral and CreditRegistry from the general market. The *only* thing that makes the Nigerian cooperative case different is that Quod competed for the *same* thick-file population Serasa already covered, whereas the founder would serve a thin-file population the incumbents *do not* cover. That difference is the whole ballgame: **the founder must never frame the bureau as a competitor to the incumbents, because as a competitor it loses by the Quod logic; it can only survive as a complement the incumbents (and CoopCHECK) might even want to buy data from.**

**Defensible verdict.** The platform does **not** genuinely solve the cold start; it **partly** solves one input (data supply) while doing nothing about the other three (legal right, standalone value, lender demand), and it *appears* to solve the whole problem only because the data-supply piece is the most visible. Concretely: the platform gives a plausible *data-collection channel* (subject to VIII.5's legal chain), but a bureau needs data supply **and** paying queries **and** legal permission **and** predictive value, and the platform supplies at most one and a half of the four. The cold-start is genuinely solved only if (a) the VIII.5 legal chain is built at launch, (b) enough tenant cooperatives are live to make the file worth querying, and (c) the file is positioned as a thin-file complement sold *to* lenders (and possibly *to* the incumbents), not as a rival general bureau.

### VIII.5 The data-rights chain — the section that must be got right

Trace the chain from the individual member to the bureau, one link at a time. Each link needs its own lawful basis, and a break at any link taints everything downstream.

**The chain:** Cooperative member → cooperative society (**controller**) → platform (**processor**) → bureau (**a separate controller for a new purpose**).

**Link 1 — Member → cooperative society.** The member joins under the society's bye-laws and takes loans under its terms. Under the NDPA 2023 the cooperative is the **data controller** of members' data. The NDPA (s.25) sets out six lawful bases mirroring the GDPR: consent; performance of a contract; legal obligation; vital interests; public interest/official authority; and legitimate interests. [CF] The cooperative's processing of its own members' loan data for its own lending is grounded in *performance of the membership/loan contract* and *legitimate interests*. No difficulty — *for the cooperative's own purposes.*

**Link 2 — Cooperative society → platform.** Part VII established the platform is a **processor** and will qualify as a data processor of major importance requiring NDPC registration. A processor may process only on the controller's **documented instructions** and only for the controller's purposes, under a written processing agreement. This link is fine **so long as the platform stays in its lane** — running the ledger. The moment the platform diverts the same data to a bureau it owns, it is no longer acting solely on the cooperative's instructions for the cooperative's purposes; it is processing for its *own* new purpose, and at that instant becomes a controller needing its own lawful basis. A processor that repurposes data it holds for a controller is, under both GDPR and NDPA logic, in breach unless specifically authorised.

**Link 3 — Platform/cooperative → bureau (the new-purpose link).** This is where the chain most often breaks. Two things must both be true.

*First, the operative lawful basis for the transfer.* Three candidates, and choosing right matters:
- **Consent** is fragile and, under the CRA, arguably unnecessary for furnishing (a CIP may disclose to a bureau without prior consent), and it would collide with the CRA rule that consent lapses when its specific purpose is satisfied — unworkable for ongoing furnishing.
- **Legitimate interests** exists under NDPA s.25, but NDPC guidance on its application is still developing, with little precedent — a thin reed. [CL]
- **The CRA's own statutory permission** is the correct, robust basis: **the cooperative acting as a CIP under the Credit Reporting Act, furnishing a *licensed* bureau — not consent, not bare legitimate interest.** [AI] This is why VIII.1's "are cooperatives CIPs?" question was pivotal: the entire basis hangs on the cooperative qualifying as a CIP and the receiving entity being a *licensed* bureau. If the founder's bureau is unlicensed, there is no statutory permission and the transfer is unlawful.

*Second, the purpose-limitation problem.* Data collected to run a ledger was collected for *that* purpose; credit reporting to third-party lenders is a **different purpose**. Under NDPA purpose- and storage-limitation principles, personal data may not be further processed incompatibly with its collection purpose absent a fresh lawful basis. The CRA supplies that basis *only if* the cooperative furnishes a *licensed* bureau as a CIP, and *only if* members received transparency (a privacy notice) that their data may be shared with credit bureaux. This is not automatic. **Data collected to run a ledger is not automatically available for credit reporting.**

**What the documents must say — at launch, not retrofitted.** For the chain to hold:
1. **The cooperative's bye-laws and member terms** must disclose that the society is (or may become) a CIP under the CRA, that members' credit data may be furnished to a licensed bureau, and that this may affect their access to credit elsewhere; members must receive an NDPA-compliant privacy notice.
2. **The platform's processor agreement** must (a) confine the platform to processing for the cooperative's purposes, and (b) *separately and explicitly* authorise a defined onward transfer of specified fields to the named/affiliated bureau, identifying it as an independent controller under CRA statutory permission. Silence is not permission; a generic "we may share data with affiliates" clause is insufficient and likely non-compliant.
3. **The bureau's licence** must be in hand before any furnishing, because the statutory permission runs to *licensed* bureaux only.
4. **Cross-border**: if the platform or bureau stores data outside Nigeria, NDPA ss.41–43 govern — an adequacy decision, or safeguards (SCCs, BCRs, codes, certification), or an s.43 derogation. The US is *not* on the NDPC adequacy whitelist, so a US-hosted stack (relevant given CreditRegistry's US parentage) needs SCCs. [CF]

**State it plainly: this chain must be established in the platform's and cooperative's terms at launch, not retrofitted.** Retrofitting is not merely inconvenient; it is often impossible, because you cannot lawfully repurpose data already collected under a narrower notice without going back to every member — and a cooperative with thousands of members cannot practically re-paper consent. The Experian study established that the estates must be firewalled and that operational capability is not legal permission. The mirror-image lesson here: because the founder holds the capability from day one, the *only* protection against acting on capability without permission is to have written the permission — bye-laws, member notices, processor-agreement transfer clauses, bureau licence — *before the first transaction is processed.* If he waits until the bureau is ready to build the legal chain, the data already accumulated will be legally unusable and his apparent head start will evaporate.

### VIII.6 The conflict problem

Experian sits *outside* its furnishers. This founder would sit *inside* them, at every layer simultaneously: the **software vendor** to cooperatives; the **owner of a competing cooperative** (the anchor tenant); the **operator of the apex institution**; and the **credit bureau that scores their members**. This is not one conflict but a lattice, and Part III's seven capture types illuminate why. Part VII established that a founder-owned platform is an **external-capture** vector by construction; a founder-owned bureau compounds it, converting the platform's data-processing position into an information-and-pricing position over the whole sector.

**Concrete conflicts:**
- **Data advantage over tenant cooperatives.** Through the platform the founder sees every tenant's full ledger. As bureau owner he could — absent a firewall — use one tenant's borrowing patterns to advantage his own anchor cooperative, or price bureau services discriminatorily.
- **Seeing a competitor cooperative's book.** The anchor cooperative competes for members with other tenants on the same platform, whose books the founder can see in operational detail. Even if he never acts on it, the *capability* is a governance defect no disclosure fully cures, because tenants cannot verify his forbearance.
- **Pricing power over dependent institutions.** Cooperatives built on the founder's core-banking software face high switching costs (Part VII's lock-in). A founder who also owns the bureau they must query can extract rents at two chokepoints — software licensing and bureau enquiries — over institutions that cannot easily leave.
- **Reputational fragility.** The arrangement depends on tenants not fully appreciating its scope. The day a tenant board realises that their software vendor also owns a competing cooperative, runs the apex that clears their funds, *and* owns the bureau that scores their members and sees their book — trust collapses. The arrangement is fragile precisely because its defensibility depends on it not being examined.

**Required firewalls, disclosures and governance.** To make even a subset defensible: legal-entity separation with genuinely independent boards (the 2008 Guidelines already require a five-to-eleven-director board with at least one independent director and a non-executive majority); a binding information firewall so the bureau receives only CRA-permissible, member-noticed data through the *same* CIP channel any third-party cooperative uses (no privileged back-door); enforceable, auditable non-discrimination undertakings on bureau and platform pricing; full plain-language disclosure to every tenant of the founder's cross-layer ownership at onboarding; and a prohibition on the anchor cooperative receiving any data or pricing advantage.

**Honest assessment: firewalls are necessary but not sufficient, and at least one layer should be given up.** [AI] Experian's "firewall the estates" finding assumed a single-role actor firewalling *data domains*. Here the actor holds *four conflicting roles*, and firewalls between data domains do nothing to cure conflicts between roles. The cleanest resolution — recommended here — is that the founder should **not own both the competing anchor cooperative and the bureau.** Owning the platform (vendor, outside the cooperatives) and a bureau is defensible with firewalls, because a vendor scoring the sector is structurally similar to what a bureau already is. Owning a *competing cooperative* and the bureau that scores its rivals' members is not defensible by any firewall, because the conflict is at the level of role, not data. The lattice cannot be held intact and defensible at once.

### VIII.7 Identity resolution in a Nigerian context

The Experian study established that identity matching is the core operation and the origin of the worst failures — the mixed file (matching too loosely) and the fragmented file (too tightly) — and that the threshold is a tunable policy choice whose commercial incentive tilts toward looser matching because lenders value a high hit rate. In the US this is severe because there is no universal identifier and matching is probabilistic. Nigeria's position is materially different, and mostly better.

**The national identifiers.** Nigeria has two overlapping 11-digit numbers:
- The **Bank Verification Number (BVN)**, a biometric identifier issued via NIBSS linking accounts across banks. Per NIBSS, **BVN enrolments rose 6.8% year-on-year to 67.8 million as at December 2025, up from 63.5 million in 2024** (the series runs 51.9m in 2021, 56.0m in 2022, 60.1m in 2023) — growth driven substantially by the CBN's directive to restrict/freeze accounts lacking both BVN and NIN, which "took effect from April 2024," and by the Non-Resident BVN scheme. [CF]
- The **National Identification Number (NIN)**, issued by NIMC, capturing broader civil-identity data — **over 121 million unique enrolments by mid-2025.** [CF]

**The NIN–BVN linkage.** The CBN mandated linkage of NIN and BVN to bank accounts (directives from December 2023, account-freezing enforcement from April 2024), welding the two identifiers together on financial records. [CF] This is the most important structural fact for a Nigerian bureau's matching problem: **where a person has both a BVN and a NIN, linked, the bureau has a near-unique, government-anchored key** that collapses the probabilistic matching the US bureaux wrestle with into a largely deterministic lookup. CREDICORP's plan to anchor every credit profile on the NIN (VIII.3) is, whatever else, a direct attack on the identity-resolution failure mode.

**So is Nigeria's identity infrastructure a genuine structural advantage over the US model? Yes — for the banked, matched population; much less so for the thin-file cooperative population the founder targets.** [AI] The uncomfortable asymmetry: the deterministic-key advantage is strongest exactly where the founder needs it least (banked people with linked BVN/NIN and existing files) and weakest exactly where he needs it most (thin-file, semi-banked or unbanked cooperative members who may have a NIN but a weak or absent BVN, inconsistent name transliterations across Nigerian languages, no fixed address, shared or informal phone numbers). For a cooperative-sector bureau, the matching problem is *not* solved by the national identifiers alone, because a meaningful share of cooperative members sit where those identifiers are incompletely captured. The CoopID/Cooperative Verification Number under the RH-CRRP is precisely an attempt to create a *cooperative-specific* key to bridge this gap — another reason the state programme occupies the founder's niche.

The practical implication: a cooperative-sector bureau must invest in **matching where BVN/NIN are absent or weak** — name normalisation across languages, address and phone heuristics, biometric or photo capture at the cooperative level, and conservative thresholds to avoid the mixed-file harm that falls hardest on people least able to dispute it. It must resist the commercial tilt toward loose matching, because in a thin-file population a false match is not a nuisance but a catastrophe: it can attach a stranger's default to the only file a person has.

### VIII.8 The dispute machinery — designing against the failure the Experian study documented

The Experian finding that matters most: **the process that determines whether the data is correct — disputes — is the only one that generates no revenue, is therefore run at minimum cost, and is what regulators eventually sue over** (the CFPB action over ACDV/e-OSCAR handling, with reported vendor economics as low as US$0.57 per dispute letter and a jury trial listed). A founder building from scratch has the rare chance to design the dispute operation as a first-class function.

**What Nigerian law requires.** The CRA and the 2008 Guidelines create a regime stricter on timing than the US:
- **Access**: a subject may inspect their information at any bureau; one free report a year; a free report on adverse action. [CF]
- **Dispute**: a subject may lodge a written complaint with the bureau or CIP. [CF]
- **Timeframe**: the bureau must investigate and communicate its decision **within 10 working days** (2008 Guidelines s.6.4); the disputed item must be flagged "under dispute" during investigation. [CF]
- **Provider liability**: inaccuracy must be promptly remedied by the *data provider*, who bears cost and legal liability; the bureau has no duty to independently verify unless information appears inaccurate on its face. [CF]
- **Escalation**: unresolved complaints go to the CBN (s.13); if unresolved within a further 10 days, the subject may go to court.
- **Penalty**: the ₦10,000,000 fine for negligent/intentional inaccurate reporting. [CF]

**Applying the Experian lesson.** The Nigerian 10-working-day clock is tighter than the US 30-day FCRA window, so an under-resourced dispute function fails *faster* here. And the CRA places primary correction liability on the CIP — i.e., on the cooperatives — so for a founder-owned bureau the founder's own tenant cooperatives carry the liability for bad data the founder's bureau publishes; a structural incentive to get furnishing quality right at source.

**What an adequately-resourced dispute operation costs at small scale.** [AI] Estimated, since no Nigerian bureau publishes dispute unit economics. At launch scale (a few hundred thousand cooperative files): a dispute is a human-plus-system workflow — intake, identity verification, routing to the furnishing cooperative, investigation, decision, and written response within 10 working days. Dispute volume is low in absolute numbers but high per file relative to a mature bureau, because thin-file data is dirtier. Budgeting a two-to-four-person dispute unit with a case-management/audit-trail system and a QA layer is on the order of a low-tens-of-millions of naira annually — trivial against the ₦500m capital floor, and *cheap insurance* against the ₦10m-per-incident fine and reputational collapse. The design principle from the Experian failure: **do not treat the dispute as a document-matching formality that discards the consumer's evidence.** The US failure was that consumer documents were not forwarded to furnishers by default and disputes were compressed into three-digit codes. A founder-built system should forward the member's evidence in full, log every step, and — because the furnishing cooperative is on the same platform — can resolve most disputes by direct reference to the source ledger, a genuine structural advantage of the platform-bureau design when used *for* the consumer rather than against.

**Design the obligation in from the start.** This is the one section where the founder can build something demonstrably better than the incumbent model: a dispute process that (a) meets the 10-day clock by default, (b) forwards evidence in full, (c) resolves against the source ledger the platform already holds, and (d) is resourced as a first-class function. Doing so is cheap at small scale and becomes a marketing and regulatory asset. Failing to do so reproduces exactly the failure the Experian study documented — under a tighter clock and a ₦10m-per-incident penalty.

### VIII.9 The business model

**Who pays, and what the bureau earns.** In the incumbent model lenders pay — via per-enquiry fees and subscriptions — and consumers get one free report a year plus paid self-enquiry and monitoring products. Discoverable Nigerian pricing is fragmentary: consumer self-enquiry runs around **₦400 per report** (CRC), with monitoring/performance products cited around **₦150**. [CF] The B2B/lender price list — per-enquiry pulls, API-call pricing, subscription and batch-score fees — is **not published** by any of the three bureaux; the only located figures are from an unverified third-party document and cannot be relied upon. [U] Value-added products across the incumbents include scores (SMARTScore, X-Score, CRC's FICO-based score), portfolio monitoring/review, skip tracing, identity verification and fraud (snapcheck), and business-information reports. Notably, both CreditRegistry and CRC offer *free* automated loan *reporting* (furnishing) to licensed digital lenders via integrators like Lendsqr — furnishing is subsidised to build the file; querying is where revenue sits. This confirms the reciprocity economics: the bureau pays (in free furnishing tools) to acquire data and charges for access.

**Realistic unit economics for a cooperative-sector bureau.** [AI] The founder's bureau would have an awkward structure: its **furnishers are cooperative societies** and its **buyers are — initially — those same cooperatives** (plus, hopefully, external lenders wanting thin-file coverage). Walking the economics:
- *Revenue per cooperative is small.* A cooperative queries mainly to assess a member's outside indebtedness — but it already knows its own member intimately (that is the point of a cooperative), so willingness to pay for a report on its own member is low. The cross-cooperative use case (over-borrowing across societies) is real but thin at low tenant counts.
- *External-lender revenue is the prize but is gated by coverage.* A fintech or MFI will pay for a thin-file report *only once the file is large enough to return hits* — which requires many furnishing cooperatives. This is the cold-start again: revenue lags coverage, coverage lags tenant count.
- *Fixed costs are high relative to a niche file.* The ₦500m capital floor, ongoing CBN supervisory/returns burden (monthly returns, audited accounts within three months of year-end, unrestricted CBN database access), the dispute unit, data-security and NDPC major-importance registration, and matching investment (VIII.7) are largely fixed and independent of file size.
- *Break-even requires either many tenants or high-value external queries* — neither of which exists at launch. [AI]

**Direct answer: a cooperative-sector credit bureau is not a viable standalone business at the founder's scale and capital; it is best understood as a *feature of the platform* that should not be a separate fifth entity until specific triggers are met.** [AI] The reasoning: (1) the ₦500m capital floor is ten times the founder's total ₦50m starting capital, and Part VII established that ₦50m starts the platform *or* buys one Super-Agent licence — the bureau is out of reach at the front of the sequence by an order of magnitude; (2) the revenue base (cooperatives querying their own members) is structurally thin, and the external-lender revenue that would justify the build is gated behind coverage the founder cannot reach quickly; (3) the state is building the same thing (CoopCHECK, CREDICORP national scoring) with more capital and a better identity rail; (4) the conflict lattice (VIII.6) makes the standalone bureau a governance liability.

What *is* viable is a **data-furnishing and thin-file-scoring feature within the platform** — the platform, as a CIP-enablement layer, helps each tenant cooperative furnish a *licensed incumbent or CoopCHECK* and surfaces the resulting scores back into the cooperative's lending workflow. That captures most of the value the founder wants (better member scoring, a data-driven position in the sector) without the ₦500m, the licence, the dispute liability, or the conflict lattice. The bureau's *economic* attractiveness (earning from the whole sector) is real, but capturable as a *partnership margin* long before it is capturable as an *owned licensed entity*.

### VIII.10 ECOWAS — the option, honestly assessed

Regional expansion is not a bigger version of the same business; it is a **different legal object.**

**Credit reporting is nationally licensed, everywhere in the region.** There is no ECOWAS credit-reporting passport. **Ghana** operates under the **Credit Reporting Act 2007 (Act 726)**, supervised by the Bank of Ghana, with its own licensing, capital and organisational requirements (ss.2–18) and — since the **Credit Reporting Regulations 2020 (L.I. 2394)** — an expanded set of mandatory participants (telcos, utilities, MMOs, fintechs, retailers). [CF] Ghana's licensed bureaux are XDS Data Ghana (first, operational 2010), Dun & Bradstreet Credit Bureau, and others. A Nigerian licence confers nothing in Ghana; the founder would license afresh under Act 726 and build a Ghanaian file from zero. The **UEMOA/BCEAO** francophone bloc uses a *regional* framework — the *Loi uniforme* regulating Bureaux d'Information sur le Crédit (BIC), adopted by the UMOA Council of Ministers in 2013 and transposed by each member state — under which the BCEAO appointed a **single regional bureau, Creditinfo VoLo** (a joint venture of Iceland's Creditinfo Group and VoLo Africa), operational since 1 February 2016, headquartered in Abidjan, covering all eight member states, with the BCEAO acting as the *interface* between furnishers and the BIC (the "indirect" model) and the centralised database sited in one member state. [CF] This is a *closed, single-licensee regional monopoly* by design — no room for a founder-owned entrant.

**Data-localisation and cross-border transfer restrictions may forbid the very pooling that would make a regional bureau valuable.** [AI] The value of "regional" comes from pooling a person's history across borders — but Nigeria's NDPA ss.41–43 restrict outbound transfer to adequate jurisdictions or with safeguards, and the UEMOA framework deliberately localises its database within the Union. The thing that would make regional expansion worth doing is the thing the law most restricts.

**There is no ECOWAS instrument harmonising credit reporting** — a sharp contrast with payments, where the **Pan-African Payment and Settlement System (PAPSS)** provides a cross-border rail. [CF] So a "regional bureau" is today either eight national licences and eight national files, or an attempt to displace a BCEAO-appointed monopoly (impossible in UEMOA). Neither resembles "a bigger version of the Nigerian business."

**What would have to be true.** [AI] (1) The Nigerian bureau would have to be a proven, profitable, licensed operation first — it is not, and on VIII.9's analysis should not even be a separate entity yet. (2) A specific ECOWAS or bilateral instrument harmonising credit reporting and permitting cross-border pooling would have to exist — it does not. (3) A target market would have to be *open* — anglophone Ghana is (via fresh licensing) but francophone UEMOA is closed. **State plainly: ECOWAS is an option to be revisited if and when the Nigerian entity is proven and a harmonising instrument emerges; it is not a near-term plan, and nothing in the founder's sequence should be built to accommodate it.**

### VIII.11 Sequencing and capital

**What the licence actually requires.** To operate a Nigerian bureau the founder must: incorporate a company limited by shares with "Credit Bureau" in the name; meet the **minimum capital of ₦500,000,000** (last published CBN figure — treat as a floor); pay the ₦250,000 application fee; assemble a board of five to eleven directors with an independent director, a non-executive majority, and a CEO with a degree plus ten years' relevant experience; submit a feasibility study and three-year financial projections; pass Approval-in-Principle (180 days to satisfy conditions) and on-site verification; and thereafter carry a continuing compliance load — monthly CBN returns, audited accounts within three months of year-end, unrestricted CBN database access, NDPC registration as a controller/processor of major importance, and a resourced dispute unit. [CF] The capability requirement is therefore not just capital: it is a credentialed management team, a supervised technology stack, and a compliance function — none of which the founder possesses at the front of the sequence.

**Where the bureau sits in the sequence.** Part VII established that ₦50m starts the platform *or* buys one Super-Agent licence, not both, and that the five-entity, three-year ambition is realistically a five-to-seven-year plan without external capital or a radical de-scope. The bureau's ₦500m capital floor is **ten times the founder's total starting capital** and dwarfs every other layer. It is, unambiguously, the **last** entity in any rational sequence — and, on VIII.9's analysis, possibly one that should never be built as a separate entity at all. Attempting it early would consume all capital, fail the coverage test (an empty file is worthless), and collide with CoopCHECK.

**Trigger conditions.** The bureau should be attempted only when *all* of the following are true:
1. **Platform scale** — enough live, paying tenant cooperatives that aggregated furnishing would produce a file large enough to return hits an external lender would pay for (a multi-tenant threshold, not a single-anchor one; Part VII: one captive customer validates nothing).
2. **Legal chain in place** — the VIII.5 data-rights chain built into platform and cooperative terms *from launch*, so accumulated data is lawfully repurposable.
3. **Capital** — ₦500m+ of dedicated bureau capital *in addition to* platform working capital, realistically implying external capital, which itself changes the founder-owned thesis.
4. **Demonstrated external demand** — one, preferably several, external lenders committed (ideally contractually) to querying a thin-file cooperative bureau, proving the complement thesis *before* the build.
5. **CoopCHECK clarity** — the status of the state's CoopCHECK/CREDICORP national-scoring build is known; if CoopCHECK is live and functional, the rational move is to *furnish and partner with it*, not compete.
6. **Conflict resolution** — the VIII.6 lattice resolved, most likely by not owning both the competing anchor cooperative and the bureau.

Until those triggers converge, the correct posture is VIII.9's conclusion: **build the furnishing-and-scoring capability as a platform feature that helps tenants furnish and query a licensed incumbent (or CoopCHECK), and defer or abandon the standalone fifth entity.** The bureau is the most economically attractive layer and the least legally and financially reachable; the discipline required is to capture its value as a partnership margin now and revisit ownership only when the triggers convert.

### VIII.12 Ten propositions on building a bureau from a platform

1. **Holding the data is not permission to use it.** The founder will have operational capability from the first processed transaction and legal permission only if he builds the data-rights chain first. The gap is not a commercial risk but a regulatory offence — the single most important finding in this Part.
2. **A bureau needs four things, and the platform supplies at most one and a half.** Data supply, legal right, standalone predictive value, and paying lender demand. The platform plausibly supplies a data-collection channel and nothing automatic about the other three. "The platform solves the cold start" is mostly an illusion produced by the visibility of the data-supply piece.
3. **The Quod verdict governs.** If Brazil's five largest banks, acting together with regulatory blessing and their own data, could not dislodge Serasa (~60% share a decade on), one founder with one platform and ₦50m cannot dislodge three fifteen-year incumbents. The bureau survives only as a **complement** serving the thin-file population the incumbents do not cover — never as a competitor.
4. **The niche is real but the state is already in it.** The gap between ~64% formal inclusion and ~13% credit-file coverage is the opportunity — but CoopCHECK (powered by CreditRegistry) and CREDICORP's NIN-anchored national scoring are the state building the founder's exact niche with more capital and a better identity rail. Plan around competition, not tailwind.
5. **The capital floor forecloses the front of the sequence.** ₦500m is ten times the founder's total starting capital; the bureau is the last entity in any rational sequence, and requiring dedicated capital on top of platform capital implies external investment that itself dilutes the founder-owned thesis.
6. **Purpose limitation is the trap.** Ledger data is not automatically available for credit reporting. The operative basis is the cooperative acting as a licensed-bureau CIP under the CRA — not consent, not bare legitimate interest — and it works only if the bureau is licensed and members were noticed at collection.
7. **The conflict is at the level of role, not data, and no firewall cures it.** Being vendor, competing-cooperative owner, apex operator and scorer at once is a lattice of conflicts. Firewalls protect data domains; they do nothing about conflicting roles. At minimum, do not own both the competing anchor cooperative and the bureau.
8. **Nigeria's identity rail helps least where the founder needs it most.** BVN (67.8m) and NIN (121m+), now being linked, make matching near-deterministic for the banked — but the thin-file cooperative population is precisely where BVN/NIN are weak or absent, so the bureau must invest in careful matching and resist the tilt to loose matching, because a false match is catastrophic for someone with only one file.
9. **The dispute machine is the one place to beat the incumbent model.** Disputes generate no revenue and are run at minimum cost until a regulator sues — the Experian failure. Nigeria's 10-working-day clock and ₦10m-per-incident fine make under-resourcing fail faster. A platform-built bureau can resolve most disputes against the source ledger it already holds, forward member evidence in full, and resource the function as first-class — cheaply, at small scale, and as a genuine differentiator.
10. **Capture the value as a feature and a margin, not a fifth company.** The bureau's prize — earning from the whole sector — is capturable as a platform furnishing-and-scoring feature and a partnership margin with a licensed incumbent or CoopCHECK, long before it is capturable as an owned licensed entity. Build the feature now; defer or abandon the standalone entity until the six triggers in VIII.11 converge. **The honest conclusion is that the bureau should be a product of the platform, not the fifth company — unless and until scale, capital, a proven external-demand signal, a resolved conflict structure, and clarity on the state's CoopCHECK build all arrive together.**

## Recommendations

**Stage 0 — Now, before any bureau work (build the option, spend nothing on a licence).**
- **Write the data-rights chain into launch documents.** Draft the cooperative bye-laws/member privacy notices and the platform processor agreement so that (a) each cooperative can act as a CIP under the CRA, (b) members are noticed that their data may be furnished to a *licensed* bureau, and (c) the processor agreement contains an explicit, defined onward-transfer clause to a named/affiliated licensed bureau. **Do this at launch — it cannot be retrofitted.** This preserves the option at near-zero cost and is the single highest-value action in the Part.
- **Build the bureau as a platform *feature*, not an entity.** Ship a furnishing-and-scoring module that lets each tenant furnish, and pull scores from, an *existing licensed* incumbent (or CoopCHECK when live). This captures the member-scoring value and the sector position without the ₦500m, the licence, the dispute liability, or the conflict lattice.
- **Resolve the conflict lattice on paper now.** Decide — and document — that the founder will not own both the competing anchor cooperative and any future bureau. This de-risks every downstream conversation with tenants, regulators and investors.

**Stage 1 — When the platform reaches multi-tenant scale (the coverage trigger).**
- **Prove external demand before building.** Secure written commitments from at least one, preferably several, external lenders (fintech/MFI) that they will query a thin-file cooperative file. If you cannot get these commitments, the standalone bureau is dead on arrival — stop here and keep the feature.
- **Establish CoopCHECK's status.** If CoopCHECK/CREDICORP national scoring is live and functional in the cooperative niche, pivot decisively to *furnishing and partnering*, not competing. Sell your aggregated cooperative data *into* the state utility or the incumbents as a differentiated feed.

**Stage 2 — Only if all six VIII.11 triggers converge (multi-tenant scale, legal chain in place, ₦500m+ dedicated capital, proven external demand, CoopCHECK clarity, conflict resolved).**
- **Apply for the licence with a dispute operation designed in.** Budget a resourced dispute unit (two-to-four staff, case management with audit trail, evidence-forwarding, source-ledger resolution) from day one — cheap insurance against the ₦10m-per-incident fine and the reputational failure the Experian study documents.
- **Raise external capital explicitly** for the bureau, accepting that this dilutes the founder-owned thesis; ₦500m+ cannot come from the ₦50m base.

**Benchmarks that would change the recommendation.**
- *Toward building sooner:* CoopCHECK stalls or fails to launch **and** two or more external lenders contractually commit to querying **and** external bureau capital is secured.
- *Toward abandoning entirely:* CoopCHECK launches and functions in the cooperative niche, **or** CREDICORP's national NIN-anchored bureau becomes operating fact with mandatory furnishing — either of which commoditises the founder's only advantage. In that world, be a furnisher and a partner, never a fourth licensee.

## Caveats

- **The ₦500m minimum capital is the last *published* figure (2008 Guidelines, revised 2013).** Whether the CBN has since raised it is **UNKNOWN** on the public record. Treat ₦500m as a floor given naira depreciation. [U]
- **Whether CREDICORP builds its own bureau or partners with the three incumbents is genuinely unresolved** in public sources as of this writing; the whole competitor-versus-tailwind assessment turns partly on this, and it should be re-checked before any Stage 2 decision. [U]
- **CoopCHECK and CREDICORP national scoring are policy intention, not operating fact.** Nigeria's record of announced-but-unbuilt digital infrastructure is long; these may stall. But planning on their failure is a hope, not a strategy. [CL]
- **No Nigerian bureau publishes audited accounts,** so all revenue, record-count and "largest bureau" claims are self-reported and mutually inconsistent (FirstCentral's own materials cite both "1 billion/3,000" and "2 billion/40 million"). The US$8.5m CRC revenue figure is a data-broker estimate, not a filing. Financial projections built on these should carry wide error bars. [U/H]
- **B2B lender pricing is not publicly disclosed** by any incumbent; the unit-economics modelling in VIII.9 rests on analytical inference and consumer-side prices, not a published lender rate card. [U]
- **The cooperative-as-CIP conclusion rests on practitioner commentary and the breadth of the 2008 Guidelines' data-source list, not on an express statutory naming of cooperatives.** It is well-supported [SC] but would benefit from a CBN confirmation before capital is committed.
- **Legitimate interest under the NDPA is a developing area** with little published NDPC guidance; the recommendation to rely on CRA statutory permission rather than legitimate interest is deliberate and should be confirmed with Nigerian data-protection counsel at the drafting stage. [CL]
