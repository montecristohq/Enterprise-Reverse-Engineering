# PART IV — SHARED INFRASTRUCTURE

**Framing.** Part II established that the institutional protection scheme relocates *capital* to the network level — a cooperative solution to the cooperative capital problem. Part IV's argument is the exact parallel: **shared infrastructure relocates *cost and capability* to the network level.** A single small cooperative cannot afford a core banking system, a payments connection, a fraud engine, a compliance function or a mobile app; a network of cooperatives, acting through a jointly-owned entity, can. Both moves are the same cooperative-of-cooperatives logic — solving the small institution's problem collectively rather than individually — and both carry the same structural risk: that the shared entity, being unfireable by its owners, loses discipline. This Part is deliberately short because the reader already possesses two book-length forensic studies of the German infrastructure (Atruvia AG and the DZ BANK Group); it cross-references those rather than repeating them, and spends its length instead on the models that transplant to Nigeria and on what a Nigerian cooperative fintech actually needs.

## IV.1 The build / buy / federate choice

The problem is scale economics. Core banking systems, payment-rail connections, card processing, fraud engines and regulatory-compliance functions all have very high fixed costs and low marginal costs; they are natural candidates for sharing. A small cooperative faces three options.

- **Build alone.** Unaffordable, and wasteful even where affordable, because the fixed cost is duplicated across every institution. (CONFIRMED FACT: no primary thrift-and-credit society in Nigeria builds its own core banking platform.)
- **Buy from a vendor.** Affordable and immediate, but it creates dependency on a supplier whose incentives are its own, transfers no ownership to the cooperative, and — critically — surrenders the data and the customer relationship to a commercial third party.
- **Federate.** Build collectively through a jointly-owned utility that the primary cooperatives own and (in principle) control. This is the distinctively cooperative answer: it preserves ownership and cost-recovery pricing while capturing the scale economy.

ANALYTICAL INFERENCE: federation is superior to buying *only* where the network is large enough, cohesive enough and well-governed enough to sustain a shared entity. Where those conditions fail — as they do in Nigeria — "federate" collapses back into "buy," and the cooperative becomes a vendor's customer after all. This is the through-line of the Part.

## IV.2 The German model — the mechanism, cross-referenced

The reader possesses detailed studies of **Atruvia AG** and the **DZ BANK Group**; what follows extracts only the mechanism.

Atruvia AG is the captive IT utility of the German cooperative banking sector. It was formed in 2015 by the merger of Fiducia IT AG (southern Germany) and GAD eG (northern Germany), and was renamed from Fiducia & GAD IT AG to Atruvia AG in 2021. Per IBM's Atruvia case study, more than 800 banks rely on its **agree21** core-banking process, with some **81 million customer accounts**, running on eight IBM z15 systems across four data centres that process **80 billion core-banking transactions annually** (peaks of 12,000 transactions per second); IBM describes Atruvia as "the digitization partner of Germany's 820 cooperative banks," employing almost **8,400 people** and reporting group sales of around **EUR 1.77 billion in 2020**. The mechanism to extract is fourfold: (1) the primary banks *own* the utility, so its surplus is theirs; (2) it runs at cost-recovery rather than for external profit; (3) a levy/assessment structure funds shared development, so a new digital feature is built once and deployed to all; and (4) the distinctive risk is precisely that a captive supplier the owners cannot practically fire may lose cost discipline and slow to innovate — the same influence-cost and control problems Cook identifies, transposed from the bank to the utility.

The **DZ BANK Group** is the central institution: it provides wholesale banking, payments, treasury and product manufacture (asset management, insurance, mortgage finance, leasing) to the primary banks, which own it — DZ BANK is majority-owned by around 700–800 local cooperative banks. The primary bank keeps the member relationship; DZ BANK manufactures the products the primary bank could not build alone.

The solvency analogue to shared infrastructure is the **BVR institutional protection scheme**, run by the Bundesverband der Deutschen Volksbanken und Raiffeisenbanken (BVR) — described in the network's own materials as the oldest bank-protection scheme in Germany — now operating alongside the statutory deposit-guarantee entity BVR Institutssicherung GmbH (BVR-ISG), created to satisfy the EU Deposit Guarantee Schemes Directive. This is the point Part II made from the capital side and Part III from the monitoring side: in Germany, capital, capability *and* solvency assurance are all relocated to the network. Per the BVR's consolidated statements the network numbered 735 cooperative banks in 2022 (down from 771 in 2021), which is itself the German answer to the horizon and scale problems — continuous consolidation of the primaries while the shared infrastructure endures.

## IV.3 The Dutch, Quebec and US models

**The Netherlands — federation's logical endpoint.** After its 2016 reorganisation, Rabobank became a single legal entity: the previously independent local Rabobanks merged into one cooperative bank with one banking licence and one balance sheet. Shared infrastructure thereby became simply *internal* infrastructure. SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS / ANALYTICAL INFERENCE: the Dutch case shows that full merger is the logical endpoint of federation — when the shared layer grows large enough relative to the primaries, the cheapest governance solution is to dissolve the boundary between them altogether. The cost is the loss of local legal autonomy; the benefit is the elimination of the influence and control costs of running a shared utility across hundreds of separate owners.

**Quebec — the intermediate form.** The **Fédération des caisses Desjardins du Québec** has, since the 2001 consolidation of eleven regional federations into one, acted as the single central body for the caisses. It provides the network's technology, payments, treasury and product manufacture; it acts as financial agent, treasurer and the group's representative within the Canadian banking system; and it inspects the caisses. Per the Fédération's own 2024 regulatory filing, as at 30 September 2024 there were 203 member Desjardins caisses in Québec plus Caisse Desjardins Ontario Credit Union; each caisse pays an annual assessment/contribution to the federation. Desjardins thus sits between the German model (many primaries, separate central entities) and the Dutch (one legal entity): the caisses remain distinct cooperatives, but almost every capability above the branch is manufactured centrally.

**The United States — the most transferable model, and the one to study.** The US credit-union system solves the small-institution problem through three distinct, layered mechanisms, none of which requires the primaries to merge.

- **CUSOs (Credit Union Service Organizations).** A CUSO is a separate business entity that credit unions own — often jointly — to provide services (data processing, lending back-office, mortgage origination, payments, wealth management, compliance) at scale. They are regulated by the National Credit Union Administration (NCUA) chiefly through rules on how much a federally-insured credit union may *invest in* and *lend to* CUSOs, and through a mandatory **CUSO Registry**. Per the NCUA's "CUSOs at a Glance" (effective 31 December 2024), there were **1,108 registered CUSOs** — 1,032 natural-person CUSOs and 76 corporate CUSOs — of which 758 were wholly-owned; federally-insured credit unions reported roughly **$844 million in loans** to CUSOs and about **$4.0 billion invested** in them. The economics: several small credit unions jointly own one CUSO, and each obtains a capability (say, mortgage lending or a shared data centre) that none could staff or fund alone.
- **Corporate credit unions** — the "credit unions' credit union" tier providing settlement, liquidity and payments to natural-person credit unions. This tier consolidated sharply after the 2008–2010 crisis, when several of the largest corporates failed and were resolved by the NCUA under its 2010 corporate-credit-union rule (12 CFR Part 704). The principal survivors today include **Alloya Corporate FCU** (the largest, serving over 1,300 credit unions — a company officer describes Alloya as providing financial services to nearly 30% of all US credit unions), **Catalyst Corporate FCU** (serving more than 1,400 member and client credit unions), **Vizo Financial**, **Corporate One FCU**, **Corporate America CU** and **Millennium Corporate CU**. The cooperative logic runs one level up: in 2024 three corporates — Alloya, Catalyst and Vizo — united as joint owners of the payments fintech **Aptys Solutions**, a consortium whose members collectively represent 3,884 credit unions. That is a cooperative-of-cooperatives-of-cooperatives.
- **Shared branching and shared ATM networks.** Through the national shared-branching network and the CO-OP/Velera shared ATM and card-processing networks, a small credit union offers its members a national footprint — the ability to transact at thousands of other credit unions' branches and tens of thousands of surcharge-free ATMs — without owning any of them. What a small credit union gets that it could not build alone: a nationwide physical presence, real-time settlement, card issuing and processing, and instant-payment access (several corporates and CUSOs were among the early adopters certified for the Federal Reserve's FedNow instant-payment service).

## IV.4 Kenya

Kenya's SACCO movement — the largest cooperative financial movement in Africa — shares infrastructure through several channels, and its recent history contains the single most important cautionary tale in this Part.

- **The Co-operative Bank of Kenya.** The bank was established by and remains majority-controlled by the cooperative movement: **CoopHoldings Cooperative Society Limited**, owned by cooperative societies, holds about **65%** of the bank, with the remaining ~35% held by individual and institutional investors via the Nairobi Securities Exchange. This gives SACCOs a wholesale bank they collectively control — clearing, liquidity, settlement, FOSA support and product manufacture. (As of 2026 the bank was pursuing a restructuring into a non-operating holding company, "Co-op Bank Group.")
- **KUSCCO as apex — and its collapse.** The Kenya Union of Savings & Credit Co-operatives (KUSCCO), founded 1973, was the umbrella body providing advocacy, a central finance facility and, critically, a deposit-taking Central Finance Fund into which SACCOs placed liquidity. A **PwC forensic audit** (a 208-page report submitted February 2025) revealed a fraud of about **KSh 13.3 billion** (roughly USD 120 million); per the audit summary KUSCCO was insolvent by about **KSh 12.5 billion**, with assets of only about KSh 5.2 billion against liabilities of about KSh 17.7 billion. As reported by Business Daily, the audit found KUSCCO had **collected KSh 24.8 billion in deposits from 247 SACCOs** into a facility that sat outside prudential supervision. As of 2026 the government was restructuring KUSCCO into a federation and stripping it of its banking operations, and SASRA indicated affected SACCOs would not recover all their money.
- **M-PESA and mobile-money integration.** SACCOs plug into Safaricom's M-PESA for member deposits, withdrawals and loan disbursement, obtaining a mobile-money rail none could build.

CONFIRMED FACT and the lesson: SACCOs get, collectively, a bank they control and a national mobile rail. But the KUSCCO failure shows exactly what Part III predicted — concentrating infrastructure (here, liquidity pooling) in an *unsupervised* apex converts a shared-capability benefit into a shared-catastrophe risk. KUSCCO's fund was deposit-taking but sat outside SASRA's prudential perimeter; the substitute monitor was absent, and Olson's logic did the rest. An apex is only as safe as its supervision.

## IV.5 Nigeria — what exists and what does not

Nigeria has apex cooperative bodies on paper. The **Co-operative Federation of Nigeria (CFN)** is the national apex promotional body (it is named in the NCSA itself). The **National Cooperative Financing Agency of Nigeria (CFAN)** — created in 1978 as the National Cooperative Credit Union of Nigeria Limited (NACCUN) and rebranded in 2015 — presents itself as the national financial apex for financial cooperatives, coordinating credit unions, credit-union leagues and the 36 State Cooperative Financing Agencies. There are state cooperative federations (for example, LASCOFED in Lagos).

CONTESTED / ANALYTICAL INFERENCE: the honest finding is that very little *functioning* shared financial-technology or payments infrastructure exists at the Nigerian cooperative apex level. There is no cooperative-owned core-banking utility on the Atruvia model, no cooperative central bank on the DZ BANK or Co-op Bank of Kenya model actually operating, no cooperative-owned switch or payments consortium, and no cooperative institutional protection scheme. The apex bodies are principally promotional, advocacy and training organisations; the federation literature (source-hierarchy tier 5, to be treated with caution as promotional) describes ambitions rather than operating platforms. The **RH-CRRP's** proposed **Cooperative Verification Number (CVN)**, **CoopID**, National Cooperative Smart Registry and proposed **Cooperative Bank of Nigeria** are, as of mid-2026, *policy intention* — prospective infrastructure, not operating fact (see V.8).

## IV.6 The fintech layer — what a cooperative fintech actually needs

This is the section that makes Part IV useful to the reader. A cooperative fintech in Nigeria requires a defined stack; for each layer, the question is whether it can be **shared** (across cooperatives, via an apex), **bought** (from a commercial vendor), or **accessed only through a licensed sponsor**.

| Layer | What it is | Realistic source in Nigeria today |
|---|---|---|
| Core banking / ledger | System of record for members, savings, loans | **Bought** from a vendor; no cooperative-owned utility exists |
| Payment rails | Interbank transfers, instant payments | **NIBSS/NIP** — but access is *through a licensed institution* |
| Card issuing/acquiring | Debit/prepaid cards, POS | **Bought/sponsored** via a bank or licensed processor (AfriGo/Verve/scheme) |
| KYC / identity | BVN and NIN verification | **NIBSS BVN** and NIMC NIN — via licensed/authorised integrators |
| AML / transaction monitoring | Screening, STR/CTR filing | **Bought** (vendor) plus in-house compliance; obligation is the cooperative's own |
| Mobile app | Member-facing channel | **Bought/built** — commoditised |
| Remittance | Inbound cross-border | **Only through a licensed IMTO** as its agent/payout partner (see V.5) |

Two structural facts govern this table. First, **NIBSS** (the Nigeria Inter-Bank Settlement System, established 1993, jointly owned by the CBN and the banks) operates the rails — NIBSS Instant Payments (NIP), launched July 2011, plus the BVN system (with reported enrolments of the order of 67–68 million) and, since a December 2023 CBN circular, the mandatory electronic retrieval of BVN/NIN data at account opening. NIP participation extends to banks, microfinance banks and mobile-money operators — i.e., to *licensed* institutions, not to bare cooperative societies. Second, the CBN's IMTO rules (V.5) bar unlicensed entities from moving money cross-border.

**The Part's central practical point.** For a Nigerian cooperative fintech, the "federation" that supplies infrastructure will in practice be a **commercial vendor or a sponsor bank, not a cooperative apex — because no cooperative apex capable of supplying it exists.** The German founder inherits Atruvia; the Kenyan founder inherits Co-op Bank and M-PESA; the American founder inherits CUSOs, corporates and shared branching. The Nigerian founder inherits an advocacy body and a promise. That dependency has a price, and the price is paid in the currency of Part III: control. A vendor sets pricing, owns the roadmap, and can be acquired by a competitor; a sponsor bank can reprice, restrict or terminate access, and sees the cooperative's transaction data. Every capability bought rather than federated is a lever of degeneration handed to an outsider — because the outsider, not the members, then holds the capability the members depend on. The mitigation is contractual (data-ownership clauses, exit rights, escrowed source code, multi-homing across vendors) rather than structural, and it is second-best.

## IV.7 Four propositions on shared infrastructure

1. **Shared infrastructure is the capability analogue of the institutional protection scheme:** both relocate the small institution's unsolvable problem to the network. (SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS / ANALYTICAL INFERENCE.)
2. **Federation dominates buying only above a threshold of network size, cohesion and supervision;** below it, "federate" degrades into "buy." (ANALYTICAL INFERENCE.)
3. **An apex that pools liquidity or deposits without prudential supervision is not infrastructure but a concentrated failure point** — the KUSCCO lesson, and the reason Nigeria's proposed Cooperative Bank must be born supervised. (CONFIRMED FACT + ANALYTICAL INFERENCE.)
4. **For a Nigerian cooperative fintech today, infrastructure means vendors and a sponsor, not a cooperative apex;** plan for it, price the control cost, and mitigate contractually. (ANALYTICAL INFERENCE.)

# PART V — THE NIGERIAN REGULATORY PATH

**Framing note (read first).** This Part rests on statute, CBN circulars and secondary legal commentary rather than on audited accounts, because Nigerian cooperative societies file nothing public and the state cooperative laws are inconsistently published. It is simultaneously the most useful Part of this document for the reader and the least evidentially secure. Every material claim below is dated; where a figure cannot be verified against a primary CBN source, it is marked UNKNOWN with a pointer to where the reader should look. **This is the Part most likely to go stale** — the IMTO regime in particular has changed materially in each of 2023, 2024, 2025 and 2026 — and it is deliberately structured so that it can be lifted out and refreshed without disturbing the rest of the study. All statements are current as of **10 August 2026** unless otherwise dated.

## V.1 The three-regime problem

The organising insight, stated up front: **registering a cooperative society, holding a CBN banking/payments licence, and holding an IMTO licence for international remittance are three different things, under three different legal regimes, with three different capital requirements and three different supervisors.**

| Regime | Instrument | Supervisor | Entity form | Capital order of magnitude |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Cooperative society | NCSA 1993 + state law | State Director/Registrar of Co-operatives | Registered cooperative society | Member share capital; no prudential minimum |
| 2. CBN licence | BOFIA 2020 + CBN guidelines | Central Bank of Nigeria | CAMA company | ₦50m (Super-Agent) to ₦5bn (National MFB / PSB) |
| 3. IMTO | CBN IMTO Guidelines 2024 | CBN (Trade & Exchange Dept.) | CAMA company, licensed in home jurisdiction | USD 1,000,000 |

The trap this creates: **a founder can get the cooperative structure right and still be unable to move money.** The cooperative regime and the CBN regime do not connect — CAMA 2020 does not govern cooperative societies, and a cooperative society is not a CAMA company, so a cooperative can never itself hold a CBN licence or an IMTO licence. What a cooperative *can* do is **own shares in** a CAMA company that holds the licence. That is not a drafting nicety; it is the whole architecture of Part II's forced two-tier structure, and it is why the three regimes must be understood as a sequence of doors, some of which close behind the founder at each step.

## V.2 Regime one — the cooperative society

**Registration.** A cooperative society is registered not with the Corporate Affairs Commission but with the **state Director/Registrar of Co-operatives** (in Lagos, within the Ministry of Commerce, Industry and Co-operatives; the governing law is the Lagos State Co-operative Societies Law, Cap. C15, Laws of Lagos State). The federal **Nigerian Co-operative Societies Act 1993 (Cap N98 LFN 2004)** operates alongside the state laws; cooperatives are a residual/state matter, so the state law is the operative registration statute and the NCSA supplies the federal template. CONFIRMED FACT: a cooperative society cannot be registered at the CAC.

**Minimum membership.** In Lagos and most states, a general/multipurpose society requires a minimum of **10** members, though commentary varies — some sources cite a minimum of **20** members for a savings-and-credit society and **50** for an agricultural society. CONTESTED IN THE LITERATURE (law-firm commentary diverges); the reader should confirm the current threshold with the specific state Registrar. The NCSA/state law also imposes age and capacity conditions and requires "Co-operative" in the name.

**Bye-laws.** Registration requires a set of **bye-laws** conforming to the Act (name and registered address; objects; qualifications and terms of membership; and — where the society lends to members — additional bye-laws on loan conditions and the consequences of default). No amendment of the bye-laws is valid until registered, and the Director may alter proposed bye-laws to bring them into conformity with the Act.

**Cost and time.** Registration fees are modest — commonly cited around **₦10,000** (plus stamp duty and incidental costs) — and the process typically takes on the order of **2–3 months** to a certificate of registration, subject to the Registrar's satisfaction. UNKNOWN (precise, current, state-by-state schedule): confirm with the state Ministry, as fees are set by the Minister/Commissioner and vary.

**What a registered society may lawfully do — and what it may not do with members' money.** A registered thrift-and-credit society may take **members' savings and deposits** (the NCSA contemplates member deposits at §19), lend to members, and take security: §16 gives the society a **charge and set-off** over a member's shares, interest and deposits for debts owed to it. Governance is one-member-one-vote with a chairman's casting vote (§25); no member may hold more than **20%** of the share capital (§27). The decisive limit for this study is external, not internal: **a cooperative may deal in its own members' money, but the moment it takes deposits from the general public it crosses into "banking business" and needs a CBN licence** (V.3). It may not issue third-party payment instruments, run current accounts for the public, or move money cross-border, without the relevant CBN licence.

**Tax position.** SCHOLARLY CONSENSUS / to confirm: registered cooperative societies have historically enjoyed favourable tax treatment, with the profits of a cooperative generally exempt from companies income tax where they arise from transactions with members, and members taxed on their own income. ANALYTICAL INFERENCE with a live caveat: Nigeria's tax framework was overhauled by the 2025 tax-reform legislation taking effect from 2026, and the founder should obtain current advice on how the new regime treats cooperative surpluses and members' returns — mark this UNKNOWN pending confirmation against the 2026 tax statutes.

**Audit and supervision — the weakness carried forward.** Under §36 the society may appoint its **own auditors** (self-chosen, not from a compulsory independent audit federation), and §46 gives the Director a **surcharge** power against officers for losses. Parts I and III established the consequence: there is no compulsory external auditing federation on the German Prüfungsverband model, the Registrar is under-resourced, and the substitute monitor is therefore effectively absent — which Part III identified as the single best predictor of degeneration. The society is lightly born and lightly watched.

## V.3 The deposit boundary — resolving what Part I flagged

This is the pivotal legal question of the whole document: **at what point does a cooperative society taking member deposits become an institution requiring a CBN licence?**

**The statute.** Under **BOFIA 2020 §2**, no person shall carry on **banking business** in Nigeria except with a licence issued by the CBN, and §2(2) makes unlicensed banking business a **criminal offence**. "Banking business" is defined in **§131** as the business of receiving deposits on current account, savings account or other similar accounts; paying or collecting cheques drawn by or paid in by customers; the provision of finance or of consultancy/advisory services on corporate and investment matters; or such other business as the CBN Governor may designate by Gazette order — and the definition expressly reaches digital, electronic and virtual operations.

**The deeming provision.** The operative expansion is **§2(5)**, which deems a person to be receiving deposits and thus conducting banking business **(a)** if the person **accepts deposits from the general public** as a feature of its business, or **if the person solicits for deposits**; and **(b)** where certificates or instruments are issued acknowledging fixed repayable amounts. Two triggers, therefore: *accepting* deposits from the general public, or *soliciting* deposits.

**Is the member/non-member distinction the operative line?** ANALYTICAL INFERENCE (reasoning shown). The phrase "from the general public" is the hinge. A genuine cooperative takes deposits from its **members**, who are its owners, joined by a common bond, and who have paid in share capital — they are not "the general public." On the better reading, a bona fide members-only thrift-and-credit society is *outside* §2 because it does not accept or solicit deposits from the general public; it deals with its own members. This is consistent with the fact that thousands of cooperative thrift-and-credit societies operate lawfully in Nigeria without CBN licences and have done so for decades. The member/non-member distinction is thus the operative line.

**How robust is the line, and where does it collapse?** The line is robust only if membership is *real*. It collapses in three ways, each of which a prudent founder must avoid:
1. **Open-to-all "membership."** If a society solicits "membership" from the general public as a mere formality in order to take their money — no genuine common bond, instant online sign-up, membership as a fig-leaf for deposit-taking — then in substance it is soliciting deposits from the general public, and §2(5) bites. Substance governs form.
2. **Soliciting deposits.** §2(5)(a) reaches *soliciting* independently of acceptance. Public advertising of returns on "deposits" or "investments" is itself a trigger, even before a single naira is taken.
3. **Fixed-return instruments.** §2(5)(b) reaches certificates/instruments promising fixed repayable amounts — the classic architecture of a deposit, and of a Ponzi.

**Is there CBN guidance or enforcement directed specifically at cooperatives taking deposits?** UNKNOWN / to the author's knowledge, NONE. I am not aware of a published CBN circular, guidance note, or enforcement action addressed specifically to *registered cooperative societies* taking member deposits, as of August 2026. This is a genuine gap: the boundary is defined by BOFIA's text and by analogy, not by a cooperative-specific instrument. The reader should monitor the CBN's circulars page and the RH-CRRP proposals (V.8), which for the first time contemplate CBN involvement in cooperative financial oversight.

**The practical enforcement channel — SEC and EFCC.** The line is policed in practice not by CBN circulars but by anti-fraud enforcement, and this shapes how a genuine cooperative fintech will be perceived. "Cooperative" and "investment club" branding is a favoured cover for Ponzi and illegal-deposit schemes. On **11 March 2025**, per an EFCC statement by its Head of Media and Publicity, Dele Oyewale, the Commission alerted Nigerians to **58 companies** "posturing as investing entities," none registered with the CBN or SEC; five had already been convicted and another five had pleaded guilty. The SEC flagged around ten schemes in 2025; **AbdulRasheed Dan-Abu, Head of the SEC's FinTech and Innovation Department, stated (per Vanguard, 24 October 2025) that Nigerians "have lost over N174 billion to more than 440 Ponzi schemes in recent years,"** warning that operators and investors face prosecution under the Investment and Securities Act 2025; the high-profile CBEX collapse occurred in April 2025. CONFIRMED FACT.

**A defensible position on where the line sits.** Synthesising the statute and the enforcement pattern:
- A **members-only** thrift-and-credit society, with a **genuine common bond**, **real member share capital**, **no public solicitation of deposits or returns**, and **no fixed-return instruments issued to non-members**, sits lawfully outside BOFIA §2 and needs no CBN licence. (ANALYTICAL INFERENCE, high confidence.)
- The **margin a prudent founder should leave** is wide, because enforcement is substance-based and reputationally brutal: (i) keep membership genuine and documented (common bond, admission process, share capital); (ii) never advertise deposits or returns to the public; (iii) never issue fixed-return certificates to non-members; (iv) do not let a mobile app's "sign up" become de facto public deposit-taking; and (v) register with **SCUML** and maintain AML controls (V.6) so the society is visibly a compliant cooperative, not a scheme. The instant a product is designed to reach *non-members'* money, assume BOFIA §2 applies and get a licence first.

## V.4 Regime two — the CBN licensing landscape

The following map is current as of **August 2026** and dated where a specific circular governs. Each licensed entity must be a **company incorporated under CAMA**; a cooperative participates as a **shareholder** in that company.

### Microfinance banks (MFBs)
Under the CBN's revised minimum-capital regime (announced 7 March 2019; deadlines extended by circular FPR/DIR/GEN/CIR/07/054 of 1 May 2020 to April 2021/April 2022), the thresholds now in force are:

| MFB category | Minimum capital | Permitted footprint |
|---|---|---|
| Unit MFB, Tier 2 (rural/unbanked) | **₦50 million** | Single office, rural |
| Unit MFB, Tier 1 (urban) | **₦200 million** | Single office, urban |
| State MFB | **₦1 billion** | One state |
| National MFB | **₦5 billion** | Nationwide |

MFBs may take deposits, lend, and offer savings and micro-payments; they may **not** engage in foreign exchange, large-corporate lending, or cross-border transfers on their own account. Supervisor: CBN (with NDIC deposit insurance). ANALYTICAL INFERENCE: as of August 2026 there is no confirmed CBN circular *further* raising MFB minimums after the 2019/2020 exercise; treat any onward increase as UNKNOWN pending the CBN's FPRD circulars. (Note: the CBN's **28 March 2024** recapitalisation raised *commercial/merchant/non-interest bank* minimums — ₦500bn/₦200bn/₦50bn for commercial banks by international/national/regional tier, deadline 31 March 2026 — but that exercise did not, on the evidence gathered, restate MFB figures.)

**Can a cooperative own an MFB? Yes — and there is precedent.** The CBN's MFB guidelines permit an MFB to be established by individuals, groups, community development associations, private corporate entities and foreign investors; the 2020 MFB regulation expressly provides a **"Government-Sponsored Co-operative Model"** in which a state government supports a cooperative society to set up a Unit MFB, with the members progressively buying out the government (an initial 75:25 government-to-cooperative ratio) to become full owners within five years. And the decisive real-world precedent: **NPF Microfinance Bank Plc**, listed on the NGX, is controlled by the **Nigeria Police Co-operative Society Limited**, which holds roughly **62.6%** of it. CONFIRMED FACT (via NGX filings; confirm the current exact percentage against the latest annual report). This resolves Part II's open question: a cooperative society *can* lawfully own — even control — a CBN-licensed MFB, provided the MFB itself is the CAMA company that holds the licence.

**Shareholding and ownership caps.** The MFB guidelines bar any individual, group or corporate entity from holding a **controlling interest in more than one MFB** without CBN approval. Law-firm commentary cites a **49% single-shareholder cap** attributable to the 2020 Revised MFB Guidelines; CONTESTED — this cap does not appear in the 2012 text and its exact wording and application to *corporate/cooperative* (as opposed to individual) shareholders should be confirmed against the final signed 2020 Guidelines. The NPF precedent (62.6% held by one cooperative) indicates that, at minimum, controlling cooperative ownership has been permitted in practice.

### Payment Service Bank (PSB)
Minimum capital **₦5 billion**; non-refundable application fee ₦500,000 and licensing fee ₦2,000,000 (per the CBN Guidelines for Licensing and Regulation of PSBs, 2018, revised 27 August 2020). A PSB may maintain savings accounts and accept deposits from individuals and small businesses (covered by deposit insurance), carry out payments and remittances including **inbound cross-border personal remittances**, issue debit/prepaid cards, and invest in FGN/CBN securities. Critically, **a PSB may not grant loans, advances or guarantees, and may not trade FX** (beyond its permitted deposit-related functions). Why it matters: the PSB can *pay out* inbound remittances and hold customer funds — attractive for a remittance-oriented fintech — but its no-lending prohibition guts the core cooperative credit function, and its ₦5bn capital wall is the same wall Part II showed retained earnings cannot climb.

### Payments licence categories (New Licence Categorisation, CBN Circular of 9 December 2020; capital confirmed by the CBN's consolidated 2021 circular)
| Category | Minimum capital | May hold customer funds? | Core permitted activity |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Switching and Processing** | **₦2 billion** | No (unless also MMO) | Switching, card processing, clearing/settlement agent, non-bank acquiring |
| **Mobile Money Operator (MMO)** | **₦2 billion** | **Yes** (only MMOs may) | E-money issuing, wallets, agent networks |
| **Payment Solution Services (PSS)** — umbrella | **₦250 million** | No | May hold PSSP + PTSP + Super-Agent together |
| **Payment Solution Service Provider (PSSP)** | **₦100 million** | No | Payment gateways/portals, solution development, merchant aggregation |
| **Payment Terminal Service Provider (PTSP)** | **₦100 million** | No | POS deployment/ownership, terminal services |
| **Super-Agent** | **₦50 million** | No | Agent recruitment/management, agency banking |

Non-refundable application fee ₦100,000 and licensing fee ₦1,000,000 apply. Only **MMOs** may hold customer funds; a PSSP may not hold customer funds, issue e-money, or create wallets. Switching and MMO activities may be combined only under a **Payments Service Holding Company (PSHC)** structure with delineated subsidiaries. Supervisor: CBN (Payments System Management Department).

### Non-interest / cooperative-adjacent category
Non-interest (Islamic) MFBs and non-interest banks exist as categories under the CBN framework; there is **no dedicated "cooperative bank" licence category** in the CBN regime as of August 2026 (the proposed Cooperative Bank of Nigeria under RH-CRRP would, if realised, presumably be licensed under BOFIA as a bank — see V.8). UNKNOWN: the licence category the proposed Cooperative Bank would take.

### Licensing process, fees and escrow
For payments licences: application to the CBN, **Approval-in-Principle (AIP)**, then **Final Licence**, with a "no-objection" required before any additional licence, and the **minimum capital deposited into a CBN escrow account** (the PSP Share Capital Deposit Account) in one lump sum in the applicant company's own name ("not an individual or related company"); escrowed funds are invested in treasury bills and refunded/released after the licensing decision. For MFBs, the minimum capital is likewise deposited into a CBN escrow account and released to the promoters after the licence is granted (existing institutions transforming into MFBs may be exempted from the deposit, subject to CBN verification). Timelines are measured in months and are not fixed by rule; ANALYTICAL INFERENCE: budget 6–18 months from application to operational licence for a payments category, longer for an MFB or PSB.

### Shareholding and fit-and-proper rules bearing on cooperative ownership
The licensed entity must be a **CAMA company**; the cooperative holds shares in it. Directors and significant shareholders undergo **fit-and-proper** vetting. For a PSHC there is a **5% change-of-control threshold** requiring prior CBN approval for transfers of shareholding of 5% or more. There is **no explicit single-shareholder percentage cap** for the PSSP/Super-Agent/Switching categories (only the PSHC 5%-transfer notification rule); UNKNOWN whether a cooperative shareholder faces any category-specific cap beyond fit-and-proper. Net effect: a cooperative can capitalise and own the licensed subsidiary, but the *subsidiary* is the regulated CAMA entity and the *cooperative's* control must be secured by instrument (Part III.8) rather than assumed.

## V.5 Regime three — international remittance and the IMTO regime

**The most important section for the reader's stated end-goal, and the one that changes fastest.** Everything here is dated; the regime has moved in 2023, 2024, 2025 and 2026.

**What an IMTO licence is.** An **International Money Transfer Operator (IMTO)** licence, issued by the CBN's Trade and Exchange Department, authorises a company to facilitate the transfer of funds from persons abroad to recipients in Nigeria. It is governed by the **Revised Guidelines on International Money Transfer Services in Nigeria**, released **31 January 2024**, which repealed the 2014 Guidelines.

**Minimum operating capital.** **USD 1,000,000** (or the naira equivalent) for both foreign and indigenous IMTOs. This is a dramatic *reduction* for indigenous applicants from the 2014 regime's ₦2 billion (and an increase from USD 50,000 / ₦50 million for foreign applicants). ANALYTICAL INFERENCE: pegging indigenous capital to the dollar creates naira-devaluation risk for a local applicant, and commentators (e.g., Pavestones Legal) have criticised this. As of August 2026 the USD 1m figure stands. CONFIRMED FACT (2024 Guidelines).

**Fees.** Non-refundable **application fee of ₦10,000,000**, and an **annual licence renewal fee of ₦10,000,000** (payable by 31 January), a steep increase from the ₦500,000 application fee under the old regime.

**Two-stage licensing.** AIP first (allowing pre-operational steps such as opening accounts, but not commercial operations), then a request for **Final Approval within three months** of the AIP.

**Home-jurisdiction licensing.** An IMTO applicant must be a licensed/regulated money-transfer entity **in its home jurisdiction**; management must meet bank-management eligibility criteria (BOFIA §47 fit-and-proper carries across).

**Prohibitions.** IMTOs may **not** buy foreign exchange from the domestic FX market for settlement; may **not** engage in **outbound** transfers (a change from 2014, which permitted outbound up to USD 2,000); and are limited to **inbound** person-to-person (family maintenance), business-to-person and tourist transfers.

**Naira payout rule.** All inbound transfers must be **paid to beneficiaries in naira** (through a bank account or cash), at the prevailing Nigerian Foreign Exchange Market rate on the day of receipt. Cash payments are capped at **USD 200**; amounts above USD 200 must go through a bank account. The ±2.5% allowable-rate cap was removed in September 2023 (the "Removal of Allowable Limit" circular), permitting market-rate payouts. A **24 March 2026** CBN circular (signed by the Director, Trade and Exchange Department, Dr. Musa Nakorji) reiterated that IMTOs must operate designated **naira settlement accounts** with Authorised Dealer Banks and route all remittance transactions through them; per commentary on the 2026 compliance circular, IMTOs must reference real-time Bloomberg BMatch rates for pricing, and foreign-currency settlements were prohibited with effect from May 2026.

**The banks-and-fintechs bar.** The 2024 Guidelines prohibit **all banks and financial-technology companies** from holding IMTO licences — a significant tightening from 2014 (which barred only deposit-money banks). **Banks may, however, act as agents of an IMTO.** CONFIRMED FACT. This is the single most consequential rule for the reader: a "fintech" is, by CBN's own posture, *not* an eligible IMTO applicant.

**Supporting diaspora architecture (dated).** The CBN's remittance-policy objective is explicit: raise formal monthly inflows to **USD 1 billion**. Per the CBN's 2024 Balance of Payments data (Acting Director of Corporate Communications, Hakama Sidi-Ali), **IMTO inflows surged by 43.5% to USD 4.73 billion in 2024, up from USD 3.30 billion in 2023** (personal remittances overall USD 20.93bn). CBN Governor Olayemi Cardoso has cited a rise in monthly remittance inflows from about **USD 250 million to about USD 600 million by September**; CONTESTED IN THE SOURCES — Businessday reports Cardoso dating this rise to 2024 ("$250 million earlier in 2024 to $600 million by September 2024"), while Arise and other outlets report the same figures for April–September 2025. The reader should not over-rely on the month/year; the direction (a roughly doubling of monthly inflows) is agreed. The **USD 1bn/month target is set for end-2026** — a stated objective, not an achieved result, and inflows reportedly dipped in Q1 2026 (HYPOTHESIS/target, not fact). Supporting measures: the **Non-Resident BVN (NRBVN)** platform, launched with NIBSS on **13 May 2025**, letting diaspora Nigerians obtain a BVN remotely; the **Non-Resident Nigerian Ordinary Account (NRNOA)** and **Investment Account (NRNIA)** introduced **10 January 2025**; and a **PAPSS** (Pan-African Payment & Settlement System) documentation-easing circular of **28 April 2025** permitting banks to source FX for PAPSS through the official market and reducing paperwork for transactions up to USD 2,000 (individuals) / USD 5,000 (companies).

**Can a cooperative or a cooperative-owned entity hold an IMTO licence?** Reasoning to a clear answer (ANALYTICAL INFERENCE, high confidence):
- The IMTO licensee must be a **company** (CAMA), **licensed as a money-transfer operator in its home jurisdiction**, with **USD 1m** capital. A cooperative society is not a company and cannot itself hold the licence.
- A **cooperative-owned CAMA company** could in principle apply — *except* that the company would need to be a **licensed money-transfer entity in a home jurisdiction**, which a newly-formed Nigerian company is not; and if the entity is characterised as a **"fintech,"** it is **barred outright** by the 2024 Guidelines.
- **Conclusion: NO** — a Nigerian cooperative fintech cannot realistically obtain its own IMTO licence in the near term. The home-jurisdiction-licensing requirement and the fintech bar together close the door.

**What, then, can a cooperative lawfully do in the remittance chain, and what does it earn?**
1. **Act as a payout agent / sub-agent of a licensed IMTO.** The Guidelines allow IMTOs to appoint agents (entities using their own premises, staff and/or technology to deliver money-transfer services on the IMTO's behalf). A cooperative's licensed subsidiary can be the **last-mile payout point**, paying beneficiaries in naira. Earnings: a share of the transfer fee / a payout commission per transaction, plus the deposit/relationship value of the inflow landing in the member's account. This is the realistic entry point.
2. **Partner with a licensed bank that is itself an IMTO agent.** The cooperative's licensed entity plugs into a bank's remittance flow.
3. **Use a licensed switching/PSSP arrangement** to handle the domestic leg (the naira payout and settlement), while a licensed IMTO handles the cross-border leg.

**The bottom line for the reader's end-goal:** remittance is reachable, but **not by the cooperative holding an IMTO licence**. It is reachable by the cooperative (through a CAMA subsidiary) becoming the trusted, compliant, naira-paying **last mile** for one or more licensed IMTOs — capturing payout commission and the deposit relationship — and, if scale justifies the ₦5bn, eventually by a **PSB** subsidiary that may itself pay out inbound remittances directly.

## V.6 The AML, sanctions and data regimes

These apply regardless of licence status and impose a real fixed cost on even a small cooperative fintech.

- **Money Laundering (Prevention and Prohibition) Act 2022 (MLPPA).** Repealed the 2011 Act; provides the CDD, record-keeping, suspicious-transaction-report (STR) and currency-transaction-report (CTR) regime. Under §6, designated non-financial businesses must declare their activities and, before any transaction exceeding **USD 1,000**, identify the customer via standard data form and photo ID, keep a chronological register, and preserve records for at least five years.
- **SCUML registration.** The Special Control Unit against Money Laundering (SCUML), a department of the EFCC (statutorily established under MLPPA 2022 §17), registers, monitors and supervises **Designated Non-Financial Businesses and Professions (DNFBPs)**. ANALYTICAL INFERENCE / to confirm: cooperative societies that conduct financial (savings/credit) activity fall within the DNFBP perimeter and should register with SCUML; registration is required before commencing operations (or within three months for existing businesses). This is also, per V.3, a reputational shield — a SCUML-registered cooperative is visibly not a Ponzi. Confirm the society's specific designation with SCUML.
- **Nigerian Financial Intelligence Unit (NFIU).** Receives and analyses STRs/CTRs; the reporting destination for the cooperative's AML function.
- **Terrorism (Prevention and Prohibition) Act 2022 (TPPA).** Repealed the 2011 Act; imposes counter-terrorism-financing obligations and sanctions-screening duties on reporting entities.
- **Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 (NDPA) and the NDPC.** Establishes the National Data Protection Commission and imposes data-processing and breach-notification duties; data controllers processing above threshold volumes (e.g., 1,000 data subjects in six months / 2,000 in twelve) must engage a licensed Data Protection Compliance Organisation and file an annual **Compliance Audit Return (CAR)** (deadline 15 March). A member-facing fintech processes exactly this kind of personal data.

**Practical burden.** ANALYTICAL INFERENCE: for a small cooperative fintech, the AML/data stack is not optional and not cheap — it requires a designated compliance officer, KYC tooling (BVN/NIN verification), transaction monitoring, SCUML and NDPC registrations, and annual filings. Budget for it from day one; it is a fixed cost that the federation-vs-vendor logic of Part IV applies to (buy the monitoring tool; you cannot outsource the legal obligation).

## V.7 The sequencing problem — the Part's practical core

Given three regimes, the founder must sequence. Four paths, each with capital, timeline, what it permits, what it forecloses, and the Part III governance consequence.

### Path A — stay a cooperative society
- **Capital:** member share capital only; no prudential minimum. **Timeline:** ~2–3 months to registration. **Permits:** member savings, member credit, internal thrift, member-only digital app. **Forecloses:** public deposits, third-party payments, cards, remittance. **Ceiling:** the society can digitise its own members' savings and loans and nothing more; it cannot touch non-members' money or move money cross-border. **Governance:** maximal member control (one-member-one-vote, §27 20% cap) — but also maximal degeneration risk from the absent monitor (§36 self-audit).

### Path B — cooperative + a licensed payments entity (the forced two-tier of Part II)
- **Cheapest lawful entry point:** a **Super-Agent** licence at **₦50 million**, or a **PSSP** at **₦100 million** if payment-gateway/aggregation functionality is needed. **Timeline:** ~6–18 months to a final licence (plus escrow lock-up). **Permits (Super-Agent):** agency banking, agent networks, cash-in/cash-out on behalf of principals — and, as an **IMTO agent**, last-mile naira payout of remittances. **Forecloses:** holding customer funds (only an MMO may), lending as a bank, issuing e-money (PSSP cannot). **Governance:** the cooperative owns the CAMA company; control must be secured by instrument (majority stake, golden share, reserved matters — Part III.8) *before* outside investors capitalise it.
- ANALYTICAL INFERENCE: **Super-Agent (₦50m) is the cheapest lawful door into the payments/remittance chain** and is the recommended first licensed step. An MMO (₦2bn) is the cheapest lawful way to *hold customer funds*, but that is a large jump.

### Path C — cooperative + a microfinance bank
- **Capital:** ₦50m (Unit Tier 2) / ₦200m (Unit Tier 1) / ₦1bn (State) / ₦5bn (National), deposited into CBN escrow. **Timeline:** 12–24 months. **Permits:** deposit-taking from the public, lending, savings, micro-payments, NDIC insurance — the fullest banking function short of a commercial bank. **Forecloses:** FX and cross-border on own account (needs an IMTO agent/partner for remittance); one controlling MFB per owner. **Cooperative ownership:** **yes** — confirmed by the CBN's Government-Sponsored Co-operative Model and the NPF Microfinance Bank precedent (~62.6% cooperative-controlled). **Governance:** a cooperative can control the MFB, subject to fit-and-proper vetting and the (contested) 49% single-shareholder question — secure control by instrument and confirm the cap.

### Path D — partnership (defer the licence)
- **Capital:** minimal. **Timeline:** immediate to a few months (contracting). **Permits:** operate as **agent/sub-agent/partner** of an already-licensed institution — a bank, an MFB, an IMTO, an MMO — delivering members and last-mile distribution while the licensed partner carries the licence, the compliance perimeter and the balance-sheet risk. **Forecloses:** ownership of the rail, control of pricing and roadmap, and the deposit/data relationship (the Part IV control cost, in full). **Governance:** cheapest and fastest, but maximal dependency — the partner can reprice or terminate.

### Recommended sequence (and its defence)
**Register the cooperative society first (Path A) → operate initially in partnership (Path D) → stand up a Super-Agent subsidiary (Path B) → become the naira-payout agent of a licensed IMTO → and only then, if scale justifies the capital, add an MFB (Path C) or PSB for direct remittance payout.**

Defence: this sequence spends capital in the order that the law and the reader's budget permit, and — crucially — it **keeps the doors that close behind the founder open as long as possible**. Registering the cooperative first locks in member ownership and the §27/§25 governance before any outside investor appears (Part III.8). Partnering first (Path D) generates revenue and operational learning at near-zero licensing cost and defers the escrow lock-up. The Super-Agent step (₦50m) is the cheapest lawful entry into the regulated payments chain and, as an IMTO agent, directly serves the remittance end-goal. The MFB/PSB step is deferred because ₦200m–₦5bn cannot be bridged by retained earnings (Part II's g\* = b × ROE result) and must await either an institutional-protection-scheme-style pooled raise or outside investors whose control is pre-empted by instrument. **Benchmarks that would change the sequence:** if the reader can raise ₦5bn of investor capital up front with control secured, skip to a PSB (direct remittance payout, holds customer funds, no lending — good for a pure-remittance play) or a National MFB (adds lending); if remittance volume through a partner exceeds the economics of agency, accelerate the Super-Agent → IMTO-agent step; if the RH-CRRP Cooperative Bank becomes real and CBN-supervised (V.8), re-evaluate whether membership in it substitutes for building a licensed subsidiary at all.

## V.8 What the RH-CRRP reform may change

Status as of **August 2026**: the **Renewed Hope Cooperative Reform and Revamp Programme (RH-CRRP) 2025–2030** is a Federal Government framework, approved by the National Council on Cooperative Affairs (NCCA) and driven by the Federal Ministry of Agriculture and Food Security (Minister of State / Supervising Minister of Cooperative Affairs, Sen. Dr. Aliyu Sabi Abdullahi). **All of the following is policy intention with the dates shown, not operating fact.** (Source-hierarchy tier 5 — almost all available material is government/federation promotional communication and must be tested, not taken at face value.)

- **National Steering Committee (NSC):** inaugurated as the apex coordinating/policy body for the programme (chaired at ministerial level). *Established; operational status of downstream deliverables pending.*
- **Review of the NCSA:** regulatory reform of the cooperative statute is named as a pillar. *No amended Act enacted as of August 2026 — HYPOTHESIS/intention.*
- **Cooperative Bank of Nigeria:** proposed member-owned bank. A **₦200 billion share-capital mobilisation campaign** was launched (announced at the North-West zonal engagement, Kaduna, **18 June 2026**), with a proposed equity split of **65% cooperative societies** (through the Cooperative Trust & Investment Society of Nigeria, "CoopTrust"), **30% institutional investors/DFIs/individual cooperators**, and **5% Employee Share Ownership Scheme**. *Announced and in mobilisation; not yet licensed or operating — the ₦200bn is a campaign target, not raised capital.*
- **Digital architecture (NCDAP):** the National Cooperative Smart Registry (NCSR), **Cooperative Verification Number (CVN)** for each society, **CoopID** for each member (both to be integrated with the NIN architecture), and a **CoopCHECK Credit Bureau** (stated to be powered by CreditRegistry). Flagged off (Lagos/LASCOFED, **11 May 2026**; approved at the 8th NCCA meeting, March 2026). *Announced and in early rollout; coverage and functionality UNKNOWN.*
- **CBN involvement in cooperative financial oversight:** contemplated in the reform's framing (a supervised Cooperative Bank; harmonisation with national financial systems). *No CBN instrument bringing cooperatives under prudential supervision has been issued as of August 2026 — HYPOTHESIS.*

**What each element would change for the reader if implemented:**
- A **CBN-supervised Cooperative Bank** would, for the first time, give Nigerian cooperatives a wholesale central institution on the Co-op Bank of Kenya / DZ BANK model — potentially the shared rail, liquidity pool and product manufacturer that Part IV found absent. It could shorten the reader's path (membership in it might substitute for building a licensed subsidiary) — **but only if born supervised**, or it risks the KUSCCO outcome (IV.4).
- **CVN/CoopID** would supply the identity and verification layer cooperatives now lack, easing KYC and, via CoopCHECK, credit-risk assessment — reducing the reader's build/buy cost for identity and credit data.
- **A revised NCSA** could, in principle, introduce the **asset lock** on reserves that Part I found missing, a compulsory audit federation to fix the absent monitor (Part III), and a coherent federal supervisory regime — the three fixes this study identifies. Whether it will is UNKNOWN.

The reader should track (i) whether the Cooperative Bank obtains a CBN licence and under what category, (ii) whether an amended NCSA is actually enacted, and (iii) whether CVN/CoopID achieve real coverage — and treat none of it as usable infrastructure until it does.

## V.9 Ten dated propositions on the Nigerian path

1. **(As of Aug 2026)** A genuine members-only thrift-and-credit cooperative needs no CBN licence; a cooperative that solicits or takes the *general public's* deposits commits an offence under BOFIA 2020 §2(2). *Stable — statutory.*
2. **(As of Aug 2026)** A cooperative society can never itself hold a CBN or IMTO licence; it must own a CAMA company that does. *Stable — structural.*
3. **(As of Aug 2026)** A cooperative *can* lawfully own — even control — a CBN-licensed MFB; NPF Microfinance Bank (~62.6% cooperative-controlled) is the precedent. *Stable, but confirm the current percentage.*
4. **(As of Aug 2026)** The cheapest lawful entry into the regulated payments/remittance chain is a **Super-Agent** licence at **₦50 million**. *Moderately stable — capital figures can be revised by CBN circular.*
5. **(As of Jan 2024, standing Aug 2026)** IMTO minimum capital is **USD 1,000,000** for both foreign and indigenous applicants; application and annual renewal fees are **₦10,000,000** each. *Volatile — the IMTO regime changes yearly.*
6. **(As of Jan 2024, reinforced 2026)** **Banks and fintechs are barred from holding IMTO licences**, but banks may act as IMTO agents; inbound transfers must be **paid in naira**; IMTOs may not buy domestic FX or do outbound. *Volatile.*
7. **(Conclusion, high confidence, Aug 2026)** A Nigerian cooperative fintech **cannot realistically obtain its own IMTO licence**; its lawful route to remittance is as a **naira-payout agent/sub-agent** of a licensed IMTO, or via a PSB subsidiary (₦5bn). *Stable while the fintech bar and home-jurisdiction rule stand.*
8. **(As of Aug 2026)** No cooperative-owned core-banking, switch, or central institution actually operates in Nigeria; shared infrastructure means **vendors and a sponsor bank**, not a cooperative apex. *Could change if the RH-CRRP Cooperative Bank is realised.*
9. **(As of Aug 2026)** AML (MLPPA 2022 / SCUML), CTF (TPPA 2022) and data (NDPA 2023 / NDPC) obligations bind even a small cooperative fintech and are a non-negotiable fixed cost. *Stable — statutory.*
10. **(As of Aug 2026)** The RH-CRRP — Cooperative Bank, ₦200bn mobilisation, CVN/CoopID, NCSA review — is **policy intention, not operating fact**; treat it as such until instruments and licences exist. *Most likely of all propositions to change; re-check quarterly.*
