# Enterprise Reverse-Engineering Project

## Standalone Research Prompt

### Research Target

- **Company:** [COMPANY NAME]
- **Industry / industries:** [INDUSTRY OR LEAVE FOR AGENT TO DETERMINE]
- **Primary geographies:** [GEOGRAPHIES OR LEAVE FOR AGENT TO DETERMINE]
- **Research date:** [CURRENT DATE]
- **Special focus, if any:** [OPTIONAL]

You are conducting an **institutional-grade, forensic reverse-engineering study** of the company named above.

This is not a conventional company profile.

This is not a short equity-research note.

This is not a SWOT analysis.

This is not a pitch-deck summary.

This is not a generic strategy exercise.

Your objective is to **take the enterprise apart component by component until its underlying machinery is understood**, and then reconstruct the relevant system for this volume.

Treat the company as though it were a complex machine placed in front of a multidisciplinary forensic engineering team.

Depending on the company, that machine may include combinations of:

- legal entities,
- products,
- services,
- factories,
- logistics networks,
- software,
- physical infrastructure,
- intellectual property,
- regulated permissions,
- brands,
- distribution,
- data,
- suppliers,
- financial assets,
- employees,
- operating processes,
- customer relationships,
- capital,
- managerial systems.

The standard is not:

> “I understand what this company does.”

The standard is:

> **“I understand how the relevant parts of this enterprise work, why they are structured that way, how they interact, where money/materials/data/authority/risk move, what they depend on, how value is created and captured, and what can break.”**

# Non-Negotiable Depth Rule

**Depth must follow the subject.**

Do not allocate equal space to sections.

Do not compress important material merely to keep the report short.

Do not aim for a predetermined page count, word count, or token count.

Do not shorten later sections because earlier sections became long.

Do not stop because the answer “feels long.”

A section ends only when its analytical questions have been adequately answered.

If a topic requires 2 pages, write 2 pages.

If it requires 10 pages, write 10 pages.

If it requires 30 pages, write 30 pages.

If an important subsystem cannot be understood without explaining prerequisite concepts, explain those prerequisites.

If a revenue stream requires 12 pages, give it 12 pages.

If an obscure legal subsidiary requires only one paragraph, give it one paragraph.

**Completeness before concision.**

There is no reward for brevity and a substantial penalty for superficiality.

Do not confuse repetition with depth. Every additional paragraph must add evidence, mechanism, decomposition, comparison, causality, or synthesis.

Before ending any major section, ask:

> **Am I stopping because the subject is adequately understood, or because I am trying to keep the report short?**

If the answer is the latter, continue.

# Industry-Adaptation Rule

This framework is deliberately industry-agnostic.

Do not force concepts onto the company when they do not apply.

Instead, identify the company's actual operating reality and adapt the analysis accordingly.

Examples:

- For a **manufacturer**, emphasize materials, plants, production systems, suppliers, logistics, utilization, yield, quality, maintenance, working capital, and distribution.
- For a **bank**, emphasize deposits, funding, lending, balance-sheet risk, regulatory capital, liquidity, credit, payments, treasury, and asset-liability management.
- For a **software company**, emphasize product architecture, subscriptions, infrastructure, data, development processes, retention, sales efficiency, and cloud economics.
- For a **marketplace**, emphasize both sides of the network, liquidity, matching, take rate, trust, fraud, and network effects.
- For a **pharmaceutical company**, emphasize R&D, clinical trials, patents, regulatory approvals, manufacturing, commercialization, and pipeline economics.
- For an **airline**, emphasize fleet, routes, utilization, slots, fuel, maintenance, labor, loyalty, revenue management, and regulation.
- For a **retailer**, emphasize merchandising, stores, inventory, procurement, real estate, logistics, pricing, and customer frequency.
- For a **defense contractor**, emphasize programs, procurement cycles, security regulation, production capacity, backlog, government customers, supply chains, and classified dependencies.
- For a **payments/fintech company**, emphasize licenses, money movement, settlement, safeguarding, payment rails, treasury, ledgers, fraud, and compliance.

Use sector-specific concepts where they improve accuracy.

Do not mechanically discuss payment rails for a restaurant chain or clinical trials for an industrial manufacturer.

# Analytical Model

For every material component, answer:

## What?
What entities, products, services, processes, assets, systems, licenses, facilities, contracts, teams, datasets, channels, controls, or revenue streams exist?

## How?
How does it actually function? Explain mechanisms rather than merely outcomes.

## Who?
Who owns, operates, regulates, controls, approves, depends on, profits from, or bears risk for it?

## Where?
Where does it exist physically, legally, technologically, economically, organizationally, and jurisdictionally?

## When?
What timing matters: production cycles, sales cycles, settlement, procurement, inventory turns, project milestones, accounting recognition, reporting cycles, product development, customer lifecycle, or strategic chronology?

## Why?
Why is it designed this way? What historical, economic, technical, legal, regulatory, or strategic logic explains the structure?

Then apply five cross-sectional lenses:

### Structure
What are the constituent pieces?

### Flow
What moves through them?

Possible flows include:

- money,
- materials,
- inventory,
- products,
- information,
- customers,
- authority,
- accounting entries,
- data,
- intellectual property,
- risk,
- regulatory responsibility.

### Control
Who has decision rights? Who can authorize, block, override, or terminate an action?

### Economics
Who pays? Who receives? What does it cost? Where is value created and captured?

### Risk
What can fail? What happens if it fails? Who bears the loss or obligation?

# Evidence Classification

Explicitly distinguish:

- **CONFIRMED FACT** — directly supported by reliable evidence.
- **COMPANY CLAIM** — stated by the company but not necessarily independently verified.
- **THIRD-PARTY ESTIMATE** — externally reported or estimated.
- **ANALYTICAL INFERENCE** — reasonably inferred from multiple known facts.
- **HYPOTHESIS** — possible explanation requiring further evidence.
- **UNKNOWN** — available evidence is insufficient.

Never silently convert an inference into a fact.

When reputable sources disagree:

1. identify the disagreement,
2. compare source dates,
3. determine whether circumstances changed,
4. identify which source appears more authoritative,
5. preserve uncertainty where necessary.

Do not manufacture false precision.

# Historical Discipline

Companies evolve.

Always distinguish:

- historical architecture,
- transitional architecture,
- current architecture.

Do not present an old product, facility, license, supplier, technology, organizational practice, or business model as current without verification.

Use **“As of [date]”** for material current-state claims when appropriate.

- “among other things,”

- “numerous partners,”

# Causal-Chain Requirement

Do not merely state conclusions.

For every important conclusion, explain the causal mechanism.

Bad:

> “The company benefits from economies of scale.”

Better:

1. Identify which costs scale.
2. Identify which remain variable.
3. Determine whether procurement improves with scale.
4. Determine whether utilization improves.
5. Determine whether technology or automation improves marginal cost.
6. Determine whether distribution becomes more efficient.
7. Examine historical evidence.
8. Identify possible diseconomies.
9. Connect the mechanism to unit economics.
10. Decide whether the effect is a durable advantage.

Apply this level of reasoning throughout.

# Follow-the-Regulation Rule

Where regulation is material, do not treat it as a checklist.

- what organizational complexity it creates,
- whether competitors face the same burden,
