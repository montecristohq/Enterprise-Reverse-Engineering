## III.1 Product and service universe
Decompose all material products, services, subscriptions, platforms, brands, business lines, hardware, software, interfaces, consumables, aftermarket, financing and ancillary offerings.

For each: customer, payer where different, job, price, revenue, direct cost, margin, geography, regulation, dependencies, substitutes, strategic purpose.

## III.2 Product hierarchy
Core products, supporting products, bundles, platforms, add-ons, consumables, service layers, premium tiers, ecosystem products.

## III.3 THE MONETISATION LADDER
**Establish the sequence by which a customer becomes more valuable, and what each rung is worth.**

Identify the entry product, the conversion events, and the products that follow. Quantify: what proportion of customers reach each rung, and **what a customer at each rung is worth relative to one at the entry rung.** Where a single conversion event dominates — a deposit, a subscription, a second product — name it and establish the multiple.

Then state the finding prior studies reached twice: **the escalation ladder is usually also the risk ladder.** The rungs that monetise best frequently carry the most credit, conduct or regulatory exposure. Establish whether that holds here.

## III.4 Product lifecycle
Ideation, development, launch, growth, maturity, maintenance, discontinuation.

## III.5 Customer journey
Awareness → onboarding → purchase or contract → usage or delivery → support → repeat → cross-sell → renewal → exit.

## III.6 Core value-flow reconstruction
Select representative high-value workflows. **Trace each of the following separately, because they diverge and the divergence is the finding:**
- the customer-facing event,
- the physical or material flow,
- the money flow,
- the data and information flow,
- internal operational state,
- accounting recognition,
- legal and regulatory responsibility.

## III.7 Transaction, order or service teardown
For each step: customer, entity, channel, supplier or input, facility or system, inventory, process, quality control, pricing, direct cost, revenue recognition, delivery, settlement, support, failure mode, timing, risk.

**Name every party that takes a cut**, including those outside the group. A step-by-step trace that omits the intermediaries has understated the cost base.

## III.8 Velocity and duration *(the time dimension of the unit)*
**Establish how long capital, inventory or obligation is tied up per unit, and how many times it turns per year.**

Depending on the subject: receivable or loan duration; inventory days; project length; contract term; subscription life; asset life; time from input to cash.

Then compute what the turnover implies: **the same annual volume at twice the velocity requires half the capital.** Where the subject's velocity differs materially from its peers or from the category the market places it in, that difference is frequently the most under-appreciated economic fact about the business, and Volume VI should be told to price it.

## III.9 Product dependencies
Prerequisites, cross-sell, retention products, loss leaders, high-margin layers, ecosystem dependencies, optionality.

## III.10 Product-level economics
Revenue, gross margin, contribution, service burden, acquisition burden, retention, capital intensity, working capital, and failure, warranty, credit or fraud costs.

## III.11 Capacity and the supply side
Where relevant, the scarce capacity: factories, aircraft, rooms, compute, skilled labour, drivers, sellers, beds, spectrum, licensed professionals, balance sheet.

## III.12 Quality and customer outcome
What constitutes success and failure for the customer, and how quality is measured — by the enterprise, and independently.

## III.13 Failure and exception paths
Stockouts, defects, rejected applications, outages, failed payments, refunds, warranties, recalls, disputes, fraud, regulatory holds.

**Establish what happens when the normal path does not complete**, who bears the loss, and whether the exception path is designed or improvised. This section is routinely thin and routinely important.

## III.14 Product-market evolution
Original wedge, adjacent products, discontinued products, bundling, vertical integration, platformisation, premium and down-market moves, customer shifts.

**Where the enterprise has drifted from its original constituency, establish whether the drift was chosen or forced** — pulled by opportunity or pushed by unit economics. The answer matters more than the drift.

## Final questions
- What is the true core product?
- Which product acquires customers? Which retains them? Which has the best economics?
- Which is strategically important despite being small?
- How long is capital tied up, and how often does it turn?
- What does the customer actually buy — product, access, convenience, trust, status, network, financing, infrastructure, or something else?

**Completion test:** a reader should understand exactly what the enterprise delivers, how value moves from inputs to customer outcome, who takes a cut at each step, and how quickly the cycle repeats.
